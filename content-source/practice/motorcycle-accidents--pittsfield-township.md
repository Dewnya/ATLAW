## Pittsfield Township Motorcycle Accident Lawyer

A motorcycle crash in Pittsfield Charter Township is governed by a rule many riders learn only after they are hurt: under Michigan no-fault, a motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle.” That definition decides:

- who pays your medical bills, and how much they pay
- whether you have a no-fault claim at all
- how the insurer will argue the helmet question

The township adds its own risks:

- **Carpenter Road, Washtenaw Avenue, and Ellsworth Road,** where drivers turn left across a rider’s path into shopping centers, gas stations, and apartment drives. Three of Washtenaw County’s ten highest-crash intersections in 2024 were on Carpenter Road.
- **Michigan Avenue (US-12), State Street, and Ann Arbor-Saline Road,** the higher-speed roads to Saline and into Ann Arbor, with long gaps between signals and deer. The township recorded 96 deer-involved crashes in 2024, more than the City of Ann Arbor.
- **I-94 and US-23,** which meet at the township’s northeast corner, with merges, trucks, and work zones.
- **A township that owns no roads.** When the road itself caused the crash, the claim is against the Washtenaw County Road Commission or MDOT, each with its own notice clock.

## Where Pittsfield Township motorcycle crashes happen

**Carpenter Road, Washtenaw Avenue, and Ellsworth Road.** These are the township’s commercial roads, lined with big-box stores, shopping centers, gas stations, and apartment complexes. Carpenter at Packard, at Ellsworth, and at Michigan Avenue, and Hogback at Washtenaw, were all among Washtenaw County’s ten highest-crash intersections in 2024. The rider crashes are:

- **A driver turning left** across the rider’s path into a shopping center, a gas station, or a side street. Under Michigan law, a driver must yield to oncoming traffic before turning left (MCL 257.650). This is the classic motorcycle crash: the driver “never saw” the bike or misjudged its speed.
- **A driver pulling out** of a parking lot or apartment drive into the rider’s lane (MCL 257.652).
- **A lane change** into a motorcycle in the next lane (MCL 257.642(1)(a)).
- **Rear-end crashes** in the queues at the signals and in the turn lanes.
- **Buses and delivery trucks** stopping, pulling out, or turning across a rider near the stores and bus stops.

**Michigan Avenue, State Street, and Ann Arbor-Saline Road.** These carry riders south toward Saline and north into Ann Arbor, past subdivisions, schools, and open land. The crashes there are different from the corridor crashes:

- a driver turning left across the rider’s path at an unsignalized intersection or subdivision entrance
- a driver pulling out from a side road, misjudging a motorcycle’s speed
- a driver crossing the center line on an undivided stretch
- gravel and sand at intersections and driveways, and leaves in the fall
- **deer,** especially at dawn and dusk; the township recorded 96 deer-involved crashes in 2024

**I-94 and US-23.** At the junction and the interchanges, the crashes are:

- merges at the ramps, and at the I-94 and US-23 junction
- lane changes into a motorcycle in a driver’s blind spot
- trucks, with larger blind spots, wind blast, and tire debris
- rear-end crashes in the slowdowns at the ramps and in work zones

The township recorded 148 interstate crashes in 2024. See our [Pittsfield Township truck accident page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/truck-accidents/pittsfield-township).

**Drinking and riding.** 31 percent of Michigan’s 2024 rider deaths involved alcohol. The township recorded 34 alcohol-involved crashes of all kinds in 2024. A bar or restaurant that over-serves a driver who then hits a rider can be liable under the dram shop act. The notice is due within 120 days after you hire a lawyer, and suit within two years (MCL 436.1801(3)). See our [drunk driving accident page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/drunk-driving-accidents).

**The road itself.** A single-vehicle crash caused by a pothole, a broken pavement seam, or a drop-off in the traveled lanes is a claim against the agency with jurisdiction, under the highway exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1402). The township owns no roads, so there are two possible road defendants:

- **The Washtenaw County Road Commission** for Carpenter, Packard, Ellsworth, State Street, Platt, Hogback, and the subdivision streets: written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **MDOT** for I-94, US-23, US-12 (Michigan Avenue), and M-17 (Washtenaw Avenue east of US-23): written notice filed with the Court of Claims within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).

A defect that was readily apparent to an ordinary observer for 30 days or more is conclusively presumed known to the agency (MCL 691.1403). A notice sent to the wrong agency, or to the township, does not protect the claim, which is why we confirm jurisdiction at the exact location first. Gravel spilled by a truck or tracked out of a construction site is a claim against the hauler or the contractor, not the road agency.

## Who pays a Pittsfield Township rider’s medical bills

**If a car or truck was involved, you have no-fault benefits, but not from your motorcycle insurer, and not first from your own car insurer.** Under MCL 500.3114(5), your Personal Injury Protection benefits come, in order, from:

1. the insurer of the owner or registrant of the motor vehicle involved
2. the insurer of that vehicle’s operator
3. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s operator
4. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s owner or registrant

Priority follows the vehicles’ insurers, not who was at fault. A rider does not claim PIP from “the at-fault driver’s insurer” as such; the insurer of the vehicle involved is first in line whoever caused the crash. “Involved” does not require contact; a car that cut you off is involved even if it never touched you. If no insurer in that chain exists, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays (MCL 500.3172), with its $250,000 medical cap, unless you owned the motorcycle and it was uninsured (MCL 500.3113(b)).

The benefits are the ones a driver would get under MCL 500.3107:

- medical expenses, up to the PIP limit on the policy that pays
- 85 percent of lost wages for up to three years, up to $7,455 a month for crashes on or after October 1, 2026 (the cap DIFS sets each year)
- $20 a day in replacement services
- attendant care
- mileage

Written notice is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). See our [no-fault insurance claim page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

**The medical limit is set by a policy you did not choose.** Since July 2, 2020, Michigan drivers have chosen a PIP medical level for each policy (MCL 500.3107c). The policy first in priority pays first, up to its PIP level. If the driver who hit you chose $250,000, or a lower level, a lower-priority policy with more coverage, such as your own car policy, may then pay the rest. This is why optional first-party medical coverage on a motorcycle policy matters.

**If no car or truck was involved, no-fault does not apply.** This is where a rider’s claim differs most from a driver’s. A driver who hits a deer on Michigan Avenue has PIP benefits from their own policy. A rider who hits a deer, with no motor vehicle involved, generally has none. The same is true of:

- a slide on gravel, sand, or leaves at an intersection or in a corner
- a pothole, a pavement drop-off, or a broken seam
- a low-speed drop in a parking lot

Your bills go to your health insurance and to any medical coverage you bought on the motorcycle policy. Any injury claim is against whoever caused the hazard, if anyone did: the road agency for a defect, or the hauler or contractor that left gravel on the road. A car or truck that forced you to swerve, even without contact, is a vehicle “involved” in the crash, and that changes the answer.

**Your motorcycle.** Your own collision or comprehensive coverage pays for the bike. The mini tort claim does not cover a motorcycle, because a motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle” under the no-fault act (Nerat v Swacker, 150 Mich App 61, 1986). That makes your own collision and comprehensive coverage the main source for the bike.

**Mopeds.** A moped is not a motorcycle under the no-fault act, and the rider rule above does not apply. A moped rider hit by a car is generally treated like a pedestrian or cyclist, with benefits from the household policy first and, if there is none, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3115(1)).

## Your claim against the driver who hit you

For pain and suffering and economic losses beyond no-fault, you can sue the at-fault driver if your injury meets the threshold: serious impairment of body function, permanent serious disfigurement, or death (MCL 500.3135). A serious impairment is an objectively manifested impairment of an important body function that affects your general ability to lead your normal life. It does not have to be permanent (MCL 500.3135(5); *McCormick v Carrier*, 2010).

Motorcycle injuries often clear that threshold. Common examples include:

- fractures
- road rash requiring grafts
- torn ligaments
- [brain injuries](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/brain-injury)
- [spinal cord injuries](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/spinal-cord-injury)

You also recover excess economic damages above the PIP limit that applied to you, including lost wages above the monthly cap (MCL 500.3135(3)(c)). **You have three years to sue** (MCL 600.5805(2)). A fatal crash is brought by the estate through the Washtenaw County Probate Court. The wrongful death claim also has three years, with an extension for the estate’s personal representative (MCL 600.5852). See our [wrongful death page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/wrongful-death).

**What the claim is worth depends on the policies.** Some drivers carry only the $50,000 opt-down minimum (MCL 500.3009(5)), and some carry nothing. The recovery above the other driver’s limit turns on your own **uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage**. That coverage must be on the motorcycle policy, because many car policies exclude the insured while riding an owned motorcycle. Your UIM insurer’s written consent is needed before you accept the at-fault driver’s limit. See our [uninsured motorist page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).

**Comparative fault is the fight.** Michigan’s rule is modified comparative fault, and it concerns your own share of fault (MCL 500.3135(2)(b), 600.2959):

- **More than 50 percent at fault:** you recover no noneconomic damages.
- **50 percent or less:** your award is reduced by your share.
- **Exactly 50 percent:** you recover half.

Economic damages beyond no-fault are reduced by your share as well; they are not recovered “regardless of fault.” Every driver and company that contributed to the crash can be named, whatever its share. Insurers argue speed, lane position, lane splitting (illegal in Michigan), and “came out of nowhere.” The evidence that answers them:

- the crash report from the Pittsfield Township Department of Public Safety, or the State Police on I-94 and US-23
- witnesses
- the other driver’s phone records
- store, gas station, and apartment cameras along Carpenter, Washtenaw, and Ellsworth, and doorbell cameras in the subdivisions
- photographs of the intersection, the driveway, the road surface, the gravel, or the road edge
- the vehicles’ event data recorders
- the rider’s own camera

## The helmet question

Since 2012, Michigan has let riders 21 and older ride without a helmet if they meet two conditions (MCL 257.658):

- They carry at least $20,000 in first-party medical coverage.
- They have either held a motorcycle endorsement for two years or passed an approved safety course.

Riders under 21 must wear a helmet, and a passenger has rules of their own. If you met those conditions, riding without a helmet was legal, so it was not a traffic violation. In a head-injury case, the insurer will argue a helmet would have reduced the injury. That is a damages and causation argument for the jury, not a bar to the claim. In a leg or shoulder case, it is irrelevant.

## When the vehicle that hit you was a bus or a public vehicle

**A TheRide bus, a township police car or truck, a Road Commission truck, or a school bus.** TheRide runs routes on Washtenaw Avenue, Carpenter Road, State Street, and Packard Road. A crash with any of these vehicles is a claim under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity against the agency that owns it (MCL 691.1405), in the ordinary courts.

**A State or University of Michigan vehicle.** A crash with an MDOT truck, a State Police car, or a University of Michigan vehicle is a claim against the State in the Court of Claims, decided by a judge, with a verified notice within six months (MCL 600.6431).

In each case the agency is the owner of the “motor vehicle involved,” so its insurer, or the agency itself if it is self-insured, is generally first in line for your no-fault benefits under the rider priority rule. An individual officer or employee is personally liable only for gross negligence (MCL 691.1407(2)). Dash camera, body camera, and dispatch records are overwritten on short schedules, so we request them by FOIA promptly.

## What to do after a Pittsfield Township motorcycle crash

1. **Get medical care the same day.** Trinity Health Ann Arbor, in neighboring Superior Township, and University of Michigan Health in Ann Arbor are Level I trauma centers.
2. **Get the police report,** and note which agency responded. If a car caused the crash without making contact, make sure the report says so.
3. **Note where you were.** A mailing address that says Ann Arbor, Ypsilanti, or Saline does not settle whether the crash was in the township, and that decides the road owner and the district court.
4. **Identify every vehicle involved,** including the one that never touched you, and write down the vehicle number of any bus or public vehicle.
5. **Photograph everything:** the bike, the scene, the intersection or driveway, the road surface and any gravel or debris, your gear, and your injuries. Do not repair or sell the bike.
6. **Read your policies.** Check your motorcycle policy for first-party medical, UM/UIM, collision, and comprehensive coverage, and your car policy’s PIP level.
7. **Apply for no-fault benefits with the right insurer promptly, if a motor vehicle was involved.** Do not give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurer.
8. **If a road defect, a State vehicle, or a bar was involved, call within days.** The road-defect and State notices are already running, and the dram shop notice runs from the day you hire a lawyer.

## Frequently asked questions


**How many motorcycle crashes happen in Pittsfield Township?** The State’s county profile does not break out motorcycle crashes by community. Countywide, 105 motorcycles were involved in Washtenaw County crashes in 2024. Five riders were killed and 25 seriously injured.

**A driver turned left in front of me into a shopping center on Carpenter Road. Who is at fault?** Usually the driver turning left, who must yield to oncoming traffic, including a motorcycle. Insurers will argue your speed, so store and gas station cameras and witnesses matter, and footage is overwritten within days.

**Does the at-fault driver’s insurance pay my medical bills?** Not because the driver was at fault. Under the rider priority rule, the insurer of the car involved pays first, whoever caused the crash. Fault matters for your separate claim for pain and suffering.

**I hit a deer on Michigan Avenue near Saline. Does no-fault pay?** Usually not, if no car or truck was involved. A motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle” under no-fault, so a deer strike with no other vehicle generally produces no PIP claim. Your health insurance and any medical coverage on your motorcycle policy pay instead, and comprehensive coverage pays for the bike. If a car forced you off the road, or hit you after you went down, the answer changes.

**A pothole on a county road caused my crash. Who do I notify?** The Washtenaw County Road Commission, in writing within 120 days, not the township, which owns no roads. I-94, US-23, Michigan Avenue, and Washtenaw Avenue east of US-23 are MDOT’s, with written notice filed in the Court of Claims within 120 days.

**I was riding a moped. Is that the same?** No. A moped is not a motorcycle under the no-fault act, so the rider priority rule does not apply. A moped rider hit by a car is generally treated like a pedestrian or cyclist.

**How long do I have to sue after a motorcycle crash?** Three years against the at-fault driver (MCL 600.5805(2)), and three years for a wrongful death claim, with an extension for the estate. Shorter deadlines apply to other parts of the claim: one year to notify the no-fault insurer, 120 days for a notice to the Road Commission, 120 days for a road-defect notice against MDOT, six months for a crash with a State vehicle, and 120 days for a dram shop notice, with two years to sue a bar.

**I wasn’t wearing a helmet. Can I still recover?** Yes, if three conditions were met: - you were 21 or older - you carried the required $20,000 in medical coverage - you had either two years of endorsement or a safety course. The insurer can argue a helmet would have reduced a head injury. That goes to damages, not to whether you have a claim.
