## Michigan Slip and Fall Lawyer

For twenty years, a fall on someone else’s property in Michigan was close to unwinnable. If the hazard was “open and obvious,” the property owner owed you no duty, and the case ended before it began. That rule is gone. In July 2023 the Michigan Supreme Court overruled it in *Kandil-Elsayed v. F & E Oil*, and a slip and fall claim now turns on the questions it should have turned on all along: did the owner know about the hazard, did they do anything about it, and how much of the fall was your fault.

If you were hurt in a fall and were told, or assumed, that you had no case, that may no longer be true. This page explains the rules as they stand now.

## What changed in 2023, and why it matters to your case

Under the old rule (*Lugo v. Ameritech*, 2001), a property owner had no duty to protect visitors from a danger an average person would have noticed: a wet floor, a pothole in a parking lot, ice on a walkway. Because almost every hazard is visible in hindsight, insurers used the rule to deny most fall claims outright, and courts dismissed most of the ones that were filed.

In *Kandil-Elsayed v. F & E Oil, Inc.* (Michigan Supreme Court, July 28, 2023), the Court held that whether a hazard was open and obvious no longer decides whether the owner owed a duty. It is now one factor in two other questions: whether the owner breached the duty of reasonable care, and how much fault, if any, belongs to the person who fell. Both are questions for a jury.

In practice: a grocery store that left a spill on the floor for an hour cannot win the case by saying the spill was visible. A jury decides whether the store should have cleaned it up and whether you should have seen it. If the jury assigns you some of the fault, your recovery is reduced by that share. If it assigns you more than half, you recover no damages for pain and suffering (MCL 600.2959). That is the fight in a Michigan fall case now, and it is a fight that can be won.

## What you have to prove in a Michigan slip and fall case

**1. The owner owed you a duty.** Michigan sets the duty by why you were on the property.

- **Invitee**: you were there for the owner’s commercial benefit, as a customer, patient, tenant in a common area, or guest of a business. The owner must inspect the property, fix dangers it finds, and warn of the ones it cannot fix. This is the highest duty and covers most falls.
- **Licensee**: a social guest in a home. The owner must warn of hidden dangers it knows about, but need not inspect.
- **Trespasser**: the owner must only refrain from willful or wanton harm.

**2. There was a dangerous condition.** A wet or recently mopped floor, a spill, ice or snow, a broken step, an unmarked change in floor level, a loose mat, a pothole in a parking lot, inadequate lighting, a missing handrail, a protruding display.

**3. The owner knew or should have known about it.** This is where most cases are decided. “Actual notice” means an employee saw it or caused it. “Constructive notice” means the hazard existed long enough that a reasonably careful owner would have found it. A spill that happened thirty seconds before you fell is hard to pin on the store; one with cart tracks through it and dried edges is not. Store surveillance footage, cleaning logs, and incident reports are the evidence, and the owner controls all three, which is why a lawyer requests them in writing immediately.

**4. The condition caused your injury**, and you suffered real damages: medical bills, lost wages, pain, and the lasting effects.

## Snow and ice falls in Michigan

Winter falls are the most common fall case in metro Detroit and the most contested. The rules:

- A property owner must take reasonable steps, within a reasonable time after snow or ice accumulates, to make walkways safe. “Reasonable” depends on the facts: a store open to the public at 9 a.m. after an overnight storm is expected to have salted its entrance; a homeowner is not expected to shovel during a blizzard.
- After *Kandil-Elsayed*, the owner cannot defeat the claim by arguing the ice was visible. The question is what they did about it and whether you had a realistic alternative route. Ice covering the only path from the parking lot to the door is a strong case; ice in a corner nobody needed to cross is not.
- **Black ice** that formed from refreezing meltwater or a leaking downspout is a condition the owner created, and those cases are stronger than natural accumulation.
- For **tenants**, Michigan’s landlord statute (MCL 554.139) requires the landlord to keep common areas fit for their intended use. The Michigan Supreme Court has held that a parking lot with one to two inches of snow is still “fit” for parking (*Allison v. AEW Capital*, 2008), so tenant snow cases depend on whether the accumulation actually prevented the use of the area.

## Falls on public property: sidewalks, parks, and government buildings

If you fell on a city sidewalk, in a county building, at a public school, or on state property, different and harsher rules apply, and the deadline is short.

- **Government immunity** protects cities, counties, and the state from most injury claims. The exceptions that apply to falls are the **highway exception**, which covers sidewalks and crosswalks (MCL 691.1402), and the **public building exception** (MCL 691.1406).
- **The two-inch rule** (MCL 691.1402a): a sidewalk defect less than two inches high is presumed to be reasonable repair. You can rebut the presumption, but a raised slab under two inches is a difficult case. Measure it and photograph it with a ruler before it is repaired.
- **Notice within 120 days** (MCL 691.1404, 691.1406): you must serve written notice on the right government body, stating the injury, the exact location and nature of the defect, and the names of witnesses. Miss it and the claim is gone regardless of how clear the defect was.
- Detroit, Dearborn, and most Wayne County cities place sidewalk repair responsibility on adjacent property owners by ordinance, which can make the private owner a defendant too.

If you fell on public property, call within days, not months.

## What to do after a slip and fall in Michigan

1. **Report it before you leave**, to the manager, the landlord, or the property owner, and ask for a written incident report. Get a copy or photograph it. A fall that was never reported is a fall the insurer will say never happened.
2. **Photograph the hazard immediately**, from several angles, with something for scale. The spill gets mopped and the ice melts within the hour; the photo is the case. Photograph your shoes and the clothes you were wearing.
3. **Get witness names and phone numbers**, including employees. Note the time; it matters for constructive notice.
4. **Get medical care the same day.** Fall injuries (fractures, torn ligaments, concussions, back injuries) are often worse than they feel in the first hour, and a record dated that day is what connects the injury to the fall.
5. **Write down what happened** while it is fresh: what the surface looked like, whether there were warning signs or cones, what employees said. “We’ve been meaning to fix that” is evidence.
6. **Do not give a recorded statement** to the property owner’s insurer and do not post about the fall. “I wasn’t looking where I was going” in a text to a friend has ended cases.
7. **Call a lawyer quickly.** Surveillance footage is typically overwritten in 7 to 30 days. A preservation letter stops that. For public property, the 120-day notice clock is already running.

- **Comparative fault**: the percentage the insurer can credibly assign to you. Footwear, distraction, and whether warning signs were up all matter.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan slip and fall claims

**I fell on something obvious, like ice or a wet floor. Do I still have a case?** Possibly, yes. Since the Michigan Supreme Court’s 2023 decision in *Kandil-Elsayed*, an obvious hazard no longer ends the case. The question is whether the owner should have fixed it and how much of the fault is yours. If you are found less than 50% at fault, you recover, reduced by your share.

**How long do I have to file a slip and fall lawsuit in Michigan?** Three years from the fall for a claim against a private owner or business (MCL 600.5805). For a fall on public property, you must give written notice to the government body within 120 days, or the claim is barred.

**The store says nobody knew about the spill. Does that end it?** No. The owner is responsible if it knew about the hazard or if the hazard existed long enough that it should have. Surveillance footage, cleaning logs, and the condition of the spill itself (dried edges, cart tracks, footprints) establish how long it was there.

**What if I was partly at fault?** Michigan uses modified comparative negligence (MCL 600.2959). Your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault. If you were more than 50% at fault, you cannot recover damages for pain and suffering.

**I fell on a city sidewalk. Can I sue the city?** Only within the highway exception to governmental immunity, and only if you serve written notice within 120 days. A defect under two inches is presumed to be reasonable repair (MCL 691.1402a), so measure and photograph it. The adjacent property owner may also be responsible under local ordinance.

**I fell in my apartment building. Is my landlord responsible?** Landlords must keep common areas fit for their intended use and the premises in reasonable repair (MCL 554.139), and must fix hazards they know about. Broken stairs, missing handrails, and unlit hallways are landlord cases. Snow in a parking lot is a landlord case only if it actually prevented use of the lot.

**Does Michigan no-fault insurance cover a fall?** No. No-fault applies only to motor vehicle accidents. A fall is paid by the property owner’s liability insurance (or a homeowner’s policy), after fault is established. Your own health insurance pays your bills in the meantime and is reimbursed from the recovery.

**Who pays my medical bills while the case is pending?** Your health insurance, Medicare, or Medicaid. They will have a lien on your recovery, which we negotiate down as part of the settlement. Some property insurers carry a small “med-pay” coverage that pays bills regardless of fault; we check.

**How much is a slip and fall case worth?** It depends on the injury, your economic losses, the strength of the notice evidence, the share of fault assigned to you, and the owner’s policy limits. No one can give a figure before reviewing the medical records and the evidence.

**How much does a Michigan slip and fall lawyer cost?** Nothing up front. ATLAW works on a contingency fee set in writing before we begin; you owe no fee unless we recover money for you. Michigan caps the fee at one-third of the net recovery.
