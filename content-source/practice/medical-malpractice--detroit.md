## Detroit Medical Malpractice Lawyer

Michigan has built more procedural requirements into medical malpractice claims than into any other kind of injury case: a two-year deadline instead of three, a written notice to each provider 182 days before suit, a sworn affidavit from a qualified expert filed with the complaint, strict rules about who that expert can be, and a cap on pain-and-suffering damages. Detroit adds a question the suburbs rarely face: who the defendant actually is. The physician who treated you at a Detroit Medical Center hospital may be a Wayne State University faculty member; the hospital may be the federal John D. Dingell VA Medical Center; the clinic may be a federally funded health center; the first responders were Detroit EMS. Each of those changes the deadline, the court, and the rules. ## The rules, by the numbers

| Rule | Figure | Statute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deadline to file suit | 2 years from the act or omission | MCL 600.5805(8) |
| Discovery rule | 6 months from discovery, never more than 6 years after the act (exceptions for retained objects, fraud, reproductive injury) | MCL 600.5838a(2) |
| Notice of Intent before suit | 182 days; provider has 154 days to respond | MCL 600.2912b |
| Affidavit of Merit | Filed with the complaint | MCL 600.2912d |
| Expert qualification | Same specialty; same board certification if the defendant is certified; majority of professional time in that specialty in the prior year | MCL 600.2169 |
| 2026 cap on noneconomic damages | $596,400 (standard) / $1,065,000 (catastrophic) | MCL 600.1483 **[VERIFY Treasury figures]** |
| Child injured before age 8 | Deadline runs to the 10th birthday | MCL 600.5851(7) |
| Federal provider (VA, federally funded clinic) | Administrative claim (SF-95) within 2 years; suit after denial or 6 months | 28 USC 2401(b), 2675 |
| Fault that bars noneconomic damages | More than 50 percent | MCL 600.2959 |

Economic damages, medical bills, lost earnings, and the cost of future care, are not capped, and in a catastrophic Detroit case they are most of the recovery.

## What malpractice is, and is not

A bad outcome is not malpractice. Malpractice is a bad outcome caused by a provider who failed to do what a reasonably competent provider in the same specialty would have done in the same circumstances (MCL 600.2912a). You must prove the standard of care (national for specialists; same or similar community for general practitioners), a breach of it, that the breach more likely than not caused the injury, and damages. Michigan requires proof that the patient would have had a better outcome, not merely a chance of one (MCL 600.2912a(2)), and that requirement is where many Detroit cases are lost, particularly delayed-diagnosis cases in patients who were already seriously ill. A qualified physician’s written review of the records decides, before anything is filed, whether a case exists. Many do not, and we say so.

Malpractice by VA employees is a claim against the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act, not a Michigan malpractice suit: an administrative claim on Standard Form 95 must be presented to the VA within two years (28 USC 2401(b)), the VA has six months to act, and suit is then filed in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan, tried to a judge without a jury, with Michigan substantive law and the Michigan caps applied. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: application of Michigan caps and procedural rules under the FTCA]** A veteran harmed by VA care may also claim disability compensation under 38 USC 1151 regardless of fault; see our [VA benefit claims page](about:blank).

**Federally funded health centers.** Several Detroit community health centers receive federal Health Center Program funding, and their employed providers are deemed federal employees for malpractice purposes, so a claim against them also follows the FTCA path, with its two-year administrative deadline and no Notice of Intent. Identifying a deemed clinic early is the difference between a timely claim and none. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: FSHCAA deeming; identify Detroit deemed centers at intake]**

**Detroit EMS and city providers.** Detroit Fire Department EMS is a City agency. Claims against EMS personnel are limited by the Emergency Medical Services Act to gross negligence or willful misconduct (MCL 333.20965), a limit that also covers the City as the life support agency. Private ambulance companies that contract with the City or the hospitals have the same gross-negligence protection.

**Nursing homes and rehabilitation facilities.** Detroit’s 23 Medicare-certified nursing homes produce claims that are sometimes malpractice (a medication error, an unmanaged fall risk, a missed diagnosis) and sometimes ordinary negligence (understaffing, neglect). The distinction decides whether the Notice of Intent, the two-year period, and the caps apply. (*Bryant v Oakpointe Villa*, 2004). ## The Detroit malpractice cases we see

- **Emergency department errors** at the trauma centers and community hospitals: missed stroke, missed heart attack, missed sepsis, missed fractures and bleeds on imaging, premature discharge, triage delays. Delayed-diagnosis cases often begin in the ER.
- **Birth injuries** at Hutzel, Henry Ford, and St. John: delayed response to fetal distress, mismanaged shoulder dystocia, delayed cesarean, uterine rupture, and the [cerebral palsy](/personal-injury/cerebral-palsy), [brachial plexus](/personal-injury/erbs-palsy), and [hypoxic](/personal-injury/birth-asphyxia) injuries that follow. A child injured before age eight has until the tenth birthday to sue; the parents’ own claims do not get that extension.
- **Surgical errors**: wrong site, retained objects (which also extend the limitations period), perforations, nerve injuries, and post-operative monitoring failures.
- **Anesthesia errors**: airway, dosing, and monitoring failures that cause hypoxic [brain injury](/personal-injury/brain-injury).
- **Medication errors** in hospitals, nursing homes, and pharmacies.
- **Cancer misdiagnosis and delayed diagnosis**: a missed finding on a mammogram, colonoscopy, or biopsy, where the “better outcome” causation standard is argued hardest.
- **Spinal and neurosurgical injuries**, including [spinal cord injury](/personal-injury/spinal-cord-injury) from delayed treatment of cauda equina or epidural abscess.
- **Death from malpractice**, brought by the estate under the Wrongful Death Act with the malpractice rules attached; see our [Detroit wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death/detroit).

## How a Detroit malpractice case proceeds

1. **The records.** We obtain the complete chart, including the audit trail that shows when each entry was made, from every provider involved. Michigan gives you a right to your records (MCL 333.26261 et seq.); we request them under it in the first week.
2. **Expert review.** A physician in the same specialty as the defendant reviews the records and tells us in writing whether the standard of care was breached and whether the breach caused the harm. Many cases end here, and we tell clients so directly.
3. **Identify the defendants and the forum.** Private hospital and physician group: the Third Circuit. State university employee: possible Court of Claims. VA or deemed clinic: SF-95 and the Eastern District. City EMS: the GTLA. Serving a Notice of Intent on the wrong entity, or serving one on a federal provider that does not require it while the FTCA clock runs, loses the case.
4. **The Notice of Intent** (MCL 600.2912b), served on each provider at least 182 days before suit, stating the facts, the standard of care, the breach, what should have been done, and causation. Serving it tolls the two-year period during the notice period (MCL 600.5856(c)), but only if served before the period runs, so the firm must be retained well inside the two years.
5. **The complaint and Affidavit of Merit** (MCL 600.2912d), filed in the Third Judicial Circuit Court at the Coleman A. Young Municipal Center for Detroit care, with the expert’s sworn affidavit. The defendant files an affidavit of meritorious defense.
6. **Discovery, case evaluation, and facilitation.** Depositions of the providers and the experts on both sides; the Third Circuit’s case evaluation and facilitation practices; and, for the cases that do not settle, trial to a Wayne County jury. Two to four years from retention to resolution is typical.
7. **Resolution.** Settlements for minors and estates are approved by the court. Medicare, Medicaid, and health plan liens are resolved before distribution.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long do I have to sue a Detroit doctor or hospital?** Two years from the malpractice, with a six-month discovery rule capped at six years. Because the Notice of Intent must be served 182 days before suit and the expert review comes before the notice, you should call well inside the first year.

**My care was at the Detroit VA. Is that a Michigan malpractice case?** No. It is a claim against the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act: an administrative claim to the VA within two years, then suit in federal court without a jury. There is no Notice of Intent, and the deadlines and procedures are different. Call early; the two years runs from the injury, not from when you learned the VA was at fault, in most cases.

**Is there a cap on what I can recover?** On pain-and-suffering damages only: $596,400 for most cases and $1,065,000 for the most severe permanent injuries in 2026, adjusted each January. Medical bills, lost earnings, and future care are not capped.

**Do I need a Notice of Intent?** Yes, for any Michigan provider: a written notice served on each defendant at least 182 days before filing, stating the standard of care, the breach, and causation. Serving it on time pauses the two-year clock during the waiting period.

**Who can be the expert?** A physician who practices or teaches in the same specialty as the defendant, board-certified if the defendant is, and who spent most of the prior year in that specialty. The expert is retained before the case is filed, and the cost is advanced and repaid only from a recovery.

**My child was injured at birth at a Detroit hospital. How long do we have?** A child injured before age eight has until the tenth birthday to file. The parents’ own claims for their losses are subject to the ordinary two-year period, so the family should not wait.

**The hospital says the outcome was a known complication. Is that the end?** Not necessarily. A known complication that occurs despite proper care is not malpractice; a known complication caused by a failure to follow the standard of care, or that was not recognized and treated in time, is. The records and an independent physician’s review decide which it was.

**Can I get my own medical records from a Detroit hospital?** Yes. Michigan’s Medical Records Access Act gives you the right to your records within a set time for a limited fee, and we request them on your behalf in the first week.
