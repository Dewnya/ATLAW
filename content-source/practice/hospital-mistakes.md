## Two ways a hospital is liable

**1. For its own negligence (direct liability).** A hospital owes patients duties that belong to the institution, not to any one clinician:

- **Credentialing and privileging**: granting or renewing privileges to a physician the hospital knew or should have known was incompetent or impaired. Michigan courts have considered negligent credentialing allegations against hospitals, although the availability of an independent negligent credentialing claim has not been definitively resolved. Credentialing records collected for a protected peer-review function are generally privileged.
- **Staffing**: nurse-to-patient ratios and skill mix that make monitoring, medication administration, and fall prevention impossible to perform to standard.
- **Policies and systems**: medication reconciliation, surgical counts, time-outs, infection control, sepsis screening, handoff procedures, and the equipment and alarms that implement them.
- **Supervision of residents and students** in teaching hospitals.
- **Premises and equipment**: a wet floor, a broken bed rail, a malfunctioning lift.

**2. For the negligence of others (vicarious liability).**

- **Employees**: nurses, technicians, pharmacists, residents, and employed physicians. The hospital answers for their negligence within the scope of employment.
- **Physicians the hospital calls independent contractors**: emergency physicians, radiologists, anesthesiologists, hospitalists, and specialists supplied by staffing companies. The hospital’s standard defense is that it is not responsible for them. Michigan’s ostensible agency doctrine answers that defense: a patient who looked to the hospital for care, rather than to a particular physician, and who was assigned a physician by the hospital, may hold the hospital liable unless the patient was told and understood that the physician was not the hospital’s agent (Grewe v Mt Clemens General Hospital, 1978). The Michigan Supreme Court reaffirmed the rule in Markel v William Beaumont Hospital (2022) and, by order of July 9, 2025, held that an emergency department patient assigned a treating physician without a prior relationship need not show an additional act of reliance to establish a factual question of ostensible agency. The admission paperwork, with its fine-print statement that physicians are independent contractors, is where this issue is fought.

Identifying which theory applies to which defendant is the first task, because Michigan’s expert-witness requirements and the applicability of malpractice damages caps depend on the nature of the claims.

## Malpractice or ordinary negligence: the question that decides the rules

Not every injury in a hospital is medical malpractice. Michigan’s Supreme Court set the test in Bryant v Oakpointe Villa Nursing Centre (2004): a claim is malpractice if it arose within a professional relationship and raises questions of medical judgment beyond the common knowledge of lay people; otherwise it is ordinary negligence. The difference is procedural and decisive:

|  | Medical malpractice | Ordinary negligence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deadline | 2 years, 6-month discovery rule, 6-year repose (MCL 600.5805(8), 600.5838a) | 3 years (MCL 600.5805(2)) |
| Pre-suit notice | 182-day notice of intent (MCL 600.2912b) | None |
| Affidavit of merit | Required, from a same-specialty expert (MCL 600.2912d, 600.2169) | None |
| Non-economic damages | Capped (MCL 600.1483) | Uncapped |
| Examples | A fall involving negligent medical assessment of a patient's fall risk or clinically necessary precautions; a pressure injury from failure to reposition; a medication dose error; a missed diagnosis | A fall on a wet hallway floor; a bed that collapsed; a visitor’s injury |

A hospital will argue that a fall or a pressure injury is malpractice, to invoke the shorter deadline and the cap; the injured patient’s lawyer analyzes the facts under Bryant before the two-year mark passes, because a claim filed as ordinary negligence after two years will be dismissed if a court decides it was malpractice. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current application of Bryant to nursing care claims]**

## Government and federal hospitals: different courts, different clocks

Michigan’s governmental immunity act does not shield a public hospital from liability for medical care (MCL 691.1407(4)), but suing one changes the procedure:

- **University of Michigan Health (Michigan Medicine, Ann Arbor) and other state-university hospitals.** Claims against the state and its universities are heard in the Michigan Court of Claims, without a jury, and require the claim itself or a qualifying notice of intention to be filed with the Court of Claims within six months after the event giving rise to the claim, in addition to compliance with the medical malpractice notice-of-intent requirements (MCL 600.6431, 600.2912b).
- **City- and county-owned hospitals** (Hurley Medical Center in Flint, for example) are sued in circuit court under the malpractice rules; governmental immunity does not apply to the medical care itself.
- **Veterans Affairs hospitals** (John D. Dingell VA Medical Center in Detroit, the Ann Arbor VA). Claims against the United States proceed under the Federal Tort Claims Act: an administrative claim, commonly submitted on Standard Form 95 or another qualifying written claim stating a sum certain, must be presented to the VA within two years after the federal claim accrues. Suit may be filed in federal court after a final denial or six months of agency inaction, with six months to sue after a final written denial (28 USC 2401(b), 2675). Michigan substantive law, including the damages cap, applies; there is no jury. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: application of Michigan’s NOI and affidavit requirements in FTCA cases]**
- **Federally funded community health centers** are also covered by the FTCA under the Federally Supported Health Centers Assistance Act, a trap for the unwary: a claim against what looks like a private clinic may have a two-year federal presentment deadline.

## What to do if you suspect hospital negligence

1. **Request the complete medical record in writing**, under the Medical Records Access Act, including nursing notes, medication administration records, monitor strips, imaging, and the admission paperwork you signed.
2. **Write down the timeline** as you remember it, including names, times, what you were told, and by whom.
3. **Photograph visible injuries** (pressure wounds, surgical sites, bruises from falls) with dates.
4. **Do not sign a release, accept a bill write-off as a settlement, or give a recorded statement** to the hospital’s risk management or insurer before speaking to a lawyer. You may receive a letter expressing sympathy; under Michigan’s apology statute, an expression of sympathy is not, by itself, an admission of fault or a resolution.
5. **Keep treating**, and have the injury documented by independent physicians.
6. **Call a malpractice lawyer within months.** The two-year deadline, the 182-day notice, and the expert review needed to evaluate the claim leave less time than the calendar suggests, and the six-month Court of Claims notice for U-M is shorter still.

## What a Michigan hospital negligence case is worth

Economic damages, uncapped: past and future medical care, including care made necessary by the error, lost wages and earning capacity, and attendant care. Non-economic damages, capped under MCL 600.1483 at $596,400 or $1,065,000 in 2026. In a death, the losses recoverable under the wrongful death act (MCL 600.2922), including the family’s loss of society and companionship, within the cap. Hospital cases may differ from single-physician cases in collectibility: a hospital system may be self-insured or carry substantial insurance, and a case with direct and vicarious theories against the institution may provide additional sources of recovery when liability and available coverage support them.

## Frequently asked questions about suing a hospital in Michigan

**Can I sue the hospital, or only the doctor?** Often both. The hospital is liable for its own negligence (credentialing, staffing, policies) and for its employees, and under Michigan’s ostensible agency rule it can be liable for an independent-contractor physician it assigned to you unless you were told and understood otherwise (Grewe, 1978; Markel v Beaumont, 2022, 2025).

**The admission form said the doctors were independent contractors. Does that end it?** Not by itself. Whether you were actually advised and understood that the physician was not the hospital’s agent, in the circumstances of an emergency admission, is a fact question for a jury under Markel.

**How long do I have to sue a hospital in Michigan?** Two years for malpractice (with a six-month discovery rule and a six-year outer limit), three years for ordinary negligence, and a 182-day notice of intent must precede a malpractice suit. Six months to notify the Court of Claims for U-M; two years to present an FTCA claim for a VA hospital.

**Is a fall in the hospital malpractice or negligence?** It depends on whether the claim involves medical judgment (such as a negligent clinical fall-risk assessment) or common knowledge (such as a wet floor or failure to address an obvious hazard), under Bryant v Oakpointe (2004). The answer decides the deadline and the cap.

**Is there a cap on damages against a hospital?** Non-economic damages in a malpractice claim are capped ($596,400 or $1,065,000 in 2026). Economic damages are not. Ordinary negligence claims are not capped.

**Can I get the hospital’s incident report?** Usually not; incident reports prepared for qualifying peer-review functions and other peer-review materials are generally privileged in Michigan. The patient's chart, monitor data, and independently maintained hospital records may be available through discovery.

**I was treated at the University of Michigan hospital. Is the process different?** Yes. Claims against U-M are filed in the Court of Claims, require the claim itself or a qualifying notice of intention to be filed within six months, and must comply with the separate malpractice notice-of-intent requirements. They are decided without a jury.

**My family member was harmed at the VA hospital in Detroit. Can we sue?** Yes, under the Federal Tort Claims Act: an administrative claim within two years, then suit in federal court. Michigan law governs the substance.

**What is a hospital-acquired infection claim?** A claim that the hospital failed to follow infection-control standards for lines, catheters, surgical sites, or isolation, proved with infection-control records and expert testimony. Not every infection is negligence; the records show which ones are.

**How much does it cost to sue a hospital?** Nothing up front. Cases are taken on contingency, and the firm advances expert and record costs for accepted cases, which in a hospital case can be substantial.
