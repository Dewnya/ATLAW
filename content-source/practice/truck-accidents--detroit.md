A truck crash is not a car crash with a larger vehicle. The driver and the carrier are governed by federal safety regulations whose violation is evidence of negligence. ## Who is responsible, and who pays

**The carrier, not just the driver.** Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations impose safety duties on qualifying motor carriers and drivers. A carrier may be liable for a driver’s negligence within the scope of employment or for its own negligent hiring, supervision, maintenance, or safety practices. Michigan has adopted many federal motor-carrier safety regulations for intrastate operations under MCL 480.11a, subject to exceptions and modifications. The applicable requirements depend on the carrier, vehicle, and operation. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]** The rules that decide Detroit cases most often:

| Rule | 49 CFR | What it means for the case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hours of service | Part 395 | Qualifying property-carrying drivers generally may drive up to 11 hours after 10 consecutive hours off duty, within a 14-hour driving window, subject to exceptions. Cumulative 60/70-hour limits, applicable ELD records, and the proper classification of border waiting time may be relevant to evaluating fatigue and compliance. |
| Driver qualification | Part 391 | CDL, medical certificate, driving record, and a background check the carrier must do and keep. Negligent hiring and retention are claims against the carrier separate from the driver’s fault. |
| Post-crash drug and alcohol testing | Part 382 | Required after qualifying fatal crashes and certain injury or tow-away crashes when a qualifying moving-violation citation is issued within the regulatory window. Alcohol tests should be conducted promptly, with attempts discontinued after eight hours; the outer limit for controlled-substance testing is 32 hours. |
| Inspection and maintenance | Part 396 | Carriers and drivers have inspection and maintenance duties. Written defect reports and repair records may be important evidence, but no-defect written reports are not required for many property-carrying operations. |
| Cargo securement | Part 393 | Securement standards for every load; shifted loads cause rollovers on the interchange curves. |
| Handheld phone ban; hazardous conditions | 392.82, 392.14 | Commercial drivers may not use a handheld phone; must reduce speed or stop in snow, ice, and fog. |
| Carrier knowledge | 390.6 | A carrier may not coerce a driver to violate the rules; dispatch records showing impossible schedules are evidence against the carrier. |
| Minimum insurance | Part 387 | Financial-responsibility minimums depend on the carrier and cargo: $750,000 for many interstate property carriers, and $1 million or $5 million for specified hazardous-material operations. |

For accidents occurring from October 1, 2026, through September 30, 2027, the maximum work-loss benefit is $7,455 per 30-day period. Certain qualifying economic losses exceeding available no-fault benefits may be recoverable separately under MCL 500.3135(3)(c). ## The evidence, and how fast it disappears

Federal regulations generally require carriers to retain driver duty-status records and supporting documents for at least six months under 49 CFR 395.8(k), and qualifying driver vehicle inspection reports for at least three months under 49 CFR 396.11. Longer preservation duties may apply when litigation is reasonably anticipated. Bridge plaza, freeway, plant-gate, Project Green Light, and business-camera retention periods vary by system and operator. Relevant footage should be identified and preservation requested promptly. Truck engine-control and event-data records may be lost through subsequent operation, repairs, or overwriting, making prompt preservation important. The first act in a Detroit truck case is a **preservation letter** to the carrier, its insurer, the trailer owner, and the shipper, demanding that they keep the ELD data, the ECM and EDR data, the dash and cab camera footage, the driver’s qualification file and post-crash test results, the dispatch, bill-of-lading, and customs records, the maintenance file, and the truck itself for inspection. A carrier that fails to preserve material evidence when litigation is reasonably anticipated may face evidentiary consequences or court sanctions, depending on the circumstances. Depending on the circumstances, relevant records may be requested from Michigan State Police Commercial Vehicle Enforcement, Detroit Police, bridge authorities, MDOT, and other entities through applicable records-request procedures. **[CONFIRM: firm’s preservation-letter practice and timing]**

## What to do after a Detroit truck crash

1. **Call 911 and get medical care the same day**, even if you feel able to drive. Truck-crash injuries to the neck, back, and brain often present days later.
2. **Photograph the truck**: the cab, the trailer, the USDOT number and carrier name on the door, the plates of both tractor and trailer, the load, the skid marks, and the road. The USDOT number identifies the carrier and its federal safety record.
3. **Get the police report.** Detroit Police or the Michigan State Police will respond to an injury crash; the UD-10 is purchased for $15 at michigan.gov/tcps. For a fatal or serious crash, the MSP Commercial Vehicle Enforcement Division may prepare a separate post-crash inspection report, which is often the most useful document in the case.
4. **Do not speak to the carrier’s insurer or its investigator.** They will contact you within days and ask for a recorded statement. Decline.
5. **Report to your own insurer and file the Application for No-Fault Benefits** within the first week.
6. **Call a lawyer within days**, so the preservation letter goes out before the records expire.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many truck accidents happen in Detroit?**  A published analysis of the City of Detroit’s open crash records identified 1,844 commercial-vehicle-involved crashes in Detroit during 2024, including nine fatal crashes. Across Wayne County, Michigan Traffic Crash Facts recorded 3,701 crashes involving heavy trucks or buses, with 3,916 qualifying truck and bus units involved. These figures use different reporting measures, so they should not be treated as directly equivalent.

**The truck was from Ontario. Can I still sue?** Yes. Canadian carriers operating in the United States must register with the FMCSA, carry the same federal minimum insurance, and follow the same safety regulations, and they can be sued in Michigan for a Michigan crash. Where the suit is filed and which insurer responds take some sorting out, which is one reason to call early.

**Who pays my medical bills after a truck crash in Detroit?** Your own no-fault insurer, regardless of fault, after you file an Application for No-Fault Benefits; the Assigned Claims Plan if no household policy applies. The carrier’s insurer pays for pain and suffering and losses beyond no-fault once fault and a threshold injury are established.

**Why does the lawyer need to act within days?** Because the records that prove the case have expiration dates: six months for the driver’s hours-of-service logs, three months for inspection reports, days or weeks for camera footage, and the truck’s electronic data can be cleared on repair. A prompt preservation request helps protect relevant records when litigation is anticipated, although retention rules and legal preservation obligations depend on the circumstances.

**Is a truck case worth more than a car case?** The injuries are usually worse and the insurance is usually larger: $750,000 minimum for a general freight carrier against $50,000 for a minimally insured car. The value still depends on the injury, fault, and the record.

**The truck was on a residential street in Southwest Detroit. Does that matter?** It can. Detroit restricts heavy trucks to designated routes, and a truck off its route is evidence on the question of fault and on whether the carrier’s dispatch and routing practices were negligent.

**I was hit by a City of Detroit garbage truck or a DPW truck. Is that different?** Yes. A privately operated refuse truck may involve a claim against its hauler or other legally responsible parties. A qualifying crash involving a government-owned truck may implicate the Governmental Tort Liability Act’s motor vehicle exception (MCL 691.1405), depending on the circumstances. Available vehicle video, GPS information, dispatch records, and other relevant evidence should be identified and preservation requested promptly. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

**I drive a truck and was hurt on the job in Detroit. What are my options?** Workers’ compensation from your employer for medical care and wage loss, and a separate suit against any third party whose negligence caused the crash.
