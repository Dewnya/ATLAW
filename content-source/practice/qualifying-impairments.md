## Conditions That Qualify for Disability in Michigan

Every week people in Michigan search for the list of conditions that qualify for Social Security disability, and every week they find pages that give them one. Those pages are not wrong, exactly, but they answer a question Social Security does not ask. No diagnosis qualifies anyone for disability benefits. What qualifies is a medically determinable impairment that has lasted or will last at least twelve months and either **meets or equals one of Social Security’s Listings of Impairments** or **leaves the person unable to do any work that exists in significant numbers**, given their age, education, and work history. The first path is the one the lists describe. The second path is the one most claimants are approved on.

This page covers both. It sets out the fourteen body systems of the Listings, the “Blue Book,” with the actual criteria for the listings Michigan claimants most often ask about, so that you can compare them to your own records. It explains the difference between meeting a listing and equaling one. Then it explains the path for everyone whose condition is severe but does not fit a listing: residual functional capacity, the grid rules that favor claimants over 50, and the medical-vocational allowance that accounts for a large share of approvals. It closes with what records prove each kind of claim.

Among the 7.2 million disabled workers receiving SSDI in December 2024, about a third were approved for musculoskeletal conditions and more than a quarter for mental disorders. Most of them did not meet a listing. They were approved because the evidence showed they could not work.

## The five-step test, briefly

Every decision at Michigan’s Disability Determination Service and every hearing before a Michigan administrative law judge follows 20 CFR 404.1520:

1. Are you working above the substantial gainful activity level? If yes, not disabled.
2. Do you have a severe impairment that has lasted or will last twelve months?
3. **Does it meet or equal a Listing?** If yes, disabled. This is where the “conditions that qualify” live.
4. Can you do your past relevant work (jobs held in the last five years)?
5. **Can you do any other work**, considering your residual functional capacity, age, education, and experience? If no, disabled. This is where most claims are won.

Steps three and five are the two paths this page explains. Our [appeals page](/disability/ssd-appeals-process) covers where claims are lost at each step and how an appeal fixes it.

The Listings describe, for each body system, impairments severe enough that Social Security presumes a person who meets them cannot work. A listing has specific medical criteria, and **a claimant meets it only if the records document every element**. Three things to take from the table. First, the listings are written in medical measurements, not symptoms: an ejection fraction, a spirometry value, a seizure frequency, a BMI. The records must contain the measurement. Second, several listings are met on diagnosis alone (ALS, non-mosaic Down syndrome, pancreatic cancer, small cell lung cancer), and most of those conditions are also on the Compassionate Allowances list for expedited handling; our [Compassionate Allowances page](/disability/compassionate-allowances) covers that program. Third, the 2021 musculoskeletal revision and the 2017 mental revision made the two largest categories harder to meet, which is why the second path matters more than it did a decade ago.

### The mental disorder listings: the B and C criteria

Mental health claims are the second-largest category and the most misunderstood. Nearly every mental listing from 12.02 through 12.15 requires two things: **medical documentation** of the disorder (the A criteria), and either the **B criteria** or the **C criteria**.

The **B criteria** measure functioning in four areas:

1. Understanding, remembering, or applying information
2. Interacting with others
3. Concentrating, persisting, or maintaining pace
4. Adapting or managing oneself

The claimant must have an **extreme limitation in one area or a marked limitation in two**. Social Security uses a five-point scale (none, mild, moderate, marked, extreme), and “marked” means seriously limited, “extreme” means unable to function in that area independently, appropriately, and effectively on a sustained basis. A diagnosis of major depression or PTSD with a treating psychiatrist’s records showing moderate limitations across the board does not meet the listing; the same diagnosis with documented marked limitations in concentration and in adapting, supported by treatment notes, hospitalizations, and a detailed psychiatric opinion, does.

The **C criteria** are the alternative for a “serious and persistent” disorder: a medically documented history of the disorder over at least two years, with ongoing treatment or a highly structured setting that diminishes the symptoms, and only marginal adjustment, meaning minimal capacity to adapt to changes or demands not already part of daily life. The C criteria exist for the claimant who is stable only because of intensive support and would decompensate at work.

### Meeting versus equaling a listing

A claimant **meets** a listing when the records document every criterion. A claimant **equals** a listing, under 20 CFR 404.1526 and SSR 17-2p, when the impairment is at least equal in severity and duration to a listed one but does not match the criteria exactly: a listed impairment missing one finding but with other findings of equal significance, an unlisted impairment that is medically equivalent to a listed one, or a combination of impairments that together equal a listing. Equivalence requires a medical judgment by a Social Security medical consultant or a medical expert at the hearing; it is not something a claimant or a treating physician can declare. In practice, equivalence is argued at the hearing level and is most successful with a detailed opinion from a specialist explaining which listing and why.

## Path two: residual functional capacity and the grid rules

Most people who receive disability benefits did not meet or equal a listing. They were approved at step five, because Social Security concluded, from their **residual functional capacity** and their vocational profile, that there was no work they could do. This is the medical-vocational allowance, and it is the path for the claimant with degenerative disc disease and a walker she does not quite need, the claimant with heart failure and an ejection fraction of 35 percent, the claimant with depression and anxiety whose limitations are marked in one area but moderate in the others.

**Residual functional capacity** is what you can still do in a work setting on a regular and continuing basis, eight hours a day, five days a week, under 20 CFR 404.1545. Physically, it is expressed as an exertional level: sedentary (lifting 10 pounds occasionally, mostly sitting), light (20 pounds occasionally, 10 frequently, standing and walking about six hours), medium (50 pounds occasionally), heavy, or very heavy, together with non-exertional limits such as postural restrictions, reaching, handling, environmental restrictions, and the need to alternate positions or lie down. Mentally, it is expressed as the capacity for simple or detailed tasks, pace, attendance, interaction with the public and coworkers, and tolerance of change and stress.

**The grid rules**, formally the Medical-Vocational Guidelines at 20 CFR Part 404, Subpart P, Appendix 2, take the exertional RFC and three vocational factors, age, education, and prior work, and direct a result:

| Age category | Age | How the grids treat a claimant limited to sedentary work | Limited to light work |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Younger individual | 18–49 | Generally not disabled unless additional non-exertional limits erode the job base (illiteracy or inability to communicate in English is a factor at 45–49 for sedentary) | Generally not disabled |
| Closely approaching advanced age | 50–54 | **Disabled** if no past relevant work or unskilled past work, or skilled work without transferable skills | Generally not disabled |
| Advanced age | 55–59 | **Disabled** unless skills transfer with very little adjustment | **Disabled** if unskilled past work or no transferable skills |
| Closely approaching retirement age | 60 and over | Disabled | Disabled unless skills transfer with very little adjustment |

Two consequences follow. A 52-year-old warehouse worker with a bad back who can no longer lift 20 pounds and has no skills that transfer to a desk job is disabled under the grids even though his MRI does not meet listing 1.15. And a 38-year-old with the same back and the same RFC is not, unless non-exertional limitations (the need to lie down, absences, pain-related loss of concentration) are documented and eliminate the sedentary jobs the vocational expert would otherwise name. Social Security considers the age categories applicable during the period being evaluated. In a borderline situation within a few days to a few months of an older category, SSA considers whether to apply that older category rather than applying age mechanically, under 20 CFR 404.1563(b).). **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

For a claimant with mental limitations or a mix of physical and mental limitations, the grids are a framework rather than a directive, and the case is decided by the vocational expert’s testimony about whether jobs exist for a person with the specific limitations in the judge’s hypothetical. ## What the records have to show, for each path

**To meet a listing**: the specific measurement or finding each criterion names, from the specialist who makes it: the echocardiogram report with the ejection fraction, the pulmonary function test with the FEV1, the seizure log and neurologist’s notes, the psychiatric treatment records and a mental RFC opinion addressing the four B areas, the pathology report with staging. A physician’s letter that says the patient “meets listing 4.02” without the underlying test is not evidence; the test is.

**To win at step five**: a longitudinal treatment record without unexplained gaps; objective findings that correspond to the limitations claimed; and a **function-by-function opinion** from a treating source stating, in an eight-hour workday, how long the claimant can sit, stand, and walk, how much and how often they can lift, whether they need to change position or lie down, how often they would be off task and absent, and, for mental impairments, their capacity in each of the four B areas. Since 2017, under 20 CFR 404.1520c, that opinion is weighed for supportability and consistency rather than given controlling weight because it comes from a treating doctor, so the opinion must explain itself and match the records.

**For both**: consistency between what you tell your doctors, what you write in Social Security’s Function Report, and what you say at the hearing, and evidence submitted at least five business days before the hearing under 20 CFR 404.935. ## Frequently asked questions about qualifying conditions

**Is there a list of conditions that automatically qualify for disability?** Not in the sense most people mean. A small number of listings are met on diagnosis (ALS, non-mosaic Down syndrome, pancreatic cancer, small cell lung cancer, among others), and the Compassionate Allowances program expedites claims for 314 serious conditions. For every other condition, qualification depends on documented severity and functional limitations, not the diagnosis.

**My condition is not in the Blue Book. Can I still qualify?** Yes, and most people who receive benefits did not meet a listing. If your condition is severe, has lasted twelve months, and the evidence shows you cannot do your past work or any other work given your age, education, and experience, you are disabled under the law.

**Does my back condition qualify?** Since the 2021 revision, the musculoskeletal listings require a documented need for a walker, two canes, or a wheelchair, or serious loss of use of the arms, so most back claims are decided at step five. Whether you qualify depends on your RFC and, heavily, on your age: a claimant over 50 limited to sedentary work with no transferable skills is disabled under the grid rules.

**I have depression and anxiety. What do I have to prove?** Medical documentation of the disorder, and either an extreme limitation in one of the four B areas or a marked limitation in two, or the C criteria for a serious and persistent disorder. Short of that, the claim is decided at step five on your mental RFC: your capacity for simple tasks, pace, attendance, and dealing with people and change.

**Why does my age matter so much?** Because the grid rules presume that a person over 50 limited to sedentary work, or over 55 limited to light work, without transferable skills, cannot adjust to other work. The same medical findings produce a different legal result at 49 and at 50.

**What is a medical-vocational allowance?** An approval at step five, based on residual functional capacity and the vocational factors rather than on a listing. It is the most common basis for approval.

**Can several conditions together qualify when none does alone?** Yes. Social Security must consider the combined effect of all impairments, under 20 CFR 404.1523, both in determining whether they equal a listing and in assessing RFC.

**I was approved for cancer. Will I lose benefits when treatment ends?** A cancer that meets a listing is considered disabling for at least three years after treatment ends. After that, Social Security reevaluates the residual effects. Many claimants continue to qualify at step five on the lasting effects of treatment.

**Does Michigan have different rules?** No. The Listings, the five-step test, and the grid rules are federal and apply identically in every state. What varies is the hearing office handling the case, its workload, and processing times. Michigan has seven SSA hearing offices, but federal disability eligibility standards remain the same. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

**What does a lawyer cost?** Nothing unless you win, and then the lesser of 25 percent of your back pay or $9,200, approved and paid by Social Security. There is no fee on your ongoing benefits.
