## Waterford, MI Dog Bite Lawyer

Michigan’s dog bite law is short and strict. If a dog bites you without provocation while you are on public property or lawfully on private property, the owner is liable for your damages, regardless of whether the dog had ever bitten before or whether the owner knew it was dangerous (MCL 287.351). You do not have to prove negligence. In Waterford the bites happen in the yards, driveways, and streets of the subdivisions and lake neighborhoods, on docks, beaches, and boats in summer, in the apartment complexes along M-59, Dixie, and Walton, at township and county parks, and on mail, package, and food delivery routes. The victim is often a child, a neighbor out for a walk, a guest at a lake house, or a worker at the door. And the person who pays is usually not the owner: when the owner is insured, the claim is paid by the owner’s homeowner’s or renter’s insurance.

## The statute, and the two defenses

**Strict liability.** Under MCL 287.351 you prove three things. First, a dog bit you. Second, you were on public property or lawfully on private property. That includes the owner’s own property as a guest, a customer, a tenant, a delivery driver, a utility worker, a mail carrier, or a child playing with permission; the statute specifically covers people performing a duty imposed by law or by the postal service. Third, you did not provoke the dog. The dog’s history and the owner’s knowledge do not matter. There is no “one free bite” in Michigan.

**Provocation.** The owner’s main defense. Michigan courts judge it by whether the person’s conduct would provoke a dog, not by what the person intended, so it can be unintentional: stepping on a dog’s tail by accident can be provocation. Petting a dog, walking past a fence, entering a yard with permission, or knocking on a door is not; hitting, teasing, or cornering a dog may be. The witnesses to the seconds before the bite decide it.

**Trespass.** A person unlawfully on private property is not covered by the statute. A child who wanders into a neighbor’s yard or onto a neighbor’s dock is the contested case; a delivery driver or a guest is lawfully present.

**Comparative fault.** Michigan courts have held that the general comparative-fault rule does not reduce a claim under the dog bite statute; the owner’s defenses are limited to trespass and provocation (*Hill v Sacka*, Mich App 2003).

**Injuries without a bite.** A knockdown by a jumping dog, a fall from a dock or down lake stairs while fleeing a dog, or a bicycle crash caused by a chasing dog is not covered by the statute. It is a negligence claim, and comparative fault applies. The owner’s knowledge of the dog’s tendencies, a dog loose in violation of the township’s ordinance, or a broken fence are the evidence. ## Reporting a Waterford dog bite

**If a dog is attacking or still loose, call 911.** Waterford Township Police respond to emergencies involving a dangerous animal.

**For a bite that has already happened**, report it so the dog is placed under rabies observation and an official record exists. The Township’s own notices direct residents with animal questions to Oakland County Animal Control at (248) 858-1090; its office is at 1200 N. Telegraph Road, Building 42E, in Pontiac, weekdays 8:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m, and it responds to dangerous-animal calls around the clock. If Waterford officers responded, get the police report number. The report starts the rabies observation, creates the official record, and surfaces prior complaints about the dog. We obtain it, any prior complaints, and the dog’s license record with Freedom of Information Act requests to the Township and the County.

**Licensing.** Michigan law requires dogs four months or older to be vaccinated against rabies and licensed (MCL 287.266(1), (8)). Waterford residents can buy a one- or three-year license from the Township Treasurer’s Office on Civic Center Drive, the Oakland County Treasurer, or a participating veterinarian. The license and vaccination records identify an owner who denies the dog is theirs and show whether the dog was vaccinated, which decides how urgent the rabies question is.

## What Waterford’s breed ordinance does, and does not, change

Waterford Township’s animal ordinance, in Chapter 3 of the township code, prohibits keeping a pit bull. In 2016 the township board amended the ordinance so that a veterinarian, rather than an officer, determines a dog’s breed. The pit bull prohibition, a civil infraction, is Sec. 3-023; breed identification is Sec. 3-024; dangerous animals are Sec. 3-026 (a civil infraction, then misdemeanors for repeat violations); and destruction or removal is Sec. 3-027.

For a bite victim, the ordinance matters in three ways and fails to matter in one:

- **It does not change the statute.** The owner of any dog, of any breed, is strictly liable under MCL 287.351. A bite by a Labrador and a bite by a prohibited dog are the same claim.
- **It is evidence in a non-bite case.** A dog kept in violation of the ordinance, or a dog previously found dangerous, is evidence of negligence in a knockdown or chase injury, and of the owner’s knowledge.
- **It can affect the insurance.** Some homeowner’s and renter’s policies exclude certain breeds or dogs with a bite history, so the policy has to be read before the claim is valued. Breed identification disputes, which the 2016 amendment addressed, can matter to that exclusion as well.
- **It can produce a dangerous-animal finding.** A township finding, or a court finding under Michigan’s Dangerous Animals Act (MCL 287.321 to 287.323), is strong evidence in the civil case, but the civil case does not depend on it.

## Who pays in Waterford

**Homeowner’s and renter’s insurance.** Dog bite liability is covered by the standard homeowner’s policy and many renter’s policies, often with $100,000 to $500,000 in liability coverage. The owner reports the bite, the insurer pays, and the insurer cannot require the owner to give up the dog. That is why a claim against a neighbor, a friend at the lake, or a relative is less of a barrier than it sounds, and why many bite claims settle without a lawsuit. Policies differ on breed and bite-history exclusions, and coverage must be read. An umbrella policy above the homeowner’s limit may also apply, and is checked.

**Lake homes, docks, and boats.** A guest bitten at a lake house, on a dock, or on a boat is lawfully present, and the owner’s statutory liability applies wherever the bite happens. A bite on a boat may be covered by the boat owner’s policy or the homeowner’s policy, depending on how each is written. A bite at a cottage rental may involve the renter’s dog and the property owner’s policy.

**Apartments and landlords.** Waterford’s apartment complexes along M-59, Dixie, and Walton are where bites happen in shared hallways, courtyards, walks, and parking lots. A tenant who owns the dog is liable under the statute, but many tenants carry no renter’s insurance. A landlord is generally not liable for a tenant’s dog, except where the landlord knew the dog was dangerous and had the ability to have it removed, through the lease or otherwise, and did not (*Szkodzinski v Griffin*, Mich App 1988). A complex that knowingly allowed a dog prohibited by the township ordinance is a question for that standard. Prior complaints to the management office, pet registration records, and the complex’s pet policy are the evidence, and we ask for them early. In a condominium, the association’s bylaws usually regulate pets, and an association that knew of a dangerous dog and had the power to require its removal may face a similar claim. See our [tenant injury page](/personal-injury/tenant-injuries).

**Delivery and utility workers.** Package and food delivery drivers, meter readers, and home health aides bitten on a Waterford route have the statutory claim against the owner and a workers’ compensation claim against their employer at the same time. The comp insurer has a lien on the recovery (MCL 418.827(5)). **Postal carriers** are federal employees: their work-injury benefits come under the Federal Employees’ Compensation Act, not Michigan’s Act, and the federal government is repaid from any recovery against the owner (5 USC 8101 et seq, 8132). Gig drivers who are classified as independent contractors may have no comp coverage, which makes the owner’s insurance the main source. See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

**Uninsured owners.** Where the owner rents and carries no insurance, the claim is against the owner personally and is worth what the owner can pay. That is when the search for another responsible party matters: the landlord, a co-owner or keeper of the dog, or a business that kept it on its property (see our [premises liability page](/personal-injury/premises-liability)).

## Injuries, children, and scarring

Dog bites cause puncture and crush wounds, torn tendons and nerves, hand and forearm fractures, and facial injuries. Wounds are often left open and treated with antibiotics, and infection is a routine complication; a bite near lake water carries its own infection risk. The biting dog is observed for ten days; if it cannot be found, the victim receives post-exposure rabies treatment. Waterford patients are often treated at McLaren Oakland or Trinity Health Oakland in Pontiac; serious facial injuries in children may be treated by pediatric plastic surgeons in Royal Oak or Detroit. For older adults, a knockdown by a dog can mean a hip or wrist fracture, which is a negligence claim even without a bite. Waterford patients are often treated at McLaren Oakland or Trinity Health Oakland in Pontiac; serious facial injuries in children may be treated by pediatric plastic surgeons in Royal Oak or Detroit.

**Children** are often bitten on the face and head. A facial bite on a child can mean scar revision surgeries over years as the child grows, and psychological injury that Michigan law compensates and a counselor should document. A minor’s settlement must be approved by the court, with the funds protected until adulthood (MCR 2.420); for a Waterford child that is usually the Oakland County Probate Court or the circuit court where the case is pending. A child has until the later of the ordinary three years or one year after the 18th birthday to sue (MCL 600.5851(1)). The evidence does not keep that long; settle or sue when the plastic surgeon’s projection of future revisions is clear.

**Damages** include medical treatment and future scar revision, lost wages (including a parent’s time off work), pain and suffering, permanent disfigurement, and psychological injury, with no cap. A fatal attack is a wrongful death claim brought by the estate; see our [wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death).

## What to do after a dog bite in Waterford

1. **If the dog is still attacking or loose, call 911.** Waterford Township Police respond to dangerous-animal emergencies.
2. **Get medical care the same day**, even for a bite that looks minor; the record dated that day is the case.
3. **Identify the dog and the owner**: name, address or unit number, and vaccination status. If the owner refuses, note the address and the dog’s description; the license records can identify the owner.
4. **Report the bite** to Oakland County Animal Control at (248) 858-1090, and get the Waterford police report number if officers responded. In an apartment complex, also report it to the management office in writing and keep a copy.
5. **Photograph the wounds** that day and as they heal, and the place where the bite happened, including any broken fence or gate.
6. **Get witness names.** Provocation is decided by what witnesses say happened in the seconds before the bite.
7. **Do not give a recorded statement** to the owner’s insurer or accept an early offer; facial scarring is not valued until it has matured.
8. **Ask about the owner’s insurance**, including umbrella and boat coverage, and in a rental, the landlord’s.

## Frequently asked questions

**Who do I call about a dog bite in Waterford?** Call 911 if a dog is attacking or still loose; Waterford Township Police will respond. For a bite that has already happened, the Township directs residents to Oakland County Animal Control at (248) 858-1090. Get the police report number if officers came.

**The dog had never bitten anyone before. Does the owner still have to pay?** Yes. Michigan’s statute makes the owner liable regardless of the dog’s history or the owner’s knowledge, as long as you were lawfully present and did not provoke the dog.

**Waterford bans pit bulls. Does that make my claim stronger if one bit me?** Not under the bite statute, which makes the owner of any dog liable. The ordinance can matter as evidence in a knockdown or chase injury, in a claim against a landlord who allowed a prohibited dog, and in whether the owner’s insurance excludes the breed.

**I was bitten by a friend’s dog at their lake house. Do I have to sue my friend?** In an insured case the claim is paid by the owner’s homeowner’s insurance, not by your friend, and the insurer cannot make them give up the dog. Many cases settle without a lawsuit.

**I was bitten by another resident’s dog in my apartment complex. Is the landlord responsible?** Possibly. The dog’s owner is liable under the statute. A landlord may be liable if it knew the dog was dangerous, for example from earlier complaints, and could have required its removal but did not.

**I am a mail carrier and was bitten on my Waterford route. What are my claims?** Two: federal workers’ compensation under the Federal Employees’ Compensation Act, through the Postal Service, and a claim against the dog’s owner. The federal government is repaid part of its benefits from the owner recovery, so the two must be coordinated.

**My child was bitten on the face. How long do we have?** Until the later of three years or one year after the child’s 18th birthday. The case should still be brought when the medical picture is clear, and a child’s settlement is approved by the court with the funds protected until adulthood.

**What counts as provocation?** Conduct that would provoke a dog, judged by what happened rather than by intent: hitting, teasing, cornering, stepping on it. Petting a dog, walking past it, or knocking on a door is not provocation.
