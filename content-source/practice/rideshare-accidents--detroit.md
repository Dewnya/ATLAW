## Detroit Uber & Lyft Accident Lawyer

An Uber or Lyft crash in Detroit is a Michigan no-fault car crash with one added question that decides what the claim is worth: what was the app doing at the moment of impact? If a ride had been accepted or a passenger was on board, a $1 million policy applies. If the driver was logged in and waiting, only the Act’s minimum limits apply, $50,000 per person and $100,000 per accident (MCL 257.2123(2)). If the app was off, the driver’s personal policy applies, and that policy may exclude rideshare driving altogether. Uber and Lyft hold the record that answers the question, and they do not volunteer it. Everything else about the case, who pays your medical bills, whether the company itself can be sued, how a Detroit court handles the arbitration clause in the app’s terms, follows from that first answer.

## The three periods, and how to prove which one applied

Michigan regulates Uber, Lyft, and similar companies as transportation network companies under 2016 PA 345, MCL 257.2101 and following. The Act requires insurance in three periods (MCL 257.2123):

| Period | What the driver was doing | Coverage | Who provides it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| App off | Personal driving | Driver’s personal policy; Michigan minimum $50,000 / $100,000 | Driver’s insurer, which may exclude rideshare use |
| App on, no ride accepted | Logged in, waiting for a request | $50,000 per person / $100,000 per crash / $25,000 property damage, plus PIP and PPI | Uber or Lyft, or the driver’s rideshare endorsement |
| Ride accepted or passenger on board | En route to pick up, or carrying a passenger | $1,000,000 combined, plus PIP and PPI | Uber or Lyft |

The moment the driver accepts a ride, the $1 million policy attaches and stays attached until the passenger is dropped off. The gap period, app on with no ride, has coverage only at Michigan’s minimums, which is why a serious injury in that period usually turns on the injured person’s own uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage.

**Proving the period.** Uber and Lyft keep trip logs, GPS traces, and timestamps for every driver session. A **preservation letter** to the company and its insurer in the first days, followed by a subpoena if necessary, obtains them. A driver who says “I was off the app” is confirmed or contradicted by the record. Screenshot the trip screen, the driver’s name and photo, the plate, the route, and the time before you leave the scene, if you can; it is the one piece of evidence the companies will not preserve for you. **[CONFIRM: firm’s practice of sending the preservation request in the first days]**

## Who pays your medical bills

**If you were a passenger.** Michigan no-fault pays your medical expenses and wage loss regardless of fault, but being a paying customer does not put Uber’s insurer first in line. In general, your own auto policy pays first, or a policy held by your spouse or a relative you live with; if there is none, you claim through the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3114, as amended by 2016 PA 345; MCL 500.3172). **[ATTORNEY CHECK: PIP priority order for a TNC passenger, carried from the rideshare hub]** In Detroit, where many passengers have no auto policy and no insured household member, the rideshare policy’s PIP is reached more often than in the suburbs, and the Assigned Claims Plan after it. Written notice to the responsible insurer is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)); see our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

**If you were the rideshare driver.** Your own policy pays PIP if it does not exclude rideshare use; if it does, the company’s policy covers PIP while the app is on. Drivers are generally independent contractors under the Act, so there is usually no workers' compensation claim, which makes the PIP claim and the claim against the at-fault driver everything. A personal policy can be rescinded if rideshare use was misrepresented when the policy was bought, which leaves the driver effectively uninsured for personal driving as well; read your policy before the crash, not after.

**If you were in another car, on a bike, or on foot.** Your own no-fault insurer pays your benefits, as in any crash (or the priority chain under MCL 500.3114 and 3115 for pedestrians and cyclists). The rideshare period decides which liability policy you claim against for pain and suffering. ## Your claim for pain and suffering

For pain and suffering and economic losses beyond no-fault, you need a threshold injury under MCL 500.3135 (death, serious impairment of body function, or permanent serious disfigurement) and three years to file (MCL 600.5805(2)). A fatal crash is brought by the estate; see our [Detroit wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death/detroit). Our [passenger injury page](/personal-injury/passenger-injury) covers passengers in any vehicle. The defendants:

- **The rideshare driver**, covered by whichever policy applied in that period: $1 million, $50,000, or the personal policy.
- **The other driver**, if someone else hit the rideshare vehicle, covered by their own policy, which in Detroit is more often the $50,000 minimum or nothing. If that driver was uninsured or fled, the rideshare policy’s UM/UIM coverage (if any) and your own UM/UIM coverage apply. See our [uninsured motorist page](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).
- **Uber or Lyft directly**, in limited circumstances. Because drivers are generally independent contractors (MCL 257.2137), the companies are not automatically liable for a driver’s negligence. They can be liable for their own: approving a driver who failed, or should have failed, the criminal and driving-record checks the Act requires before approval and annually after (MCL 257.2107); keeping a driver on the platform after impairment complaints, which the Act requires them to suspend pending investigation (MCL 257.2139); or allowing a vehicle that failed the required inspection (MCL 257.2109). These claims are contested and fact-specific.
- **The arbitration clause.** Uber’s and Lyft’s terms of service require passengers to arbitrate disputes with the company. Courts have generally enforced those clauses against claims brought against the companies themselves; they do not bind your claim against the driver’s insurer or the other driver, which proceed in the Third Judicial Circuit Court like any Detroit injury suit. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current enforceability and scope]**

## What to do after a Detroit rideshare crash

1. **Screenshot the app**: trip screen, driver, plate, route, time. If you are the driver, screenshot your status and the trip log.
2. **Call 911 and get a police report.** Give the officer the rideshare information so the UD-10 reflects it.
3. **Get medical care the same day.**
4. **Report the crash in the app** (Uber and Lyft both have in-app crash reporting), which creates a company record, and to your own insurer.
5. **Do not give a recorded statement** to the rideshare company’s insurer or the other driver’s.
6. **Read your own declarations page** for PIP level and UM/UIM coverage.
7. **Call a lawyer within the week**, so the preservation letter for app and GPS data goes out before retention windows close.

## Frequently asked questions

**I was a passenger in an Uber that crashed in Detroit. Who pays my medical bills?** Your own auto insurer first, or a policy held by your spouse or a relative you live with; if there is none, the Assigned Claims Plan. Being a paying passenger does not put Uber’s insurer first for your medical bills; it does make the $1 million policy available for your injury claim.

**Does Uber or Lyft have to carry insurance in Michigan?** Yes: $1 million while a ride is accepted or a passenger is on board; $50,000 / $100,000 / $25,000 while the app is on with no ride; nothing while the app is off, when the driver’s personal policy applies.

**An Uber hit my car on Gratiot. Who do I claim against?** The policy that applied in that period. If a passenger was on board or a ride was accepted, the $1 million policy. The driver is generally an independent contractor, so Uber itself is usually liable only for its own negligence.

**I drive for Lyft in Detroit and was hit by an uninsured driver while waiting for a ride. What now?** Your no-fault benefits come from your own policy (or the company’s if yours excludes rideshare use). Your injury claim is a UM claim under whatever UM coverage applies in the waiting period; the Act does not require Uber or Lyft to carry UM, so your own policy’s UM coverage is what matters. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

**Can I sue Uber or Lyft directly?** Only for the company’s own negligence, such as approving a driver who should have failed the background check or keeping one on after impairment complaints. The arbitration clause in the app’s terms may send that claim to arbitration; your claim against the driver’s insurer is not affected.

**How do I prove the driver was on a trip?** Uber and Lyft keep trip and GPS logs. A preservation letter, and a subpoena if needed, obtains them. Your own screenshots from the scene are the backup.

**The driver says the app was off. Does that end my claim?** No. The company’s records decide it, not the driver’s statement. And if the app was off, the driver’s personal policy applies; if that policy excludes rideshare use, the exclusion must be read against what the driver was actually doing.

**How long do I have?** One year to notify the responsible no-fault insurer, three years to sue, and as little as 120 days for a dram shop notice. The app data should be requested within days.
