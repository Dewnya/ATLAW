## West Bloomfield, MI Motorcycle Accident Lawyer

A motorcycle crash in West Bloomfield is governed by a rule many riders learn only after they are hurt: under Michigan no-fault, a motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle.” That definition decides:

- who pays your medical bills, and how much they pay
- whether you have a no-fault claim at all
- how the insurer will argue the helmet question

## Where West Bloomfield motorcycle crashes happen

**The lake roads.** The two-lane roads between Pine, Orchard, Cass, Walnut, and Union lakes draw riders to West Bloomfield. The crashes are:

- **A car turning across the rider’s path** into a driveway, a lake-access road, or a subdivision entrance, often just past a hill or a curve.
- **A car pulling out** of a hidden driveway or side road into a motorcycle the driver did not see or misjudged (MCL 257.652).
- **A car crossing the center line** on a curve.
- **A rear-end crash** behind a car stopping suddenly to turn.
- **Loose surfaces:** gravel and sand at the edges of curves, at driveway aprons, and where private gravel roads meet the paved road.

**Deer.** West Bloomfield’s woods, wetlands, parks, and lakes put deer on the roads, most often at dawn and dusk, and in the fall. For a rider, a deer strike is often a single-vehicle crash, and that changes the claim:

- **If no car was involved,** there is no no-fault claim. Your bills go to your health insurance and to any medical coverage on the motorcycle policy.
- **If a car was involved,** for example a driver who swerved or braked for the same deer and forced you down, the car is “involved” and no-fault applies through the rider priority rule below.

**Left turns and roundabouts.** The recurring account in rider cases is a driver turning left across oncoming traffic who misjudges the speed of a motorcycle or never sees it. Under Michigan law, a driver must yield to oncoming traffic before turning left (MCL 257.650). West Bloomfield produces that crash:

- at the signals on Orchard Lake Road, Maple, Fourteen Mile, and Haggerty, and at roundabouts such as Farmington Road at Maple, one of Oakland County’s ten highest-crash intersections in 2024
- at the shopping center and restaurant driveways near those signals

**Construction, landscaping, and boat traffic.** Home construction on the lake roads leaves gravel and mud at site entrances. Landscaping trailers drop mulch, stone, and brush. In summer, trucks towing boats slow and turn at the lake launches and access roads. A contractor that tracks gravel onto the road, or a company whose trailer drops its load, can be responsible. See our [West Bloomfield truck accident page](/personal-injury/truck-accidents/west-bloomfield).

**The road itself.** A single-vehicle crash caused by a pothole, a broken pavement seam, or a loose surface left by road work is a claim against the agency with jurisdiction, under the highway exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1402). West Bloomfield Township owns none of its public roads:

- **The Road Commission for Oakland County** owns Orchard Lake Road, Maple, the mile roads, the lake roads, and the public subdivision streets: written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **MDOT** owns M-10 (Northwestern Highway), which ends at Orchard Lake Road in the township: written notice filed with the Court of Claims within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **Private roads** in some subdivisions and lake associations belong to the association or the owners. A claim about one is an ordinary premises claim.

A defect that was readily apparent to an ordinary observer for 30 days or more is conclusively presumed known to the agency (MCL 691.1403). A notice sent to the township about a road defect is a notice to the wrong defendant.

**Drinking and riding.** 31 percent of Michigan’s 2024 rider deaths involved alcohol. A restaurant, bar, or club that over-serves a driver who then hits a rider can be liable under the dram shop act. The notice is due within 120 days after you hire a lawyer, and suit within two years (MCL 436.1801(3)). See our [drunk driving accident page](/personal-injury/drunk-driving-accidents).

## Who pays a West Bloomfield rider’s medical bills

**If a car or truck was involved, you have no-fault benefits, but not from your motorcycle insurer.** Under MCL 500.3114(5), your Personal Injury Protection benefits come, in order, from:

1. the insurer of the owner or registrant of the motor vehicle involved
2. the insurer of that vehicle’s operator
3. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s operator
4. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s owner or registrant

Priority follows the vehicles’ insurers, not who was at fault. “Involved” does not require contact; a car that cut you off is involved even if it never touched you. If no insurer in that chain exists, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays (MCL 500.3172), with its $250,000 medical cap, unless you owned the motorcycle and it was uninsured (MCL 500.3113(b)).

The benefits are the ones a driver would get under MCL 500.3107:

- medical expenses, up to the PIP limit on the policy that pays
- 85 percent of lost wages for up to three years, up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year
- $20 a day in replacement services
- attendant care
- mileage

Written notice is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

The first medical limit is set by a policy you did not choose. Since the 2019 reform, the PIP medical level on the policy first in priority pays first; if the driver who hit you chose $250,000, that policy pays up to $250,000, and a lower-priority policy with more coverage, such as your own car policy, may then pay the rest. This is why optional first-party medical coverage on a motorcycle policy matters.

**If no car or truck was involved, no-fault does not apply.** Several common West Bloomfield crashes produce no PIP claim:

- a deer strike with no other vehicle involved
- a slide on gravel or sand on a curve or at a driveway apron
- a pothole or broken pavement seam on a county road
- a drop on a private gravel lake road

Your bills go to your health insurance and to any medical coverage you bought on the motorcycle policy. Any injury claim is against whoever caused the hazard, if anyone did.

**Your motorcycle.** Your own collision or comprehensive coverage pays for the bike. The mini tort claim does not cover a motorcycle, because a motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle” under the no-fault act (*Nerat v Swacker*, 150 Mich App 61, 1986).

## Your claim against the driver who hit you

For pain and suffering and economic losses beyond no-fault, you can sue the at-fault driver if your injury meets the threshold: serious impairment of body function, permanent serious disfigurement, or death (MCL 500.3135). A serious impairment is an objectively manifested impairment of an important body function that affects your general ability to lead your normal life. It does not have to be permanent (*McCormick v Carrier*, 2010).

Motorcycle injuries often clear that threshold. Common examples include:

- fractures
- road rash requiring grafts
- torn ligaments
- [brain injuries](/personal-injury/brain-injury)
- [spinal cord injuries](/personal-injury/spinal-cord-injury)

You also recover excess economic damages above the PIP limit that applied to you, including lost wages above the monthly cap (MCL 500.3135(3)(c)). You have three years to sue (MCL 600.5805(2)). A fatal crash is brought by the estate; see our [wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death).

**What the claim is worth depends on the policies.** The default liability limit is $250,000 per person, and some drivers carry an umbrella policy above it, but others carry the $50,000 opt-down minimum (MCL 500.3009(5)) or nothing. The recovery above the other driver’s limit turns on your own **uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage**. That coverage must be on the motorcycle policy, because many car policies exclude the insured while riding an owned motorcycle. Your UIM insurer’s written consent is needed before you accept the at-fault driver’s limit. See our [uninsured motorist page](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).

**Comparative fault is the fight.** Michigan’s rule is modified comparative fault (MCL 500.3135(2)(b), 600.2959):

- **More than 50 percent at fault:** you recover no noneconomic damages.
- **50 percent or less:** your award is reduced by your share.
- **Exactly 50 percent:** you recover half.

Economic damages beyond no-fault are reduced by your share as well. Insurers argue speed, lane position on a curve, lane splitting (illegal in Michigan), and “came out of nowhere.” The evidence that answers them:

- the West Bloomfield Police report and witnesses
- the other driver’s phone records
- doorbell and home security cameras along the lake roads, and store cameras at the retail intersections
- photographs of the curve, the hill, or the driveway that hid the motorcycle
- the vehicles’ event data recorders
- the rider’s own camera

## The helmet question

Since 2012, Michigan has let riders 21 and older ride without a helmet if they meet two conditions (MCL 257.658):

- They carry at least $20,000 in first-party medical coverage.
- They have either held a motorcycle endorsement for two years or passed an approved safety course.

Riders under 21 must wear a helmet. If you met those conditions, riding without a helmet was legal, so it was not a traffic violation. In a head-injury case, the insurer will argue a helmet would have reduced the injury. That is a damages and causation argument for the jury, not a bar to the claim. In a leg or shoulder case, it is irrelevant.

## What to do after a West Bloomfield motorcycle crash

1. **Get medical care the same day.** Henry Ford West Bloomfield Hospital, on Maple Road, is a Level III trauma center. The most severe injuries may go to a Level I center such as Corewell Health William Beaumont University Hospital in Royal Oak.
2. **Get the police report.** Crashes in the township are worked by the West Bloomfield Police Department. If a car caused the crash without making contact, make sure the report says so. After a deer strike, report the crash and photograph the deer.
3. **Identify every vehicle involved,** including the one that never touched you.
4. **Photograph everything:** the bike, the scene, the curve, hill, driveway, or intersection, the road surface and any gravel, your gear, and your injuries. Do not repair or sell the bike.
5. **Read your policies.** Check your motorcycle policy for first-party medical, UM/UIM, collision, and comprehensive coverage, and your car policy’s PIP level.
6. **Apply for no-fault benefits with the right insurer promptly.** Do not give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurer.
7. **If a road defect, a construction site, or a bar was involved, call within days.** The 120-day road-defect notice is already running, and the dram shop notice runs from the day you hire a lawyer.

## Frequently asked questions


**A car turned into a driveway on a lake road right in front of me. Do I have a claim?** Usually. A driver turning left must yield to oncoming traffic, including a motorcycle. Insurers will argue your speed on the curve or hill, so photographs of the sight line and any home cameras matter. The car is “involved” under no-fault, so its owner’s insurer is first in line for your medical bills.

**I hit a deer on my motorcycle in West Bloomfield. Who pays my medical bills?** If no car or truck was involved, no-fault does not pay. Your health insurance and any medical coverage on your motorcycle policy pay instead, and comprehensive coverage usually pays for the bike. If a car was involved, for example one that braked or swerved and forced you down, that car’s insurer may be first in line.

**I went down on gravel on a curve. Is anyone responsible?** Possibly. A contractor or landscaping company that left gravel or debris on the road can be. The Road Commission can be, if it knew or should have known of a defect in the road and did not fix it in a reasonable time, with written notice within 120 days. There is no no-fault claim if no other vehicle was involved. Photograph the gravel before it is swept.

**Does the at-fault driver’s insurance pay my medical bills?** Not because the driver was at fault. Under the rider priority rule, the insurer of the car involved pays first, whoever caused the crash. Fault matters for your separate claim for pain and suffering.

**I have my own car insurance. Doesn’t that cover me on the motorcycle?** Not first. If a car was involved, that car’s insurer is first in line. Your own car insurer is third, after the involved vehicle’s owner’s and operator’s insurers. A motorcycle policy itself does not carry Michigan PIP. If no car was involved, there is usually no PIP claim at all.

**A pothole on a West Bloomfield road caused my crash. Do I sue the township?** No. The township does not own its public roads. The Road Commission for Oakland County does, and written notice must reach it within 120 days. A private lake-association road belongs to the association.

**I wasn’t wearing a helmet. Can I still recover?** Yes, if three conditions were met: - you were 21 or older - you carried the required $20,000 in medical coverage - you had either two years of endorsement or a safety course. The insurer can argue a helmet would have reduced a head injury. That goes to damages, not to whether you have a claim.

**How long do I have?** - One year to notify the responsible no-fault insurer. - Three years to sue the at-fault driver. - 120 days for a notice to the Road Commission. - 120 days for a road-defect notice against MDOT. - 120 days for a dram shop notice, and two years to sue a bar.
