About two out of three first applications for Social Security Disability are denied. Most of the people denied are, in fact, disabled. ## SSDI and SSI: two programs, one disability test

**Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI)** is for people who worked and paid Social Security taxes. You need enough work credits: generally 40, with 20 earned in the ten years before you became disabled, and fewer if you are under 31. In 2026, one credit is earned for every $1,890 in wages, up to four a year. The benefit is based on your earnings record; the average SSDI payment is about $1,600 a month. After 24 months of SSDI entitlement, you qualify for Medicare regardless of age.

**Supplemental Security Income (SSI)** is for people with little income and few assets, whether or not they worked. The 2026 federal benefit rate is $994 a month for an individual and $1,491 for a couple, reduced by other income. The asset limit is $2,000 for an individual ($3,000 for a couple), not counting a home and one vehicle. SSI comes with Medicaid in Michigan immediately.

Many people qualify for both, and the application covers both. The disability test is the same.

## How Social Security decides if you are disabled: the five steps

Social Security uses a five-step sequential evaluation (20 CFR 404.1520). The claim is decided at the first step where the answer is clear.

1. **Are you working at the substantial gainful activity level?** In 2026 that means earning more than $1,690 a month ($2,830 if blind). If you are, the claim is denied without looking at your medical condition. If you are earning less, go to step two.
2. **Is your condition severe?** It must significantly limit your ability to do basic work activities, and it must have lasted or be expected to last at least 12 months or to result in death. Short-term conditions do not qualify, however serious.
3. **Does your condition meet or equal a Listing?** Social Security’s “Blue Book” lists impairments by body system with specific medical criteria. If your records show you meet one, you are disabled at this step. Most claims do not meet a listing and move on.
4. **Can you do your past relevant work?** Social Security assesses your residual functional capacity (what you can still do: sit, stand, lift, concentrate) and compares it to your past relevant work: jobs held in the last five years that met Social Security’s earnings and duration requirements, which exclude work that started and stopped in fewer than 30 calendar days. If you can still do one of them, the claim is denied.
5. **Can you do any other work?** This is where most claims are won or lost. Social Security considers your capacity, age, education, and skills against the “grid rules” (20 CFR Part 404, Subpart P, Appendix 2). The grids weigh age heavily, with different rules applying at 50 and again at 55, but the result depends on the grid rule that applies to you, including your education and work history. Knowing which grid rule applies to you is central to the case.

The evidence that decides steps two through five is medical: treating doctors’ records, imaging, test results, and, most important, a treating physician’s opinion about your specific functional limits (how long you can sit, how much you can lift, how often you would miss work). A treating physician’s opinion can help, but it is not required for approval and does not automatically receive the greatest weight; Social Security considers all the relevant medical and other evidence.

## Back pay, the waiting period, and Compassionate Allowances

**The five-month waiting period.** SSDI benefits begin five full months after the date Social Security finds your disability began (the “onset date”). SSI has no waiting period but cannot be paid for months before the application.

**Back pay.** When a claim is approved, you receive benefits back to the onset date (less the waiting period), up to 12 months before the application date for SSDI. A claim that takes two years to win can produce a large lump sum; the onset date your lawyer proves is what sets it. Attorney fees are taken from back pay only.

**Compassionate Allowances.** Social Security fast-tracks more than 280 conditions (certain cancers, ALS, early-onset Alzheimer’s, and others) under the Compassionate Allowances program; a claim flagged correctly can be approved in weeks. Terminal illness claims are expedited as well. Our Compassionate Allowances page lists the conditions.

## Why claims are denied, and what a lawyer changes

- **Insufficient medical evidence**: gaps in treatment, no specialist, no imaging, no functional opinion. A lawyer obtains complete records and asks the treating doctor for a residual functional capacity statement in the form Social Security uses.
- **Earnings above the SGA limit**, or work attempts that were not explained as unsuccessful.
- **Failure to follow prescribed treatment** without a documented reason.
- **The wrong onset date**, which costs months of back pay.
- **The grid rules not argued**: a claimant over 50 limited to sedentary work can be disabled by rule, and an examiner will not apply it unless the evidence frames it.
- **At the hearing**: the vocational expert names jobs the claimant can supposedly do. Cross-examining that expert on whether those jobs exist in significant numbers and whether the claimant can actually do them is often the whole case.

## What to do if you are applying or were denied

1. **Apply as soon as you stop working**, or as soon as your earnings fall below the SGA level. Back pay for SSDI reaches back no more than 12 months before the application, so waiting costs money.
2. **Keep treating**, and see the specialist for your condition. A claim is only as strong as the records.
3. **Ask your doctor for a functional opinion**, in writing, about what you can and cannot do. We provide the form.
4. **If denied, appeal within 60 days.** Do not file a new application; appeal.
5. **Document work attempts** that failed, with dates and reasons.
6. **Call a lawyer before the hearing**, ideally before reconsideration. The hearing is the stage where representation changes the outcome most.

## Frequently asked questions about Social Security Disability in Michigan

**What is the difference between SSDI and SSI?** SSDI is based on your work record and pays according to your earnings, with Medicare after 24 months. SSI is based on financial need, pays up to $994 a month in 2026, and comes with Medicaid. The disability test is the same for both, and many people qualify for both.

**How much can I earn and still qualify?** Under $1,690 a month in 2026 ($2,830 if blind). Earning more is “substantial gainful activity” and the claim is denied at step one regardless of your condition.

**Why was my application denied?** Usually because the medical evidence did not establish specific functional limits, or because the file did not address which jobs you can no longer do. Most denials are evidence problems, not eligibility problems, and most are reversible on appeal.

**How long do I have to appeal?** Sixty days from the date on the denial notice, plus five days for mailing, at every stage. Missing it generally means starting over.

**How long does a disability claim take in Michigan?** Initial decision in roughly six to eight months; if denied, reconsideration in another four to seven; a hearing eight to twelve months after that. A claim that goes to hearing commonly takes two years from application. Compassionate Allowance claims take weeks.

**Will I get back pay?** Yes, if approved: SSDI back to your onset date (less the five-month waiting period), up to 12 months before the application; SSI back to the application date. The lump sum is often substantial after a long appeal.

**What does a disability lawyer cost?** 25% of your back benefits, capped at $9,200, paid by Social Security from the back pay. Nothing if you do not win. No fee is taken from your ongoing monthly benefits.

**Do I have to go to a hearing in person?** Hearings are held in person at the Detroit-area offices, by video, or by phone. You can request in person. We prepare you either way; the judge’s questions and the vocational expert’s testimony are the same.

**Can I work at all while my claim is pending?** Below the SGA level, yes, but any work will be examined, and it is safer to discuss it with your lawyer first. After approval, SSDI allows a nine-month trial work period (months earning more than $1,210 in 2026 count).

**What conditions qualify?** Any condition that prevents substantial work for at least 12 months: back and spine disorders, heart disease, cancer, diabetes complications, mental health conditions, neurological disorders, and many others. The listing criteria and the grid rules decide; our Qualifying Impairments page covers them.
