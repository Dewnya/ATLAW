## Where Dearborn Heights crashes happen

**Telegraph Road.** A seven- and eight-lane divided highway, with signals at Joy, Warren, Ford, Michigan Avenue, Annapolis, and Van Born and stores, restaurants, and apartment complexes most of the way. The crashes are:

- crashes at the median U-turn crossovers, where drivers turn across the opposing lanes (MCL 257.650)
- red-light and late-yellow crashes
- rear-end crashes in the stacked turn lanes
- crashes at driveways, where a driver leaving a lot must yield to traffic on the road (MCL 257.652)
- pedestrians struck crossing between the complexes and the stores

Telegraph belongs to MDOT.

**Ford Road.** Through the north end from the Dearborn line past Telegraph, Beech Daly, and Inkster, with a retail stretch and dozens of driveways. The crashes are left-turn and red-light crashes at the signals, driveway crashes at the strips, and rear-end crashes in the turn lanes. Ford Road belongs to MDOT.

**Warren Avenue and Joy Road.** The north end’s east-west streets, lined with restaurants, bakeries, markets, and places of worship, carry heavy foot traffic into the evening and late at night, especially on weekends and during holidays. The crashes are:

- pedestrians struck at crossings and mid-block, often after dark
- drivers backing out of parking spaces and lots
- crashes with double-parked cars and delivery vehicles
- intersection crashes on dense residential streets

Warren belongs to Wayne County. See our [pedestrian accident page](/personal-injury/pedestrian-accidents).

**Michigan Avenue.** Across the middle of the city, past industrial and commercial frontage and the 20th District Court, with trucks to the Michigan Avenue corridor. The crashes are left turns into businesses, rear-end crashes, and intersection crashes at Inkster and Telegraph. Michigan Avenue belongs to MDOT. **The south end.** Van Born, Annapolis, Pelham, Beech Daly, and Powers, with the Taylor and Allen Park retail at the edges and I-94 just south. The crashes are intersection crashes at the signals and four-way stops, and school-zone crashes.

**Hines Drive.** The county parkway along the Rouge runs through the north end. The crashes are run-off-road and head-on crashes on the curves, and cyclists and pedestrians struck where the trail meets the drive. Hines Drive belongs to Wayne County. See our [bicycle accident page](/personal-injury/bicycle-accidents).

**Whose road it is: the State, the county, or the City.** Dearborn Heights is a city, and road ownership is split three ways:

- **MDOT** owns Telegraph, Ford Road, and Michigan Avenue. A road-defect claim against MDOT goes to the Court of Claims, with written notice filed there within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **Wayne County** owns Warren, Hines Drive, and other major roads.
- **The City of Dearborn Heights** owns the local streets.

A claim against the county or the City requires written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1402, 691.1404). A pothole or other defect in the road can make the agency a defendant alongside the other driver; on State and county roads, signals, signs, and sight obstructions generally fall outside the highway exception (Nawrocki v Macomb County Road Commission, Mich 2000). The notice must go to the agency that actually has jurisdiction over that stretch of road, or it is no notice.

## Fault is the case

In a rear-end crash, fault is rarely disputed. At a Telegraph left turn, a Ford Road driveway, or a Warren Avenue crossing, it often is, and the other driver’s insurer may try to assign you half or more of the fault. That share matters (MCL 500.3135(2)(b)):

- **More than 50 percent at fault:** you lose your claim for pain and suffering.
- **50 percent or less:** your recovery is reduced by your share. At exactly 50 percent, you recover half.

The evidence that decides fault is perishable:

- the Dearborn Heights Police Department crash report and diagram
- **signal timing data** from MDOT or Wayne County
- cameras at the stores, restaurants, gas stations, and apartment complexes along Telegraph, Ford, and Warren, many overwritten within days
- the vehicles’ **event data recorders**, which store speed, braking, and throttle in the seconds before impact
- the other driver’s phone records, for a distracted-driving claim (see our [distracted driving page](/personal-injury/distracted-driving-accidents))
- witnesses, including other drivers and people on the sidewalk

We move promptly to request the camera footage, signal data, and vehicle data.

## Who pays after a Dearborn Heights crash

**Your no-fault (PIP) claim.** Your own auto insurer pays these benefits regardless of fault (MCL 500.3107):

- your medical expenses, up to the PIP level you chose
- 85 percent of lost wages for up to three years, up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year
- replacement services at $20 a day
- attendant care

**The PIP level is not the same as your liability limits.** Since July 2, 2020, Michigan drivers choose a PIP medical level for each policy: unlimited, $500,000, $250,000, $50,000 for some Medicaid enrollees, or an opt-out for some Medicare enrollees (MCL 500.3107c). That choice is separate from the $250,000/$500,000 liability limits that protect the other driver. The level on your declarations page decides how much of your medical care no-fault pays.

If you have no policy, a resident relative’s policy pays (MCL 500.3114). If none exists, an occupant claims from the insurer of the vehicle they were in (MCL 500.3114(4)), and then the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3172), capped at $250,000 for medical benefits. A pedestrian or cyclist hit by a car, with no household policy, claims through the Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3115).

The insurer must receive written notice within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). It must pay each bill within 30 days of reasonable proof, or owe 12 percent interest (MCL 500.3142). Providers’ charges are subject to the fee schedule in MCL 500.3157. See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

**If you had no insurance.** A person who owned a car and was required to insure it, but did not, is generally barred from PIP benefits for their own injuries (MCL 500.3113(b)). A driver who was operating their own uninsured vehicle cannot recover pain-and-suffering damages, whoever was at fault (MCL 500.3135(2)(c)). A passenger or pedestrian who did not own an uninsured car may still be covered through a household policy or the Assigned Claims Plan.

**Your claim against the at-fault driver.** For pain and suffering, you need a threshold injury: death, serious impairment of body function, or permanent serious disfigurement (MCL 500.3135(1)). A serious impairment is an objectively manifested impairment of an important body function that affects your general ability to lead your normal life. It does not have to be permanent (MCL 500.3135(5); *McCormick v Carrier*, 2010).

You can also recover economic losses that no-fault does not cover (MCL 500.3135(3)(c)):

- lost wages beyond the three years or above the monthly cap
- medical bills above your chosen PIP level

The suit must be filed within three years (MCL 600.5805(2)). The mini tort claim recovers up to $3,000 in vehicle damage not covered by your policy (MCL 500.3135(3)(e)). If the at-fault driver’s insurer will not pay it, the claim is filed in the 20th District Court.

**When the other driver has no insurance, or not enough.** Some drivers on Telegraph, Ford Road, and Michigan Avenue carry only the $50,000 opt-down minimum (MCL 500.3009(5)), and some carry nothing.

- **Against an uninsured driver:** your pain-and-suffering claim is worth what that driver can personally pay, unless you carry **uninsured motorist coverage**. A hit-and-run is usually a UM claim, with short notice terms in the policy.
- **Against an underinsured driver:** your own **underinsured motorist coverage** pays the difference up to your UIM limit, if you follow the steps in order:
 1. The at-fault driver’s insurer offers its limit.
 2. You notify your own insurer and ask for its written consent to settle.
 3. Only then do you accept the limit and sign the release.
 4. The UIM claim then proceeds.

Accepting without consent can forfeit the UIM claim under most policies. We read every policy in the household before valuing the claim. See our [uninsured motorist page](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).

**Drunk drivers and the dram shop claim.** A bar, restaurant, or store that serves a visibly intoxicated person, or a minor, is liable for the injuries that person causes under Michigan’s dram shop act (MCL 436.1801). The State Police recorded 34 alcohol-involved and 18 drug-involved crashes in Dearborn Heights in 2024. The dram shop claim has its own rules:

- Written notice must go to the business within 120 days after you hire a lawyer.
- The drunk driver must be named and kept in the suit.
- The suit must be filed within two years, not three.

See our [drunk driving accident page](/personal-injury/drunk-driving-accidents).

**Police cars, fire trucks, and buses.** All of these fall under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405):

- Dearborn Heights Police and Fire vehicles
- SMART buses on Telegraph, Ford Road, and Michigan Avenue
- school buses for the several districts that serve parts of the city
- Wayne County road and parks vehicles

## What to do after a Dearborn Heights crash

1. **Call 911 and get the police report.** Michigan requires a report for any crash with injury or $1,000 in damage (MCL 257.622). Crashes in the city are worked by the Dearborn Heights Police Department.
2. **Photograph the scene.** Get the vehicles, plates, the other driver’s license and insurance card, the intersection or driveway, the signals, and the lane markings. Note every store and apartment building with a camera facing the scene. Footage is kept for days, not weeks.
3. **If the other driver has no insurance card,** get the plate, the name, and the address, and tell the officer. Your own policy’s uninsured motorist notice terms may be short.
4. **Get medical care the same day.** Corewell Health Dearborn Hospital, a Level II trauma center, and Garden City Hospital are on either side of the city. The most serious injuries may go to a Level I center in Detroit.
5. **Report to your own insurer and file the Application for No-Fault Benefits** promptly. If you have no policy, a lawyer can identify the insurer next in priority or the Assigned Claims Plan.
6. **Read your declarations page** for your PIP medical level, UM/UIM, and collision coverage.
7. **Do not give a recorded statement** to the other driver’s insurer, sign a release, or accept a policy-limits offer before your UIM insurer consents.
8. **Call a lawyer within days** if fault is disputed, the other driver is uninsured, or the road played a part. The camera footage is the first evidence lost, and the 120-day notice to Wayne County or the City is already running.

## Frequently asked questions

**A driver turned in front of me at a Telegraph crossover. Who is at fault?** Usually the driver turning left, who must yield to oncoming traffic. These crashes are often disputed, though: the signal phase, your speed, and whether the view was blocked all matter. Store cameras along Telegraph can settle it, but footage is kept only days.

**I was hit crossing Warren Avenue at night. Who pays my medical bills?** Your own household’s auto policy pays if you have one. If not, the Assigned Claims Plan pays. Your pain-and-suffering claim is against the driver, reduced by any share of fault the evidence assigns you, which is why the lighting, the crossing, and the cameras matter.

**The driver who hit me had no insurance. What now?** Your own no-fault insurer, a household member’s insurer, or the Assigned Claims Plan pays your medical bills and lost wages regardless of fault. Your pain-and-suffering claim depends on whether you carry uninsured motorist coverage. Without it, the claim is worth what the uninsured driver can personally pay.

**Is my PIP medical coverage the same as my $250,000 or $500,000 limit?** Not necessarily. Your liability limits protect the other driver if you cause a crash. Your PIP medical level is a separate choice on the same policy, and it decides how much of your own medical care no-fault pays. Check your declarations page for both.

**How many crashes are there in Dearborn Heights?** The State Police recorded 1,251 crashes in Dearborn Heights in 2024. No one was killed, 19 people were seriously injured, and 385 were injured in all. 556 of the crashes were on Telegraph, Ford Road, and Michigan Avenue.

**A pothole caused my crash. Do I file against the City of Dearborn Heights?** It depends on the road: - **Telegraph, Ford Road, and Michigan Avenue** belong to MDOT, with notice filed in the Court of Claims within 120 days. - Warren, Hines Drive, and other major roads belong to Wayne County, with written notice within 120 days. - **Local streets** belong to the City, with the same 120 days.

**Where would my Dearborn Heights case be filed?** Mini tort claims and claims up to $25,000 go to the 20th District Court on Michigan Avenue. Injury suits over $25,000 go to the Third Judicial Circuit Court in Detroit.

**How long do I have?** - One year to give your no-fault insurer written notice. - Three years to sue the at-fault driver. - 120 days for a notice to Wayne County or the City. - 120 days for a road-defect notice against MDOT. - Two years to sue a bar, with a 120-day notice.
