## Farmington Hills Slip and Fall Lawyer

For twenty years a fall on someone else’s property in Michigan was close to unwinnable, because a hazard that was “open and obvious” ended the case before it began. That rule is gone. Since the Michigan Supreme Court’s July 2023 decision in *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil*, a Farmington Hills fall case turns on who had the duty to keep the place safe, what they knew about the hazard and when, what they did about it, and how much of the fall was your fault. In Farmington Hills those questions have local answers: the office and engineering campuses on Twelve Mile, Halsted, and Northwestern Highway, with their parking structures, plazas, and lobbies; the stores, restaurants, and shopping centers along Orchard Lake Road and Grand River; apartment, condominium, and senior communities throughout the city, where an association or a management company controls the walks; schools and city buildings, each with a 120-day notice rule; and sidewalks, which the City must maintain even beside roads it does not own.

## Where Farmington Hills falls happen, and who is responsible

**Office campuses and parking structures.** Farmington Hills’ office corridor on Twelve Mile, Halsted, Haggerty, and Northwestern Highway brings employees, visitors, and contractors onto private campuses every day: parking-structure ramps and stairwells, icy plazas and walks between buildings, wet lobby floors at the doors, and loading areas. A visitor is an invitee: a property owner or possessor must exercise reasonable care under the circumstances, which may include inspecting for hazards, correcting dangerous conditions, or providing appropriate warnings. The building owner, the property manager, the tenant that leases the space, and the snow-removal or janitorial contractor may each have had a role, and the leases and service contracts decide who. An employee who falls at their own employer’s building generally has a workers’ compensation claim against the employer (MCL 418.131), but may also have a third-party claim against a building owner, manager, or contractor that is not their employer, with the comp insurer repaid from that recovery (MCL 418.827). See our [Farmington Hills workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation/farmington-hills).

**Stores and shopping centers.** Grocery stores, pharmacies, restaurants, and retailers along Orchard Lake Road, Grand River, Twelve Mile, and Northwestern Highway: spills, freshly mopped floors without signs, merchandise in the aisle, ice at entrances, and potholes and broken curbs in shared lots. The case is decided on **notice**, actual (an employee saw or caused it) or constructive (it existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it). The lease decides whether the store or the shopping-center owner maintained the lot and the sidewalk out front, so we request it with the footage.

**Apartments, condominiums, and senior communities.** Farmington Hills has apartment complexes and condominium communities along Grand River, Twelve Mile, Orchard Lake, and Northwestern Highway, and many senior apartment buildings, independent living, and assisted living communities. In a condominium, the association, not the unit owner, usually controls the common walks, stairs, and lots, and its snow-removal contract and maintenance records are the evidence. Landlords must keep the premises and common areas fit for their intended use and in reasonable repair (MCL 554.139): broken stairs, loose handrails, dark stairwells and parking lots, and failed lighting are the usual claims. A lot with an inch or two of snow is still “fit” for parking under *Allison v AEW Capital* (2008), so tenant snow cases depend on whether the accumulation actually prevented use; the general negligence claim after *Kandil-Elsayed* is a separate route. A lease clause waiving the landlord’s liability for its own negligence is void. Older residents who fall often suffer hip and wrist fractures and head injuries that change their lives, and a fall in an assisted living community may be a care claim as well as a premises claim. See our [tenant injury page](/personal-injury/tenant-injuries) and [nursing home abuse page](/personal-injury/nursing-home-abuse).

**Sidewalks: the City, even beside a county or state road.** Most of Farmington Hills' main roads, including Eight Mile, belong to the Road Commission for Oakland County, and Grand River to MDOT. Sidewalks are different. The municipal corporation in which a sidewalk runs beside a county or state road must keep that sidewalk in reasonable repair (MCL 691.1402a(1)), and the duties of the State and the county under the highway exception stop at the roadway (MCL 691.1402(1)). So a fall on a broken public sidewalk along Orchard Lake, Twelve Mile, or Grand River is generally a claim against the City of Farmington Hills, not the Road Commission or MDOT. The City must have known, or should have known, of the defect at least 30 days before the fall; a defect under two inches is presumed to be reasonable repair unless the evidence shows otherwise, and the judge decides whether the presumption is rebutted (MCL 691.1402a(2)–(4)). Measure and photograph the defect with a ruler before it is patched, and serve written notice on the City stating the exact location, the defect, the injury, and the witnesses within 120 days (MCL 691.1404). A sidewalk inside an office campus, apartment complex, or shopping center belongs to its owner, and the claim is an ordinary premises claim.

**Schools, city buildings, and parks.** Farmington Hills is served mainly by Farmington Public Schools, with other districts serving parts of the city. Oakland Community College’s Orchard Ridge campus is on Orchard Lake Road. Each is a governmental agency: a fall caused by a dangerous or defective condition of one of their buildings proceeds under the public building exception, which requires the agency to have known of the defect and failed to fix it, with written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1406). The same rule applies to City Hall on Eleven Mile, the city’s community and recreation centers, and the public library. A fall on a city park path or field, outside a building, has no comparable exception and is difficult to bring.

**Hospitals and nursing homes.** A visitor’s fall at Corewell Health Farmington Hills Hospital or a medical office on Twelve Mile or Grand River is a premises claim. A patient’s fall, or a resident’s fall in one of the city’s two Medicare-certified nursing homes, may be premises, negligence, or medical malpractice, and which one decides the deadline. See our [Farmington Hills medical malpractice page](/personal-injury/medical-malpractice/farmington-hills).

## A parking-lot fall can be a no-fault claim

If you fell while getting into or out of your own car, or while loading it, Michigan’s no-fault law may cover your medical bills and lost wages even though no crash occurred. An injury from a parked vehicle is covered when the person was “occupying, entering into, or alighting from” it, or was injured by property being lifted onto or lowered from it during loading or unloading (MCL 500.3106(1)). That claim runs on the no-fault notice rules: written notice to the auto insurer within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). It sits alongside any premises claim against the lot’s or parking structure’s owner. A worker hurt on the job while loading, unloading, or getting into or out of a parked vehicle is generally limited to workers’ compensation for that injury, with narrow exceptions (MCL 500.3106(2)).

## The law after *Kandil-Elsayed*

Under the old rule (*Lugo v Ameritech*, 2001), an owner owed no duty to protect visitors from a danger an average person would have noticed, and insurers denied fall claims on that basis. In *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil* (Michigan Supreme Court, July 28, 2023), the Court held that whether a hazard was open and obvious no longer decides whether a duty existed. It is now a factor in whether the owner breached the duty of reasonable care, and in how much of the fault belongs to the person who fell; both are questions for a jury. That does not make every fall a case. A visible hazard still matters: if the jury assigns you some fault, your damages are reduced by that share, and above half you recover no pain-and-suffering damages, only reduced economic losses (MCL 600.2959).

Duty also depends on why you were there. A customer, client, or business visitor is an invitee, owed inspection and repair; a social guest at a home is a licensee, owed a warning of hidden dangers the owner knows about; a trespasser is owed protection from willful and wanton misconduct. Treating every visitor the same, or saying a fall claim “almost always” points to the owner, overstates or understates the claim.

## Snow and ice

An owner must take reasonable steps, within a reasonable time after snow or ice accumulates, to make walkways, entrances, plazas, and lots safe. What is reasonable depends on the property: an office campus opening at 7 a.m. after an overnight storm is expected to have treated its walks and parking-structure ramps, and a contractor with a salting contract that missed the morning run may share the fault. The law does not require an owner to keep every surface bare at every moment of a storm. Black ice from refreezing meltwater, a leaking gutter or downspout, or snow piled where it melts across the walk can be a condition the owner created, and those cases are stronger than natural accumulation.

Some Michigan communities require adjacent owners to clear snow and ice from public sidewalks by ordinance. Photograph the ice, the path, the alternatives you had, and the weather the same day.

## What to do after a fall in Farmington Hills

1. **Get medical care the same day** and tell the provider where and how you fell.
2. **Photograph the hazard immediately**, from several angles, with something for scale, before it is mopped, salted, or patched. Photograph your shoes.
3. **Report the fall** to the manager, the landlord, building security, or the City, and get the incident report number. Do not give a recorded statement to any insurer.
4. **Get witness names**, including employees, and note the time; it matters for constructive notice.
5. **Measure a sidewalk defect** with a ruler in the photograph.
6. **Keep the shoes** unworn and unwashed.
7. **Call a lawyer within days.** Footage may be overwritten in days; if the City or a school district may be involved, a short notice period is already running.

## Frequently asked questions

**I slipped on ice in an office-park parking structure on Twelve Mile. Who is responsible?** Usually the building owner or property manager, and possibly the snow-removal contractor, depending on the lease and the service contract. If you work in the building, workers’ compensation may pay your benefits, and a claim against an owner or contractor that is not your employer can be brought too.

**I fell on a broken sidewalk along Orchard Lake Road. Is that the Road Commission’s responsibility?** Generally not. The Road Commission maintains the road, but the City must keep the public sidewalk beside it in reasonable repair, so the claim is usually against the City. It must have known or should have known of the defect at least 30 days before, defects under two inches are presumed reasonable repair, and written notice must reach the City within 120 days.

**My mother fell in her senior apartment building. Do we have a case?** Possibly. A landlord must keep common areas in reasonable repair and fit for their use, and a general negligence claim may apply too. If the building provides care, as an assisted living community does, the claim may also be about supervision, which can change the deadline.

**The ice was obvious. Do I still have a case?** Possibly. Since 2023, an obvious hazard no longer ends the case. The questions are whether the owner should have salted or cleared it, whether you had a realistic alternative route, and how much of the fault is yours.

**I fell getting out of my car in a parking lot. Is that a car accident claim?** It may be. No-fault can cover injuries from entering or leaving a parked vehicle, separate from any claim against the lot’s owner.

**How long do I have?** Three years to sue a private owner; 120 days for written notice to the City or a school district; and one year to notify a no-fault insurer. The footage disappears far sooner.
