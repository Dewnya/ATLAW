## Michigan Passenger Injury Lawyer

If you were injured as a passenger in a Michigan car crash, you have the one thing most injured people do not: no one is going to argue that you caused it. What you do have to get right is which insurance company pays your medical bills, which drivers’ policies you can claim against (your own driver’s included), and the deadlines, which do not wait because you were not behind the wheel. Passengers lose claims not on fault but on paperwork, by filing with the wrong insurer, by waiting past the one-year no-fault deadline, or by settling with one driver before learning that a second policy was available.

This page explains how Michigan law treats injured passengers: whose no-fault insurance pays, who you can sue, what happens when the driver was a family member, a friend, a rideshare driver, or a bus operator, how children’s claims differ, and every deadline.

## Whose insurance pays a passenger’s medical bills

Michigan is a no-fault state. Your medical bills, 85% of your lost wages, replacement services, and attendant care are paid as personal injury protection (PIP) benefits by an insurer the law assigns, regardless of who caused the crash (MCL 500.3107). For a passenger, the order of responsibility is set by MCL 500.3114, and it is not the driver’s insurer.

1. **Your own no-fault policy**, if you have one. It follows you into any car.
2. **The policy of your spouse or of a relative you live with**, if you do not have your own (MCL 500.3114(1)).
3. **The Michigan Assigned Claims Plan (MACP)**, if neither you nor anyone in your household has a policy (MCL 500.3114(4), 500.3172). The MACP assigns your claim to an insurer. Medical benefits under the plan are capped at $250,000.

Before June 11, 2019, a passenger without household coverage claimed from the insurer of the car they were riding in. The 2019 reform removed that step. Pages that still tell passengers to file with “the driver’s insurance” are describing the old law, and a claim filed there will be returned months later, with the one-year clock still running.

Three exceptions that catch people:

- **Employees.** If you were a passenger in your employer’s vehicle, the employer’s insurer pays (MCL 500.3114(3)), and workers’ compensation may apply as well.
- **Owners of uninsured cars.** If you own or are the registrant of the car you were riding in and it was uninsured, you cannot collect PIP benefits at all (MCL 500.3113(b)). A passenger who does not own the car is not barred by the car’s lack of insurance.
- **Out-of-state passengers.** A non-resident injured in a vehicle that is not registered or insured in Michigan generally has no Michigan PIP claim (MCL 500.3113(c)). This matters in Wayne County, where visitors from Ontario and Ohio are in crashes every week; the claim then depends on their home coverage and on the lawsuit against the at-fault driver.

Whichever insurer is responsible, you must give it written notice within one year of the crash (MCL 500.3145(1)), and it must pay each bill within 30 days of reasonable proof or owe 12% interest (MCL 500.3142). ## Who a passenger can sue

PIP pays bills. It does not pay for pain and suffering. For that, you sue the driver, or drivers, whose negligence caused the crash, if your injury is a serious impairment of body function, permanent serious disfigurement, or death (MCL 500.3135(1)). The same claim recovers economic losses that PIP does not cover: wages above the monthly cap, wage loss after three years, and the difference between what PIP paid and what you lost.

As a passenger, your potential defendants are:

- **The other driver**, if they were at fault, through their liability insurance.
- **Your own driver**, if they were at fault, through theirs. A passenger is not required to choose. Michigan abolished the rule that once barred passengers from suing the driver who gave them a ride, and nothing in the law prevents a passenger from suing a friend, a spouse, or a parent who was driving negligently. The claim is paid by the driver’s insurer, not by the driver. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: policy “family member” or “household” exclusions; Michigan courts have limited their enforceability]**
- **The owner of the car**, if different from the driver. Michigan’s owner’s liability statute (MCL 257.401) makes a vehicle owner liable for the negligent driving of anyone using the car with the owner’s consent. A passenger injured by a friend driving a borrowed car has a claim against the owner’s policy.
- **Both drivers**, when fault is shared. Michigan assigns each defendant a percentage of fault and, in most cases, each pays only that percentage (MCL 600.2956, 600.6304). A passenger’s claim is therefore filed against every driver who contributed, so that the full amount is covered.
- **The employer**, if the at-fault driver was working. **The bar or store** that served a visibly intoxicated at-fault driver (MCL 436.1801, with a 120-day notice rule). **The manufacturer**, if a defect in the vehicle or a seat belt or airbag made your injury worse.

The policy limits are the reason this matters. Michigan’s minimum liability coverage is $50,000 per person and $100,000 per crash (MCL 500.3009(1)). Three injured passengers in one car share that $100,000. The second policy, the owner’s policy, and your own uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage are how a serious injury is compensated when the first policy is not enough.

## Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage for passengers

If the at-fault driver had no insurance, or not enough, uninsured/underinsured motorist (UM/UIM) coverage pays what their policy would have. A passenger can usually claim UM/UIM under two policies: the policy on the car they were riding in, which covers its occupants, and their own or a household member’s policy. Which one pays first, and whether both pay, depends on the policies’ “other insurance” language, which is why the policies are requested in the first week. UM/UIM claims have their own notice and deadline terms written into the policy, some shorter than the three-year statute. Our [uninsured motorist](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims) page explains the coverage.

## Can a passenger be blamed?

Rarely, and only in specific ways:

- **Seat belt.** Michigan law allows a failure to wear a seat belt to be considered evidence of negligence, but it cannot reduce your recovery by more than 5% (MCL 257.710e(8)). An insurer that offers you half of your claim’s value “because you weren’t belted” is misstating the statute.
- **Riding with a drunk driver.** A passenger who knew the driver was intoxicated and rode anyway can be assigned a share of fault, which reduces the recovery by that percentage and bars it entirely above 50% (MCL 600.2959). The facts matter: what you knew, what you saw, whether you had an alternative.
- **Interfering with the driver.** Grabbing the wheel or distracting the driver is fault; being in the car is not.

Passengers are also witnesses. Your account of what the driver was doing in the seconds before the crash, including whether they were on the phone, is evidence in your own case and may be the only evidence of distraction. Write it down the day of the crash.

## When the driver was family

Most passengers are riding with someone they know. Michigan law does not treat that as a reason to forgo a claim:

- A spouse may sue a spouse; a child may sue a parent, through a “next friend,” for negligent driving. The recovery comes from the liability insurer, which collected premiums for exactly this.
- The PIP claim is unaffected: if you live with the driver, their policy is likely yours under the household rule, and your benefits are paid regardless of fault.
- Clients worry about raising a relative’s premiums or “suing family.” The claim is against the policy, the premium consequence is the insurer’s decision in any crash the relative caused, and the alternative is paying for your own injury. We explain the options and follow the client’s decision.

## Children injured as passengers

Children are the passengers with the least control and the longest consequences. Michigan law treats their claims differently in three ways:

1. **PIP deadlines are not extended for children.** The Michigan Supreme Court has held that the rule pausing deadlines for minors (MCL 600.5851) does not apply to no-fault’s one-year notice and one-year-back rules (Joseph v Auto Club Insurance Association, 2012). A parent must give notice and claim benefits within the same one year an adult has.
2. **The lawsuit deadline is extended.** A child’s claim against the at-fault driver may be filed until the child’s 19th birthday (MCL 600.5851(1)). That does not make waiting wise: evidence and witnesses disappear.
3. **Settlements require a judge’s approval.** A settlement of a minor’s claim must be approved by the court, and larger amounts are placed in a restricted account or conservatorship until the child is 18 (MCR 2.420).

Michigan’s child restraint law requires a child under 4 to ride in a car seat in the rear seat where available, and a child under 8 and shorter than 4’9” in a car seat or booster (MCL 257.710d). An improperly restrained child is not at fault for being improperly restrained; the question is whether the restraint or its installation was defective, which can be a claim of its own.

## Rideshare, bus, and other commercial passengers

- **Uber and Lyft passengers** are covered by the company’s $1 million liability policy during the ride (MCL 257.2123), plus the no-fault rules above. Our [Uber and Lyft accident](/personal-injury/rideshare-accidents) page covers it.
- **Bus passengers** on SMART, DDOT, school, charter, or intercity buses face special rules: a government operator, a federal motor carrier, or a common carrier’s higher duty of care. Our [bus accident](/personal-injury/bus-accidents) page covers each.
- **Taxi, limousine, and shuttle passengers** claim against the commercial policy and, where the vehicle was a “for hire” vehicle, may fall under commercial-vehicle insurance minimums.

## What to do after you are injured as a passenger

1. **Get your own medical evaluation**, even if the driver is a friend who says they are fine. Your injury is documented in your records, not theirs.
2. **Get the drivers’ and the owner’s insurance information**, for every vehicle. Photograph the cards. You may need all of them.
3. **Report the crash to your own insurer, or your household’s**, within days. That is where your PIP claim starts, and the one-year notice runs from the crash.
4. **Do not give a recorded statement** to the drivers’ liability insurers before you understand your claims. You will be asked whether the driver you were with seemed impaired, distracted, or speeding, and the answer affects which policy pays.
5. **Do not sign a release for one driver** until you know whether a second driver, the owner, or your UM/UIM coverage is in play. Releasing the wrong party can end the dram shop claim (MCL 436.1801(5)) and complicate the rest.
6. **Call a passenger injury lawyer.** The first task is to identify every policy and give every notice inside the deadlines.

## What a passenger injury claim is worth

Two categories:

- **No-fault benefits**: all reasonable medical care to your policy limit, 85% of lost wages up to $7,455 a month for three years (for crashes on or after October 1, 2026), $20 a day for replacement services, attendant care, and mileage.
- **The claim against the at-fault drivers**: pain and suffering, loss of enjoyment of life, scarring, and economic losses PIP does not cover, if your injury meets the serious impairment threshold.

What moves the number: the severity and permanence of the injury, the medical evidence that documents it, how many policies are available and their limits, and whether fault is clear, which for a passenger it usually is. A passenger with a documented serious injury and two available policies is in a different position from one with one minimum-limits policy and no UM/UIM coverage, and an early policy search tells you which case you have.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan passenger injury claims

**I was a passenger. Whose insurance pays my medical bills?** Your own no-fault policy if you have one; otherwise a policy of your spouse or a relative you live with; otherwise the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3114). Not the driver’s insurer, unless the driver is in your household.

**Can I sue the driver of the car I was in?** Yes, if their negligence caused the crash and your injury is a serious impairment of body function (MCL 500.3135). Michigan has no rule barring a passenger from suing the driver who gave them the ride, and the claim is paid by the driver’s insurance.

**Can I sue both drivers?** Yes, when both were at fault. Michigan assigns each a percentage of fault, and in most cases each pays its share (MCL 600.6304), so the claim is made against both.

**What if the driver I was with had no insurance?** Your PIP benefits still come from your own or household policy, or from the Assigned Claims Plan; you are not barred unless you own the uninsured car. For pain and suffering, you claim against the other driver if at fault, and against uninsured motorist coverage on your own or a household policy.

**I wasn’t wearing a seat belt. Does that ruin my case?** No. Michigan law caps any reduction for not wearing a seat belt at 5% (MCL 257.710e(8)).

**Can a passenger be found at fault?** Only for the passenger’s own conduct: knowingly riding with a drunk driver, or interfering with the driver. Being a passenger is not fault.

**My child was hurt as a passenger. How long do we have?** One year for the no-fault claim, with no extension for minors (Joseph v ACIA, 2012). Until the child’s 19th birthday for the lawsuit against the at-fault driver (MCL 600.5851). Any settlement must be approved by a judge (MCR 2.420).

**The driver was my husband. Do I have a claim?** Yes. Michigan allows a spouse to sue a spouse for negligent driving, and the claim is against the insurer. Your no-fault benefits come from the household policy regardless of fault.

**I was visiting from Ontario or Ohio. Do I get Michigan no-fault benefits?** Generally not, if the vehicle you were in was not registered or insured in Michigan (MCL 500.3113(c)). You keep your claim against the at-fault Michigan driver, and your home coverage may apply. Call before you assume you have nothing.

**How much is a passenger injury claim worth?** It depends on the injury, the medical evidence, and the policies available. Michigan’s minimum coverage is $50,000 per person and $100,000 per crash, shared among everyone injured, so the second policy and your UM/UIM coverage often decide what is recoverable.
