## St. Clair Shores, MI Truck Accident Lawyer

St. Clair Shores’ heavy trucks travel three kinds of roads.

**I-94.** The freeway runs along the city’s west side between Eight Mile and Thirteen Mile, with interchanges and service drives at the mile roads. It carries commuter traffic to Detroit and freight between Detroit and Port Huron, where the Blue Water Bridge crosses to Canada.

**The service drives and the mile roads.** Trucks leave the freeway at the interchanges, run the service drives, and turn east on the mile roads toward Harper, Greater Mack, and Jefferson.

**Harper, Jefferson, and the neighborhoods.**

- **Harper Avenue,** the city’s commercial strip, carries box trucks and tractor-trailers supplying stores and restaurants.
- **Jefferson Avenue** carries deliveries to the waterfront restaurants and marinas, and boat haulers in spring and fall.
- **The neighborhoods** see construction trucks, garbage trucks, and school buses.

In 2024, according to the Michigan State Police, 1,257 heavy trucks and buses were involved in crashes in Macomb County, compared with 3,916 in Wayne County and 2,081 in Oakland. Statewide, 110 people were killed and 3,613 injured in heavy truck and bus crashes. St. Clair Shores recorded 1,356 crashes of all kinds, 454 of them on I-94.

A truck crash is not a car crash with a larger vehicle:

- **The rules are federal.** The driver and the carrier are governed by federal safety regulations, and a violation is evidence of negligence.
- **The insurance is larger.** The carrier must carry $750,000 or more in insurance.
- **The records expire.** The driver’s electronic logs and the truck’s engine data, the records that prove the case, can be lawfully discarded within months.
- **The other side moves fast.** The carrier’s insurer will have an investigator on the case within days.

## Where St. Clair Shores truck crashes happen

**The I-94 mainline.** The interchanges come about a mile apart, so trucks are constantly merging, exiting, and changing lanes among commuters. The crashes are:

- rear-end and underride crashes where traffic slows or backs up from an exit ramp
- lane changes into a car in the blind spot on the right side of the cab
- a truck merging from a short entrance ramp into traffic that cannot make room
- jackknife and loss-of-control crashes in snow and ice

A car that strikes the side or rear of a trailer can go under it. That is an **underride** crash, which is often fatal or catastrophic. Trailers must carry rear impact guards that meet federal standards (49 CFR 393.86). A missing, damaged, or substandard guard is evidence against the carrier and the trailer’s owner.

Snow and ice. I-94 can turn icy and white in minutes. Federal rules require a truck driver to use extreme caution in hazardous conditions, to reduce speed, and to stop if conditions become too dangerous (49 CFR 392.14). A truck that kept its speed into a snow squall, or a carrier that pushed a driver to keep moving, is evidence of negligence.

**The interchanges and service drives.** At the ramp terminals and service-drive signals, trucks leaving the freeway turn across the service drive onto the mile roads, and trucks heading for the freeway turn into the ramps. The crashes are:

- wide right turns from the service drive onto a mile road that catch a car on the inside of the turn
- left turns across the service drive or the mile road into the ramp
- trucks running a late signal at the ramp terminal
- rear-end crashes in the queue on the service drive

In the State Police’s 2025 intersection data, 12 Mile at I-94 had 86 crashes and 10 Mile at I-94 had 66.

**Harper Avenue.** Box trucks, beverage trucks, and tractor-trailers supply the stores and restaurants along Harper. The crashes are:

- wide right turns into a store driveway
- trucks backing into and out of loading areas and service drives
- trucks stopped in a lane for a delivery
- pedestrians struck by a truck in a parking lot

**Jefferson Avenue and the marinas.** Jefferson carries deliveries to the waterfront restaurants, and in spring and fall, boat haulers move boats to and from the marinas and storage yards. A wide or oversize load needs a permit, and its route, escort, and hours are conditions of that permit. The crashes are:

- wide turns into marina and restaurant driveways across a cyclist or a car in the next lane
- an oversize load striking an oncoming car, a pole, or a sign
- trucks backing into a marina or restaurant lot from Jefferson
- trucks stopped in a lane for a delivery

A pickup towing a private boat is not a commercial vehicle. A hauler moving boats for pay may be. Whether the federal rules apply depends on the vehicle’s weight and its use, so the first question is who owned the truck and the trailer and why they were on the road.

**Construction and garbage trucks.** Construction trucks serve building, seawall, and road work, and garbage and recycling trucks work the neighborhoods. The crashes are backing crashes, crashes with vehicles stopped in a lane, and children and pedestrians struck by a truck pulling away. Where a business’s delivery or loading practice routinely puts trucks in the street, the business can share responsibility. See our [pedestrian accident page](/personal-injury/pedestrian-accidents).

**Lost and spilled loads.** Cargo, scrap, gravel, or debris that falls from a truck is a violation of the federal cargo securement rules (49 CFR 393.100 and following) and of Michigan law (MCL 257.720). The carrier and the company that loaded the truck can both be responsible.

**Whose road it is.** The road owner can be any of three agencies:

- **MDOT** for I-94 and its ramps. A road-defect claim against the State goes to the Court of Claims, with written notice filed there within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **Macomb County** (the Department of Roads) or the City of St. Clair Shores for Harper, Greater Mack, Jefferson, Little Mack, and the mile roads, depending on the segment. The I-94 service drives may be MDOT’s or the local agency’s. Written notice is due within 120 days (MCL 691.1402, 691.1404).
- **The City** for the local streets. Written notice is due within 120 days.

## Canadian carriers on I-94

I-94 is the freight route between Detroit and the Blue Water Bridge at Port Huron, and carriers based in Canada use it. A Canadian carrier operating in the United States is held to the same federal safety rules as a U.S. carrier:

- **Registration:** it must register with FMCSA and hold a USDOT number.
- **Insurance:** it must carry the same minimum insurance.
- **A process agent:** it must designate an agent to receive legal papers in each state where it operates (49 CFR 366).

A crash with a Canadian carrier raises three practical questions:

- **The right defendant.** The registered carrier, the owner of the trailer, and any U.S. broker or shipper may all be responsible.
- **The right insurer.** The policy on file with FMCSA, and any excess coverage, are the first insurance we identify.
- **The right court.** A case against a foreign carrier is often filed in, or removed to, federal court in Detroit.

Evidence held in Canada, such as the carrier’s dispatch and maintenance records, is obtained through discovery once the carrier is a party.

## Checking a trucking company’s safety record

Every interstate carrier, and many Michigan intrastate carriers, has a USDOT number painted on the cab door. That number pulls up the carrier’s federal record:

- **The FMCSA Company Snapshot** (on the SAFER website) shows the carrier’s registered name and address, its number of trucks and drivers, and its inspections and crashes over the past two years," and add after the bullet: including out-of-service rates for its vehicles and drivers.
- **The FMCSA Safety Measurement System** groups the carrier’s inspection and crash results by category, including unsafe driving, hours-of-service compliance, and vehicle maintenance. Some results are not shown to the public.
- **The carrier’s safety rating,** if FMCSA has rated it, is “satisfactory,” “conditional,” or “unsatisfactory.” Many carriers have never been rated.

A poor record does not by itself prove fault in a crash. It can support a claim that the carrier failed to supervise or maintain its fleet, and that a shipper or broker was negligent in hiring a carrier with that record.

## Who is responsible, and who pays

**The carrier, not just the driver.** The motor carrier is responsible for its drivers’ compliance with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations. It is liable for a driver’s negligence in the course of employment. Michigan applies the federal rules to intrastate carriers (MCL 480.11a). A local hauler running between a yard in Macomb County and a store on Harper is held to the same standards as an interstate carrier.

These are the rules that decide truck cases most often:

| Rule | 49 CFR | What it means for the case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hours of service | Part 395 | 11 hours driving in a 14-hour window after 10 hours off; 60/70-hour weekly limits; recorded on an electronic logging device. Some short-haul drivers are exempt from logs but not from the limits. |
| Driver qualification | Part 391 | CDL, medical certificate, driving record, background check. Negligent hiring and retention are claims against the carrier. |
| Post-crash testing | Part 382 | Alcohol within 8 hours and drugs within 32 hours after a fatal crash, or after an injury or tow-away crash with a citation. |
| Inspection and maintenance | Part 396 | Pre-trip inspections, defect reports, maintenance records. Brake, tire, lighting, and reflective-tape cases are maintenance cases. |
| Cargo securement | 393.100–393.136 | Loads must be contained or secured so they cannot shift or fall. A spilled load is evidence of a violation. |
| Equipment: rear impact guards, lighting, reflectors | Part 393 | Underride guards (393.86) and conspicuity marking. A trailer that cannot be seen at night or lacks a working guard is a defective trailer. |
| Hazardous conditions; stopped vehicles; phones | 392.14, 392.22, 392.82 | Reduce speed or stop in snow, ice, and fog; warning devices for a stopped truck; no handheld phone use. |
| Minimum insurance | Part 387 | $750,000 general freight; $1 million oil; $5 million hazardous materials. |
| Foreign carriers’ process agents | Part 366 | A designated agent to receive legal papers in each state of operation. |

**The owner of the truck or trailer.** In Michigan, a vehicle’s owner is liable for its negligent operation by someone driving with the owner’s consent (MCL 257.401). When the tractor, the trailer, and the driver belong to different companies, each may be a defendant, although a company in the business of renting or leasing vehicles is generally shielded from owner liability by federal law (49 USC 30106). When an owner-operator is leased to a carrier, the lease generally makes the carrier responsible for the truck’s operation (49 CFR 376.12).

**Michigan no-fault still applies to you.** Your own auto insurer pays these benefits regardless of fault (MCL 500.3107):

- your medical bills, up to the PIP medical level you chose (MCL 500.3107c)
- 85 percent of lost wages, up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year
- replacement services
- attendant care

Written notice is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). If you have no household policy, a vehicle occupant claims from the insurer of the vehicle they were in, and then the Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3114(4), 500.3172). A pedestrian or cyclist with no household policy claims through the Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3115).

Your claim against the driver and the carrier is for pain and suffering and for losses beyond no-fault, including medical bills above your PIP level. It requires a threshold injury (MCL 500.3135) and must be filed within three years (MCL 600.5805(2)). Michigan’s comparative fault rule is modified, not pure:

- **More than 50 percent at fault:** you cannot recover pain-and-suffering damages.
- **50 percent or less:** your share reduces them (MCL 500.3135(2)(b); MCL 600.2959). At exactly 50 percent, you recover half.

The noneconomic damages caps apply to medical malpractice and to product liability claims, such as a claim against the maker of a failed part (MCL 600.1483, 600.2946a), not to the claim against the driver and carrier.

The difference from a car case is the money available: the carrier’s $750,000-plus policy, often with excess coverage above it. If the carrier’s coverage still falls short, your own underinsured motorist coverage may add to it. Your insurer’s written consent is needed before you accept the carrier’s limit. See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

**Other defendants.**

- the shipper or broker that loaded the truck or hired an unsafe carrier
- the store, restaurant, or marina whose driveway, staging, or delivery practice contributed
- the general contractor on a construction site whose trucks were involved
- the maintenance contractor
- the manufacturer of a failed component or a defective guard ([product liability](/personal-injury/product-liability))
- for a government truck (MDOT, the Macomb County Department of Roads, the City of St. Clair Shores, SMART, or a school bus), the agency, under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405)

**If you were the truck driver.** Workers’ compensation pays your benefits, and your employer usually cannot be sued (MCL 418.131). If you were injured in your employer’s truck, no-fault benefits generally come from the insurer of that truck (MCL 500.3114(3)). They are coordinated with comp, which is paid first (MCL 500.3109(1)). You can also sue a third party, such as another driver or a business whose dock or yard caused the crash. See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

## The evidence, and how fast it disappears

Carriers must keep two kinds of records for a limited time:

- hours-of-service records, for six months (49 CFR 395.8)
- driver inspection reports, for three months (49 CFR 396.11)

After that, the regulations no longer require them. Other evidence goes faster:

- store, restaurant, marina, and gas-station cameras along Harper and Jefferson, and dash cameras, overwrite in days to weeks
- the truck’s engine control module and event data recorder can be cleared when the truck is repaired and returned to service
- a spilled load is swept up within hours
- a damaged rear guard can be replaced before anyone measures it

The first act in a St. Clair Shores truck case is a **preservation letter**. It goes to the carrier, its insurer, the tractor and trailer owners, and the shipper or broker. Where the crash happened at a driveway, a loading area, a marina, or a construction site, it also goes to the business, the property owner, or the contractor. It demands:

- the ELD and engine data
- dash camera footage
- the driver’s qualification file and post-crash test results
- dispatch records, bills of lading, gate logs, and delivery records
- maintenance files
- for an oversize load, the permit and the escort records
- the lease, if an owner-operator was driving
- the truck and trailer themselves, for inspection of the brakes, lights, load securement, and rear guard

Once a party knows a claim is coming, destroying that evidence exposes it to spoliation sanctions.

## What to do after a St. Clair Shores truck crash

1. **Call 911 and get medical care the same day.** Henry Ford St. John Hospital, just south on Moross in Detroit, is a Level I trauma center, and Henry Ford Macomb Hospital in Clinton Township is a Level II. Corewell Health Grosse Pointe is also nearby.
2. **Photograph the truck.** Get these shots: the cab and trailer; the USDOT number and carrier name on the door, and any Canadian plate or registration; both plates, and the trailer number if it differs; the load and anything that fell from it, and any “oversize load” banner; the rear guard and reflective tape; and the ramp, service drive, or driveway if the truck was entering or leaving one.
3. **Get the police report.** Crashes on city streets are worked by the St. Clair Shores Police Department. A crash on I-94 may be worked by the State Police or the city. A serious crash may produce a separate commercial vehicle inspection report.
4. **Do not speak to the carrier’s insurer or its investigator.**
5. **Report to your own insurer and file the Application for No-Fault Benefits** promptly.
6. **Call a lawyer within days,** so the preservation letter goes out before the records expire and the truck is repaired or leaves the country.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many truck accidents happen in St. Clair Shores?** The State’s county profile does not break out truck crashes by city. Countywide, 1,257 heavy trucks and buses were involved in Macomb County crashes in 2024. A third of all crashes in the city, 454 of 1,356, were on I-94.

**A semi changed lanes into me on I-94. Who is at fault?** Usually the truck driver, who must make sure a lane is clear before moving into it. A truck has large blind spots on its right side and directly behind the trailer, and the carrier is responsible for training its drivers to check them. The truck’s dash camera and engine data are often the deciding evidence, and they can be overwritten quickly.

**The truck that hit me had Canadian plates. Can I still bring a claim here?** Yes. A Canadian carrier operating in the United States must register with FMCSA, carry the federal minimum insurance, and name an agent in Michigan to receive legal papers. The case can be filed here, though the carrier may move it to federal court in Detroit.

**A truck jackknifed on I-94 in the snow. Isn’t that just the weather?** Not necessarily. Federal rules require truck drivers to slow down in snow and ice and to stop if conditions are too dangerous. A truck that kept its speed into a squall, or a carrier that pushed a driver to keep moving, can be liable.

**A boat hauler swung wide on Jefferson and hit me. Who is responsible?** It depends on who owned the truck and the trailer and whether the move was commercial. A hauler moving boats for pay may be subject to the federal rules and to the conditions of an oversize-load permit, and its insurer pays. A private owner towing their own boat is covered by their auto policy.

**How do I check a trucking company’s safety record?** Find the USDOT number on the cab door or in the police report and look it up on the FMCSA’s SAFER website. The Company Snapshot shows the carrier’s inspections, crashes, out-of-service rates, and any safety rating. Its insurance on file is in FMCSA’s Licensing and Insurance system.

**Is there a cap on what I can recover from a trucking company?** Not on pain and suffering against the driver or carrier. Michigan’s damages caps apply to medical malpractice and product liability claims. In practice, the limit is the insurance available: the carrier’s policy, any excess policy, and your own underinsured coverage.

**Why does the lawyer need to act within days?** Because the records expire. Hours-of-service logs must be kept for six months and inspection reports for three. Camera footage lasts days or weeks. The truck’s electronic data and the trailer’s damage can disappear when the truck is repaired, and a foreign carrier’s truck may soon be out of the country.
