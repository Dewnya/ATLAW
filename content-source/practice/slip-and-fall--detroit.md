## Detroit Slip and Fall Lawyer

For twenty years a fall on someone else’s property in Michigan was close to unwinnable, because a hazard that was “open and obvious” ended the case before it began. That rule is gone. Since the Michigan Supreme Court’s July 2023 decision in *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil*, a Detroit fall case turns on the questions it should have turned on all along: who had the duty to keep the place safe, what they knew about the hazard and when, what they did about it, and how much of the fall was your fault. In Detroit those questions have local answers. Half the city rents, much of its housing stock predates 1960, the City maintains the roads but assigns the sidewalks to the adjacent owner, and a fall on City property starts a 120-day clock the day you hit the ground.

## Where Detroiters fall, and who is responsible

**Rental housing.** Broken and rotted porch steps, missing handrails, unlit stairwells and hallways, loose carpet, ice on the only walkway from the lot to the door. Michigan landlords must keep the premises and common areas fit for their intended use and in reasonable repair (MCL 554.139), and a breach of these statutory duties may support a claim when the dangerous condition causes injury, depending on the circumstances. The duty is strongest in common areas; inside the unit, the lease and the landlord’s knowledge of the defect matter. In Allison v. AEW Capital Management (2008), the Michigan Supreme Court held that a parking lot with a modest accumulation of snow and ice was not necessarily unfit for its intended use. More recently, in Bowerman v. Red Oak Management Co. (July 2026), the Court emphasized that the fitness of a common area must be evaluated in light of its intended use and the circumstances, including the needs of the tenants. Snow and ice claims therefore depend on whether the landlord maintained the area in a condition reasonably fit for its intended use. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]** Where the landlord is the **Detroit Housing Commission**, the claim proceeds under the public building exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1406), with written notice within 120 days of hiring a lawyer; suit within 2 years. Where the property is owned by the Detroit Land Bank Authority, the agency’s public status and the specific property conditions must be considered when determining whether a claim is available and what notice requirements may apply. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: DLBA status]**

**Stores, gas stations, and restaurants.** Grocery and dollar stores, gas stations, pharmacies, fast food, and the Eastern Market vendors: spills, recently mopped floors without signs, ice at the entrance, potholes and wheel stops in the lot, unmarked steps. You are an **invitee**, owed the highest duty: the owner must inspect, fix what it finds, and warn of what it cannot fix. The case is decided on **notice**: whether an employee saw or caused the hazard (actual notice) or whether it existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it (constructive notice). Footage, cleaning logs, and incident reports prove it, and the owner holds all three.

**Downtown, Midtown, and the venues.** Ford Field, Comerica Park, Little Caesars Arena, the Fox, the three casinos, Huntington Place, the Riverwalk, and the bars of Greektown and Corktown. These are high-traffic premises with extensive surveillance, which cuts both ways: the footage exists, and it is overwritten on a schedule. Some Detroit venues involve public authorities and private operators. Comerica Park is associated with the Detroit-Wayne County Stadium Authority, while the Detroit Regional Convention Facility Authority oversees Huntington Place under a long-term lease from the City. When a fall occurs at one of these venues, the responsible property owner, operator, and applicable governmental-immunity rules must be identified before determining whether a claim is available. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: ownership and GTLA status of the stadium and convention authorities]** Falls at bars that over-served the patron who fell, or who caused the fall, can add a dram shop claim (MCL 436.1801). See our [drunk driving accident page](/personal-injury/drunk-driving-accidents).

**City sidewalks.** Detroit’s ordinance places the duty to maintain and clear the sidewalk on the adjacent property owner, but that duty alone does not make the owner liable to a pedestrian; an owner who made the sidewalk more dangerous can be (Detroit City Code, Chapter 43). A claim against the City itself for a sidewalk defect proceeds under the highway exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1402), with two rules that decide most cases: a defect under two inches is presumed to be reasonable repair (MCL 691.1402a), so the defect must be measured and photographed with a ruler before it is patched; and written notice stating the exact location, the nature of the defect, the injury, and the known witnesses must be served on the City within 120 days (MCL 691.1404). Notice to the wrong agency is not notice; sidewalks along state trunklines are still the City’s (MCL 691.1402a).

**City buildings, parks, and transit.** Recreation centers, libraries, the Coleman A. Young Municipal Center, DDOT terminals, and the People Mover stations. A fall caused by a dangerous condition of a public building is a claim under MCL 691.1406 with 120-day notice. Belle Isle has been a state park since 2014, so a fall there is a claim against the Michigan Department of Natural Resources in the Court of Claims, with written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1406). Parks and open land are generally not “buildings,” and the exceptions are read narrowly. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

**Hospitals and nursing homes.** A fall by a patient at a Detroit hospital or in one of the city’s 23 Medicare-certified nursing homes may be a premises case, a nursing home negligence case, or medical malpractice with its notice and caps, depending on whether the fall resulted from a failure of medical judgment (an unassessed fall risk, a missing bed alarm) or an ordinary hazard (a wet floor). The distinction decides the deadline. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]** See our [Detroit nursing home abuse page](/personal-injury/nursing-home-abuse/detroit) and [Detroit medical malpractice page](/personal-injury/medical-malpractice/detroit).

**Workplaces.** A fall at work is a workers’ compensation claim against the employer (MCL 418.131), plus a premises or negligence claim against a third party who owned or controlled the property or created the hazard. ## The law after *Kandil-Elsayed*

Under the old rule (*Lugo v Ameritech*, 2001), a property owner owed no duty to protect visitors from a danger an average person would have noticed, and because almost every hazard is visible in hindsight, insurers denied many fall claims and courts dismissed many of the ones that were filed. In *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil, Inc.* (Michigan Supreme Court, July 28, 2023), the Court held that whether a hazard was open and obvious no longer decides whether a duty existed. It is now a factor in two jury questions: whether the owner breached the duty of reasonable care, and how much of the fault belongs to the person who fell. If the jury assigns you some fault, your recovery is reduced by that share; if it assigns you more than half, you recover no damages for pain and suffering (MCL 600.2959).

In practice, a Detroit store that left a spill on the floor for an hour cannot win by saying the spill was visible, and a landlord cannot win by saying the broken step was obvious. The questions are what they knew, what they did, and whether you had a realistic alternative route. Ice covering the only path from the lot to the door is a strong case; ice in a corner nobody needed to cross is not. The insurer will still argue your fault, your footwear, your phone, and your speed, and the evidence from the first hour answers it.

## Snow and ice in Detroit

Winter falls are among the most contested fall cases in the city. A property owner must take reasonable steps, within a reasonable time after snow or ice accumulates, to make walkways safe; what is reasonable depends on the property. A store open to the public at 9 a.m. after an overnight storm is expected to have salted its entrance; a homeowner is not expected to shovel during the storm. Black ice from refreezing meltwater, a leaking downspout, or a clogged drain can be a condition the owner created, and those cases are stronger than natural accumulation. Under Detroit City Code §8-15-103(c), property owners or occupants generally must remove snow or ice from adjoining sidewalks within 24 hours after it has fallen or formed, or apply appropriate materials to make the sidewalk safe for pedestrians. Photograph the ice, the path, the alternative routes, and the sky the same day; the ice is gone by the afternoon.

## What to do after a fall in Detroit

1. **Get medical care the same day** and tell the provider where and how you fell. Detroit Receiving, Henry Ford, Sinai-Grace, Harper, and Henry Ford St. John are where many of the city’s fall injuries are treated; a hip fracture in an older adult is a surgical emergency. **[CONFIRM: hospital list]**
2. **Photograph the hazard immediately**, from several angles, with something for scale, before it is mopped, salted, or patched. Photograph your shoes and clothing.
3. **Report the fall** to the store manager, the landlord, or the City, and get a copy of or a reference to the incident report. Do not give a recorded statement.
4. **Get witness names and numbers**, including employees, and note the time; it matters for constructive notice.
5. **Measure a sidewalk defect** with a ruler in the photograph. Under two inches is a presumption you will have to rebut.
6. **Keep the shoes** unworn and unwashed.
7. **Call a lawyer within days.** Surveillance footage is commonly overwritten in 7 to 30 days; a preservation letter stops that. If the City, the Housing Commission, a public authority, or the State may be involved, the 120-day or six-month clock is already running.

## The courts

Fall suits over $25,000 are filed in the Third Judicial Circuit Court at the Coleman A. Young Municipal Center; claims up to $25,000, which many fall cases with modest medical bills are, go to the 36th District Court at 421 Madison Street. Claims against the State or MDNR go to the Court of Claims. A fall that causes a [brain injury](/personal-injury/brain-injury), a [spinal cord injury](/personal-injury/spinal-cord-injury), or a death ([wrongful death](/personal-injury/wrongful-death)) is a circuit court case with no cap on noneconomic damages.

## Frequently asked questions

**I fell on ice outside a Detroit store. The ice was obvious. Do I have a case?** Possibly. Since the Michigan Supreme Court’s 2023 decision, an obvious hazard no longer ends the case. The questions are whether the store should have salted or cleared it, whether you had a realistic alternative route, and how much of the fault is yours. If you are found less than 50 percent at fault, you recover, reduced by your share.

**I fell on a broken Detroit sidewalk. Who do I sue?** It depends on the defect and the location. The City is liable under the highway exception only for defects it knew or should have known about, with a presumption that defects under two inches are reasonable repair, and only if you serve written notice within 120 days. Detroit’s ordinance also places sidewalk maintenance on the adjacent owner. We identify the right defendant before the notice runs.

**I fell on the stairs at my apartment building. Can I sue my landlord?** Michigan landlords must keep common areas fit for their intended use and the premises in reasonable repair. A broken step, a missing handrail, or an unlit stairwell the landlord knew about is a claim. If the landlord is the Detroit Housing Commission, the claim proceeds under the public building exception with 120-day notice.

**How long do I have?** Three years to sue a private owner or business; 120 days to give written notice to the City of Detroit, the Housing Commission, or a public authority; six months for the State. The footage that proves the case disappears far sooner.

**The store says it had no idea the floor was wet. Does that end it?** No. The question is whether the hazard existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it. Cart tracks through the spill, dried edges, and a cleaning log with a gap are how constructive notice is proven.

**My elderly parent fell in a Detroit nursing home. Is that a slip and fall case?** It may be a nursing home negligence case or a malpractice case rather than an ordinary premises case, depending on whether the fall resulted from a failure to assess and manage fall risk. The distinction decides the deadline and whether a notice of intent is required, so call early.

**What is my Detroit fall case worth?** It depends on the injury, the strength of the notice evidence, your share of fault, and the owner’s insurance. Michigan places no cap on pain-and-suffering damages in a premises case. No one can give you a number in the first conversation.
