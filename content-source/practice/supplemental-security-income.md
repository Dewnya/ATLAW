## SSI Lawyer Michigan

Supplemental Security Income is the federal program for people who are disabled, blind, or 65 or older and have little income and few resources. It is not earned through work. In December 2025, 247,605 Michigan residents received SSI, including 33,597 children, and the average federal payment was about $712 a month, well below the $994 maximum, because most recipients have some other income that reduces it.

That reduction is the heart of SSI. Social Security disability insurance asks one question: are you disabled? SSI asks that question and then a second set: what do you earn, what do you own, who do you live with, who pays for your food and shelter, and what do your spouse or parents earn? The second set is governed by rules most applicants have never heard of, and it accounts for a large share of SSI denials, suspensions, and overpayments. A Michigan claimant with a plainly disabling condition is denied because a savings account holds $2,300. A recipient’s payment drops by a third because she moved in with her sister. A child’s SSI ends because a parent’s overtime pushed the household over the deeming limit for a month.

This page explains both sets of rules: who qualifies medically and financially, what SSI pays in Michigan in 2026 and what reduces it, SSI for children, how SSI combines with SSDI, Medicaid and Michigan’s small state supplement, what happens when you work, how to apply and appeal, and what a Michigan SSI lawyer does.

## Who qualifies: the medical and non-medical tests

**Categorical eligibility.** You must be **disabled** (the same definition and five-step test as SSDI, 20 CFR 416.920), **blind** (central visual acuity of 20/200 or less in the better eye with correction, or a field of 20 degrees or less), or **age 65 or older**, in which case no disability is required. Children under 18 have their own disability standard, below.

**Citizenship and residence.** You must be a U.S. citizen or a qualified non-citizen in a limited set of categories (certain qualifying noncitizens who meet federal immigration-status, work-history, residency, or applicable exception requirements), and you must live in the United States. Many Michigan immigrants with lawful status are not SSI-eligible under these rules, which is a frequent and painful surprise. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: 20 CFR 416.1600–416.1618]**

**Income.** SSI is reduced by **countable income**, and when countable income exceeds the federal benefit rate, eligibility ends. The rules exclude the first $20 of most income each month, the first $65 of earned income plus one-half of the remainder, SNAP benefits, most need-based assistance, and a list of other items. Everything else counts, including SSDI, pensions, unemployment, child support (two-thirds of it, for a child), and gifts of cash. A worker with $700 of SSDI has $680 of countable income after the $20 exclusion and receives $314 of SSI, for a total of $1,014.

**Resources.** Countable resources must be **$2,000 or less for an individual and $3,000 for a couple** on the first day of the month, limits that have not changed since 1989. Excluded resources include the home you live in, one vehicle, household goods and personal effects, burial plots and up to $1,500 in burial funds, life insurance with a face value of $1,500 or less, and up to $100,000 in a qualifying ABLE account, subject to the program’s eligibility requirements. Other assets may count, including bank accounts, a second vehicle, cash, stocks, the countable cash value of certain life insurance policies, and property you do not live on, unless a specific exclusion applies. A resource over the limit does not reduce the payment; it ends eligibility for every month it exists. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: 20 CFR 416.1201–416.1266]**

## What SSI pays in Michigan, and what reduces it

The 2026 federal benefit rate is **$994 a month for an individual and $1,491 for a couple**, paid on the first of the month. Three things commonly reduce it.

**Countable income**, as above, dollar for dollar after the exclusions.

**Living in someone else’s household.** If you live in another person’s home for a full month and receive both food and shelter from them without paying your share, your payment is reduced by **one-third of the federal benefit rate**, about $331 in 2026, under 20 CFR 416.1131. This is the reduction that catches adults who move in with family after becoming disabled. Paying a pro-rata share of household expenses avoids it, and the arrangement should be documented.

**In-kind support and maintenance.** If someone else pays your rent, mortgage, or utilities, the value counts as income up to a cap of one-third of the benefit rate plus $20, under 20 CFR 416.1130–416.1140. Since September 30, 2024, **food is no longer counted** as in-kind support: groceries from family or meals provided do not reduce SSI, a change that simplified many Michigan recipients’ situations. The same 2024 rules expanded the definition of a public-assistance household to include qualifying households where another member receives SNAP, which can exempt certain support received within that household from the in-kind support rules. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: 2024 ISM final rules]**

**Michigan’s State SSI Payment.** Michigan supplements the federal payment with a small state payment administered by the Department of Health and Human Services and paid **quarterly**, not monthly, to recipients living independently or in the household of another; recipients in licensed adult foster care, homes for the aged, and similar settings receive a larger federally administered supplement, which averaged $114.33 a month for 10,446 Michigan recipients in December 2025. The independent-living State SSI Payment is $14 per month for an individual or $21 per month for an eligible couple in 2026. For someone living in another person’s household under the applicable payment category, it is $9.33 per month for an individual or $13.96 for an eligible couple. MDHHS issues these payments quarterly, in March, June, September, and December. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current amounts in MDHHS Reference Table RFT 248]**

**Medicaid.** Michigan is a “1634 state”: SSI eligibility is Medicaid eligibility, automatically, with no separate application. For most recipients the health coverage is worth more than the cash.

## SSI for children

A child under 18 qualifies for SSI if the child has a physical or mental impairment that causes **marked and severe functional limitations** and has lasted or will last 12 months, under 20 CFR 416.906, and the household meets the income and resource tests after **deeming**. In December 2025, 33,597 Michigan children received SSI.

**The child’s disability standard** is evaluated against the childhood Listings (Part B of Appendix 1) or, if no listing is met, by whether the impairment **functionally equals** the listings: a marked limitation in two of six domains, or an extreme limitation in one, under 20 CFR 416.926a. The six domains are acquiring and using information; attending and completing tasks; interacting and relating with others; moving about and manipulating objects; caring for oneself; and health and physical well-being. School records, IEPs, teacher questionnaires, and therapy records carry as much weight as medical records in a child’s claim.

**Deeming.** A portion of the parents’ income and resources is treated as the child’s when the child lives with them, under 20 CFR 416.1165. The calculation allocates amounts for the parents and for non-disabled siblings, applies the exclusions, and counts the remainder. The practical effect is that two working parents at modest wages often exceed the limit, and that a parent’s overtime, a new job, or a second parent moving into the home can suspend a child’s SSI for the months it applies. Parents must report income changes within ten days of the end of the month in which they occur.

**Back pay for a child** above a threshold is paid into a **dedicated account** that may be used only for the child’s medical, educational, and disability-related needs, under 20 CFR 416.640(e), and Social Security audits it.

**Age 18 redetermination.** When a child on SSI turns 18, Social Security redetermines disability under the **adult** standard, and some young adults are found ineligible under the adult standard. A child approaching 18 should have records that speak to the adult five-step test, and a young adult found no longer disabled at redetermination generally has 60 days to appeal and may elect continued benefits during a qualifying appeal if the election is made within the required time, generally 10 days after receiving the notice. The same young adult may become eligible for childhood disability benefits on a parent’s record when the parent becomes disabled, retires, or dies; our [Social Security benefits page](/disability/social-security-benefits) explains that benefit. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

## SSI and SSDI together

A disabled worker whose SSDI benefit is below the SSI rate receives both: SSDI from the earnings record and SSI to bring the total up to $994 plus $20. In Michigan, 78,964 people receive both. The combination matters because SSI brings Medicaid from the first month while SSDI’s Medicare does not begin for 24 months, and because SSI has no five-month waiting period: a worker approved for both receives SSI for the five months SSDI pays nothing. Both applications should be filed together; the field office will not assume the second. Our [SSDI page](/disability/social-security-disability-insurance) covers the work-record side.

## Applying, and when payments begin

SSI applications are filed online (started at ssa.gov and completed by phone), by phone at 1-800-772-1213, or at a field office by appointment. The field office verifies the non-medical eligibility, income, resources, living arrangement, and citizenship, in an interview that is itself part of the decision, and then sends the medical question to Michigan’s Disability Determination Service. Our [how to apply](/disability/ssd-application-process) page covers the application in detail.

Two SSI-specific timing rules:

- **SSI pays nothing for months before the application.** There is no retroactivity. Eligibility begins the month **after** the month of application (or after the month all requirements are met), under 20 CFR 416.335. A protective filing date, established by a phone call or written statement of intent to file, can secure the earlier month if the application is completed within 60 days.
- **Presumptive disability.** For certain conditions, including ALS, total blindness or deafness, Down syndrome, cerebral palsy, spinal cord injury, and terminal illness with a life expectancy under six months, the field office can begin SSI payments immediately for up to six months before the medical decision, under 20 CFR 416.931–416.934. If the claim is later denied, presumptive payments are not treated as overpayments.

**Michigan’s State Disability Assistance** pays a small monthly cash benefit to disabled adults waiting on an SSI decision; when SSI is approved, Michigan is reimbursed from the first SSI payment under an interim assistance agreement.

**Back pay** of more than three times the federal benefit rate is paid in up to three installments six months apart, under 20 CFR 416.545, except where the recipient has a debt for food, shelter, or medical needs, or is terminally ill.

## Appeals

SSI appeals follow the same four levels and the same 60-day deadline at each as SSDI appeals: reconsideration, hearing before an administrative law judge at one of Michigan’s seven hearing offices, Appeals Council, and federal court. Non-medical denials (income, resources, living arrangement, citizenship) are appealed the same way as medical denials and are often resolved at reconsideration with documents. A recipient whose benefits are **suspended or terminated** after an initial award may elect to continue receiving payments during the appeal if the request is filed within 10 days. Our [appeals page](/disability/ssd-appeals-process) covers each level.

## Working while on SSI

SSI is built so that working always leaves a recipient ahead. After the $20 and $65 exclusions, only **half** of earnings count: a recipient earning $1,000 a month loses about $457 of SSI and keeps $1,000 of wages. Other work incentives:

- **Student earned income exclusion for recipients under age 22 who regularly attend school: up to $2,410 in monthly earnings, subject to a maximum exclusion of $9,730 in calendar year 2026.**. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: 2026 amounts]**
- **Impairment-related work expenses** and **blind work expenses** deducted from earnings.
- **Plans to Achieve Self-Support (PASS)**, setting aside income and resources for a work goal.
- **Section 1619(b) Medicaid: when earnings rise high enough to end SSI cash payments, eligible recipients may retain Medicaid if they continue to meet the program’s requirements. Michigan’s standard earnings threshold is $42,987 annually in 2026, and an individualized higher threshold may apply in qualifying circumstances.**. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: Michigan 1619(b) threshold]**
- **Expedited reinstatement** within five years if work stops because of the disability.

Every month’s earnings must be reported by the tenth of the following month. Unreported wages are the leading cause of SSI overpayments; Social Security’s recovery of SSI overpayments is capped at 10 percent of the monthly payment, and waiver is available where the recipient was not at fault.

## Redeterminations and reviews

SSI recipients face two kinds of review. **Redeterminations** of non-medical eligibility, income, resources, and living arrangements, occur every one to six years, and more often when circumstances change. **Continuing disability reviews** of the medical condition follow the same schedule as SSDI. Both begin with a mailed form or a scheduled interview; ignoring either suspends the payment.

## Frequently asked questions about SSI in Michigan

**How much is SSI in Michigan in 2026?** The federal payment is $994 a month for an individual and $1,491 for a couple, reduced by countable income. Michigan adds a small state payment, paid quarterly, and a larger supplement for recipients in licensed care settings. The average Michigan recipient received about $712 a month in December 2025 because most have some other income.

**What is the resource limit?** $2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple, counted on the first of each month. Your home, one vehicle, household goods, and up to $100,000 in an ABLE account do not count. Being over the limit by any amount ends eligibility for that month.

**I moved in with my daughter. Why did my SSI go down?** If she provides both food and shelter and you do not pay your share, the one-third reduction applies, about $331 a month in 2026. Paying a documented pro-rata share of household expenses removes it.

**Does SSI pay back pay?** Not for months before you applied. Eligibility begins the month after the application month. Back pay accrues from then until approval and, if large, is paid in up to three installments.

**Can my child get SSI?** If the child has a disability causing marked and severe functional limitations and the household’s income and resources, after deeming, are within the limits. School records matter as much as medical records. In December 2025, 33,597 Michigan children received SSI.

**Will my child lose SSI at 18?** Possibly. At 18 Social Security redetermines disability under the adult standard, and many young adults are found not disabled. Prepare the records for the adult test before the birthday, and appeal within 60 days if denied.

**Can I get SSI and SSDI at the same time?** Yes, if your SSDI is below $994. SSI makes up the difference, brings Medicaid immediately, and covers the five-month SSDI waiting period. File both.

**Does SSI come with health insurance?** Yes. In Michigan, SSI eligibility is Medicaid eligibility, automatically.

**Can I work on SSI?** Yes. After the first $85, only half of your earnings count, so work always leaves you ahead. Report wages monthly. If earnings end the cash payment, Medicaid continues under section 1619(b) as long as earnings stay under Michigan’s threshold.

**What does a lawyer cost?** Nothing unless you win, and then the lesser of 25 percent of your back pay or $9,200, approved and paid by Social Security.
