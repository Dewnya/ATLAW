## Canton, MI Slip and Fall Lawyer

For twenty years a fall on someone else’s property in Michigan was close to unwinnable, because a hazard that was “open and obvious” ended the case before it began. That rule is gone. Since the Michigan Supreme Court’s July 2023 decision in *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil*, a Canton fall case turns on who had the duty to keep the place safe, what they knew about the hazard and when, what they did about it, and how much of the fall was your fault. In Canton those questions have local answers: the big-box stores, restaurants, and shopping centers along Ford Road and at Canton Center and Sheldon; apartment and condominium complexes along Ford Road, Haggerty, and Michigan Avenue, where the association or the management company controls the walks; the warehouses and distribution centers on Michigan Avenue, where drivers and contractors’ workers are visitors on someone else’s property; schools and township buildings, each with a 120-day notice rule; and sidewalks, which the township must maintain even though it does not own the roads beside them.

## Where Canton falls happen, and who is responsible

**Stores and shopping centers.** Grocery stores, big-box retailers, pharmacies, and restaurants along Ford Road from I-275 to Sheldon, and the shopping centers at Canton Center, Sheldon, and Michigan Avenue: spills, freshly mopped floors without signs, merchandise in the aisle, ice at entrances, and potholes in shared lots. A customer is an invitee: a property owner or possessor must exercise reasonable care under the circumstances, which may include inspecting for hazards, correcting dangerous conditions, or providing appropriate warnings. The case is decided on **notice**, actual (an employee saw or caused it) or constructive (it existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it). The lease decides whether the store or the shopping-center owner maintained the lot and the sidewalk out front, so we request it with the footage.

**Apartments, condominiums, and senior housing.** Canton has apartment complexes and condominium communities along Ford Road, Haggerty, and Michigan Avenue, and senior housing throughout the township. In a condominium, the association, not the unit owner, usually controls the common walks, stairs, and lots, and its snow-removal contract and maintenance records are the evidence. Landlords must keep the premises and common areas fit for their intended use and in reasonable repair (MCL 554.139): broken stairs, loose handrails, dark stairwells and parking lots, and failed lighting are the usual claims. A lot with an inch or two of snow is still “fit” for parking under *Allison v AEW Capital* (2008), so tenant snow cases depend on whether the accumulation actually prevented use; the general negligence claim after *Kandil-Elsayed* is a separate route. A lease clause waiving the landlord’s liability for its own negligence is void. Older tenants who fall often suffer hip and wrist fractures that change their lives. See our [tenant injury page](/personal-injury/tenant-injuries).

**Warehouses and distribution centers.** A truck driver, delivery driver, staffing-agency worker, or contractor who falls at a Michigan Avenue warehouse or distribution center is not that facility’s employee. Their own employer’s workers’ compensation pays their benefits, and the facility owner or the general contractor that controlled the dock, the yard, or the icy lot can be sued as a third party. The comp insurer is repaid from that recovery (MCL 418.827). The facility’s own employee generally cannot sue the employer (MCL 418.131), but may have a claim against a contractor whose work created the hazard; a worker placed by a staffing agency may be treated as the facility’s employee too. See our [Canton workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation/canton).

**Sidewalks: the township, even though it does not own the roads.** Canton Township has no jurisdiction over its roads, which belong to Wayne County and MDOT. Sidewalks are different. Under the immunity act a township is a “municipal corporation,” and the municipal corporation in which a sidewalk runs beside a county or state road must keep that sidewalk in reasonable repair (MCL 691.1401(d), 691.1402a(1)). The duties of the State and the county under the highway exception stop at the roadway (MCL 691.1402(1)). So a fall on a broken sidewalk along Ford Road or Canton Center is generally a claim against Canton Township, not Wayne County or MDOT. The township must have known, or should have known, of the defect at least 30 days before the fall; a defect under two inches is presumed to be reasonable repair unless the evidence shows otherwise, and the judge decides whether the presumption is rebutted (MCL 691.1402a(2)–(4)). Measure and photograph the defect with a ruler before it is patched, and serve written notice stating the exact location, the defect, the injury, and the witnesses within 120 days (MCL 691.1404). A sidewalk inside a private development or shopping center belongs to its owner, and the claim is an ordinary premises claim.

**Schools, township buildings, and parks.** Canton is served mainly by Plymouth-Canton Community Schools, with Wayne-Westland and Van Buren serving parts of the township. Each is a governmental agency: a fall caused by a dangerous or defective condition of one of their buildings proceeds under the public building exception, which requires the agency to have known of the defect and failed to fix it, with written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1406). The same rule applies to the township administration building, the public library, the Summit on the Park community center, and Canton Public Safety buildings. A fall on a township park path or field, outside a building, has no comparable exception and is difficult to bring.

**Hospitals and nursing homes.** A visitor’s fall at a hospital or medical office is a premises claim. A patient’s fall, or a resident’s fall in one of Canton’s two Medicare-certified nursing homes, may be premises, negligence, or medical malpractice, and which one decides the deadline. See our [Canton nursing home abuse page](/personal-injury/nursing-home-abuse/canton) and [medical malpractice page](/personal-injury/medical-malpractice).

## A parking-lot fall can be a no-fault claim

If you fell while getting into or out of your own car, or while loading it, Michigan’s no-fault law may cover your medical bills and lost wages even though no crash occurred. An injury from a parked vehicle is covered when the person was “occupying, entering into, or alighting from” it, or was injured by property being lifted onto or lowered from it during loading or unloading (MCL 500.3106(1)))). That claim runs on the no-fault notice rules: written notice to the auto insurer within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). It sits alongside any premises claim against the lot’s owner. A worker hurt on the job while loading, unloading, or getting into or out of a parked vehicle is generally limited to workers’ compensation for that injury, with narrow exceptions (MCL 500.3106(2)).

## The law after *Kandil-Elsayed*

Under the old rule (*Lugo v Ameritech*, 2001), an owner owed no duty to protect visitors from a danger an average person would have noticed, and insurers denied fall claims on that basis. In *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil* (Michigan Supreme Court, July 28, 2023), the Court held that whether a hazard was open and obvious no longer decides whether a duty existed. It is now a factor in whether the owner breached the duty of reasonable care, and in how much of the fault belongs to the person who fell; both are questions for a jury. That does not make every fall a case. A visible hazard still matters: if the jury assigns you some fault, your damages are reduced by that share, and above half you recover no pain-and-suffering damages, only reduced economic losses (MCL 600.2959).

Duty also depends on why you were there. A customer or business visitor is an invitee, owed inspection and repair; a social guest is a licensee, owed a warning of hidden dangers the owner knows about; a trespasser is owed protection from willful and wanton misconduct. Treating invitees and licensees alike, as some summaries do, overstates or understates the claim.

## Snow and ice

An owner must take reasonable steps, within a reasonable time after snow or ice accumulates, to make walkways, entrances, and lots safe. What is reasonable depends on the property: a grocery store opening at 7 a.m. after an overnight storm is expected to have treated its entrance. The law does not require an owner to keep every surface bare at every moment of a storm. Black ice from refreezing meltwater, a leaking gutter, or snow piled where it melts across the walk can be a condition the owner created, and those cases are stronger than natural accumulation.

Some Michigan communities require adjacent owners to clear snow and ice from public sidewalks by ordinance. Photograph the ice, the path, the alternatives you had, and the weather the same day.

## What to do after a fall in Canton

1. **Get medical care the same day** and tell the provider where and how you fell.
2. **Photograph the hazard immediately**, from several angles, with something for scale, before it is mopped, salted, or patched. Photograph your shoes.
3. **Report the fall** to the manager, the landlord, facility security, or the township, and get the incident report number. Do not give a recorded statement to any insurer.
4. **Get witness names**, including employees, and note the time; it matters for constructive notice.
5. **Measure a sidewalk defect** with a ruler in the photograph.
6. **Keep the shoes** unworn and unwashed.
7. **Call a lawyer within days.** Footage may be overwritten in days; if the township or a school district may be involved, a short notice period is already running.

## Frequently asked questions

**I slipped on ice outside a store on Ford Road. The ice was obvious. Do I have a case?** Possibly. Since 2023, an obvious hazard no longer ends the case. The questions are whether the store or the shopping-center owner should have salted or cleared it, whether you had a realistic alternative route, and how much of the fault is yours.

**I deliver for a trucking company and fell on the ice at a Canton distribution center. Can I sue the facility?** Possibly. Your employer’s workers’ compensation pays your benefits, and the facility owner or the contractor that controlled the yard or dock where you fell can be sued as a third party. The comp insurer is repaid from that recovery.

**I fell on a broken sidewalk along Ford Road. Is that the county’s or the State’s responsibility?** Generally neither. Canton Township does not own the roads, but it must keep the public sidewalks beside county and state roads in reasonable repair, so the claim is usually against the township. It must have known or should have known of the defect at least 30 days before, defects under two inches are presumed reasonable repair, and written notice must reach the township within 120 days.

**I fell getting out of my car in a parking lot. Is that a car accident claim?** It may be. No-fault can cover injuries from entering or leaving a parked vehicle, separate from any claim against the lot’s owner.

**How long do I have?** Three years to sue a private owner; 120 days for written notice to the township or a school district; and one year to notify a no-fault insurer. The footage disappears far sooner.
