## Michigan Medical Malpractice Lawyer

Michigan has built more obstacles into medical malpractice claims than into any other kind of injury case: a two-year deadline instead of three, a mandatory notice to the doctor six months before you can sue, a sworn expert affidavit that has to be filed with the complaint, strict rules about which experts can testify, and a cap on pain-and-suffering damages. ## What medical malpractice is, and what it is not

Malpractice is not a bad outcome. Medicine has bad outcomes without anyone being at fault. Malpractice is a bad outcome caused by a provider who failed to do what a reasonably competent provider in the same specialty would have done in the same circumstances. Michigan law (MCL 600.2912a) requires you to prove four things:

1. **The standard of care**: what a reasonably prudent doctor, nurse, or hospital in that specialty would have done. For a specialist, the standard is national; for a general practitioner, it is the standard in the same or a similar community.
2. **A breach**: the provider did something different, or failed to do what the standard required.
3. **Causation**: the breach, more likely than not, caused the injury. Michigan requires proof that the patient would have had a better outcome, not merely a chance of one (MCL 600.2912a(2)). This is where many cases are lost.
4. **Damages**: a real injury with real consequences.

The cases ATLAW sees most in metro Detroit:

- **Misdiagnosis and delayed diagnosis**: cancer, stroke, heart attack, sepsis, pulmonary embolism, appendicitis. The question is whether earlier diagnosis would have changed the outcome.
- **Surgical errors**: wrong site, retained instruments, nerve or organ damage, anesthesia errors.
- **Birth injuries**: cerebral palsy, Erb’s palsy, hypoxic brain injury from delayed C-section or mismanaged labor. These have their own page.
- **Emergency room errors**: discharged patients who were having a stroke or a heart attack.
- **Medication errors**: wrong drug, wrong dose, missed interaction, pharmacy errors.
- **Hospital negligence**: infections, falls, pressure sores, failure to monitor, understaffing.
- **Failure to obtain informed consent**: a procedure performed without disclosing a material risk that a reasonable patient would have wanted to know, where the risk occurred.
- **Nursing home negligence**, which can be a malpractice claim, an ordinary negligence claim, or both, depending on who did what.

## The Michigan malpractice rules that decide whether you can sue

**Two years, not three.** A malpractice lawsuit must be filed within two years of the act or omission (MCL 600.5805(8)). If you could not reasonably have discovered the injury within those two years, you have six months from the date you discovered it or should have (MCL 600.5838a(2)), but never more than six years after the act, except for cases involving a retained foreign object, fraud, or injury to the reproductive system. For a minor under eight, the deadline runs to the tenth birthday (MCL 600.5851(7)). For a death, the estate’s representative has additional time under MCL 600.5852, but the six-year outer limit still applies.

**The Notice of Intent.** Before you can file, you must serve every provider you intend to sue with a written Notice of Intent at least 182 days before the complaint (MCL 600.2912b). The notice must state the facts, the standard of care you claim was breached, how it was breached, what should have been done instead, and how the breach caused the injury. Serving the notice pauses the two-year clock for the 182 days (MCL 600.5856(c)), but only if the notice is served before the deadline runs. The provider has 154 days to respond. In practice, this means a malpractice lawyer needs to be retained well inside the two years, because the records have to be obtained and reviewed by an expert before the notice can be written.

**The Affidavit of Merit.** The complaint must be filed with a sworn affidavit from a qualified medical expert stating the standard of care, the breach, what should have been done, and causation (MCL 600.2912d). A complaint filed without it is subject to dismissal.

**Who can be the expert.** Michigan’s expert rule (MCL 600.2169) is among the strictest in the country. The expert must practice or teach in the same specialty as the defendant, and if the defendant is board-certified, the expert must be too. During the year before the malpractice, the expert must have spent most of their professional time practicing or teaching that specialty. A case against a board-certified emergency physician needs a board-certified emergency physician, not a general surgeon or an internist. Finding and paying that expert is part of what a malpractice lawyer does, and it is why a malpractice case costs tens of thousands of dollars to bring.

**The damages cap.** Michigan caps non-economic damages (pain, suffering, loss of enjoyment of life) in malpractice cases (MCL 600.1483). The cap is adjusted each January by the Michigan Treasury. For 2026 it is $596,400 for most cases and $1,065,000 for the most severe injuries: permanent loss of a limb’s function from brain or spinal cord injury, permanent cognitive impairment that prevents independent living, or permanent loss of reproductive ability. **[VERIFY: 2026 figures against the Treasury memo before publishing; 2025 was $586,300 / $1,047,000, 2024 was $569,000 / $1,016,000]** There is no cap on economic damages: medical bills, lost earnings, and the cost of future care, which in a catastrophic case can run to many millions.

**Comparative fault.** If the patient’s own conduct (ignoring instructions, missing follow-ups) contributed, the recovery is reduced by that share, and non-economic damages are barred above 50% (MCL 600.2959).

## What a malpractice case takes, step by step

1. **The records.** Every page of the chart, imaging, nursing notes, and the hospital’s own incident reports. Patients have a right to their records under Michigan’s Medical Records Access Act (MCL 333.26261) and HIPAA; providers must produce them, usually within 30 days.
2. **Expert review.** A qualified physician reviews the records and tells us, in writing, whether the standard of care was breached and whether the breach caused the harm. Many cases end here, honestly: the outcome was bad but the care was not negligent. We tell clients that directly.
3. **The Notice of Intent**, served on every provider, starting the 182-day period.
4. **The Affidavit of Merit and the complaint**, filed in the circuit court of the county where the care was given: Wayne County Circuit Court in Detroit, Oakland County in Pontiac, Macomb County in Mount Clemens, Washtenaw County in Ann Arbor.
5. **Discovery**: depositions of the providers, the experts on both sides, and the defense’s own experts.
6. **Mediation or case evaluation**, which Michigan courts require in most civil cases.
7. **Settlement or trial.** Most malpractice cases that survive expert review settle; those that go to trial are decided by a jury of the county where the case was filed.

Malpractice cases take longer than other injury cases, typically two to four years from retention to resolution, because of the pre-suit requirements and the expert work.

## Do you have a malpractice case? An honest checklist

You may have a case if:

- The injury is serious and lasting, or someone died.
- A provider missed, delayed, or got wrong something that another provider later identified.
- The harm would likely have been avoided with correct care.
- It happened within the last two years (or you discovered it within the last six months).

You probably do not if:

- The outcome was bad but the care was within the range of reasonable judgment.
- The injury was minor or has resolved.
- More than two years have passed and the injury was discoverable earlier.
- The only harm is that you are unhappy with how you were treated, with no physical injury.

## What a Michigan malpractice case is worth

The cap limits pain and suffering; it does not limit economic damages. The value is set by:

- **Future medical and care costs**, calculated by a life-care planner for serious injuries.
- **Lost earnings and earning capacity.**
- **Non-economic damages**, up to the cap tier that applies.
- **In a death case**, the family’s economic losses and loss of society and companionship, within the cap.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan medical malpractice

**How long do I have to sue for medical malpractice in Michigan?** Two years from the malpractice (MCL 600.5805(8)), or six months from when you discovered it if it could not have been discovered sooner, with a six-year outer limit. Because the Notice of Intent must be served 182 days before filing and requires expert review first, the practical deadline for contacting a lawyer is much earlier than two years.

**What is a Notice of Intent?** A written notice, required by MCL 600.2912b, that you must serve on each provider at least 182 days before filing suit. It sets out the standard of care, the breach, and causation. Serving it on time pauses the statute of limitations during the waiting period.

**Does Michigan cap medical malpractice damages?** Yes, for non-economic damages only: $596,400 for most cases and $1,065,000 for the most severe permanent injuries in 2026, adjusted each January (MCL 600.1483). Economic damages, including future care, are not capped.

**Do I need an expert to sue?** Yes. Michigan requires a sworn Affidavit of Merit from a qualified expert when the complaint is filed (MCL 600.2912d), and the expert must practice in the same specialty as the defendant (MCL 600.2169). The firm finds and pays the expert.

**Can I sue a hospital, or only the doctor?** Both, depending on the facts. A hospital is liable for its employees (nurses, technicians, employed physicians) and for its own failures (staffing, systems, credentialing). Many physicians in Michigan hospitals are independent contractors, which affects who is named; the records show which.

**My family member died. Can we sue?** A wrongful death claim based on malpractice is brought by the estate’s personal representative, under the same malpractice rules and cap, with additional time under MCL 600.5852. The family recovers economic losses and loss of companionship.

**How much does a malpractice lawyer cost?** Nothing up front. ATLAW advances the costs of records and expert review and works on a contingency fee within Michigan’s one-third cap, set in writing before we start. You owe no fee unless we recover money for you.

**How do I get my medical records?** Request them in writing from each provider under Michigan’s Medical Records Access Act. Providers must respond within 30 days and may charge a regulated fee. We request them for you once retained.

**What if the hospital was a VA or the University of Michigan?** Different rules. VA claims are federal (Federal Tort Claims Act), with an administrative claim within two years and no jury. University of Michigan claims go through the Michigan Court of Claims with a notice requirement. Tell us where the care was given in the first call.

**How long will the case take?** Two to four years is typical. The Notice of Intent period alone is six months, and expert discovery is extensive. Cases that settle after the expert phase resolve faster; cases that go to trial take longer.
