## Personal Injury FAQ: Michigan Injury Claims

These are the questions people ask when they call a personal injury firm for the first time, answered for Michigan. Michigan’s rules are not the national rules: it is a no-fault state for car crashes, it caps some damages and not others, it bars pain-and-suffering recovery for anyone more than half at fault, and it runs several different deadlines that depend on who injured you and how. Where an answer turns on a statute, the statute is cited so you or your lawyer can check it.

## Starting a claim

**Do I have a case?** You may have a claim if you were injured, someone else’s carelessness or wrongdoing caused the injury, and there is a source of payment, usually an insurance policy. In Michigan all three questions have twists. A car crash injury must meet the “threshold” in MCL 500.3135 before you can sue the at-fault driver for pain and suffering. A fall on someone’s property is evaluated under Michigan’s premises liability rules. Since *Kandil-Elsayed v. F & E Oil, Inc.* (2023), an “open and obvious” hazard does not automatically eliminate the property owner’s duty; instead, its obviousness is considered when evaluating whether the owner breached that duty and whether the injured person shares fault. A lawyer can tell you in one conversation whether the facts you describe fit a claim.

**What counts as personal injury?** A physical or psychological injury to a person, as distinct from damage to property, caused by someone else’s negligence or intentional act. In Michigan the common categories are motor vehicle crashes (288,880 of them in 2024, with 71,316 people injured and 1,099 killed, according to the Michigan State Police), falls and other premises injuries, dog bites, medical negligence, defective products, nursing-home neglect, and workplace injuries caused by someone other than the employer. Wrongful death is the claim the family brings when the injury was fatal.

**What should I do first?** Get medical care the same day, report the incident (to police for a crash, to the property owner or manager for a fall, to animal control for a bite), photograph everything, get witness names, and do not give a recorded statement to the other side’s insurer. Our [checklist for what to do after a car accident](/resources/what-to-do-after-an-accident) walks through the first day, week, and month.

**How soon should I call a lawyer?** Before you talk to the other side’s insurer, sign anything, or give a statement, and before any deadline in the next section. Early calls cost nothing and preserve evidence (video footage is often overwritten within days); late calls sometimes find that a deadline has passed or a release has been signed.

**Do I need a lawyer for a minor injury?** Not always. A crash with no injury and a cooperative insurer, or a mini tort claim for vehicle damage, can be handled yourself. Call when anyone was hurt beyond a bruise, when an insurer denies or delays, when fault is disputed, when the other driver fled or was uninsured, or when a release or recorded statement is requested.

## Deadlines

**How long do I have to file a personal injury lawsuit in Michigan?** Three years from the date of injury for most negligence claims, including car crashes, falls, and dog bites, under MCL 600.5805(2). Several claims have shorter or different deadlines, listed in the next answers. Missing the deadline ends the claim regardless of how strong it is.

**What are the other deadlines I should know?**

| Claim | Deadline | Statute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Negligence (crash, fall, dog bite, product) | 3 years from injury | MCL 600.5805(2) |
| Written notice of injury to your no-fault insurer | 1 year from the crash | MCL 500.3145(1) |
| Suit for unpaid no-fault benefits | Recovers only expenses within 1 year before suit (tolled while a claim is pending) | MCL 500.3145(2)–(3) |
| No-fault property protection (damage to buildings or parked vehicles) | 1 year from the crash | MCL 500.3145(5) |
| Medical malpractice | 2 years from the negligent act, or 6 months from discovery, with a 6-year outside limit; 182-day notice of intent required before suit | MCL 600.5805(8), 600.5838a(2), 600.2912b |
| Wrongful death | Generally 3 years, with a saving provision that can extend the time for the estate’s representative | MCL 600.5805(2), 600.5852 |
| Dram shop (bar or store that served a drunk driver) | Written notice within 120 days of retaining a lawyer; suit within 2 years | MCL 436.1801 |
| Highway or sidewalk defect against a government agency | Written notice within 120 days of the injury | MCL 691.1404 |
| Claims against the State of Michigan | Notice or claim filed in the Court of Claims within 6 months (1 year for some claims) | MCL 600.6431 |
| Injured person is a minor | A minor generally has until one year after turning 18 to bring an ordinary negligence claim, subject to applicable exceptions. Medical malpractice has separate deadlines for young children under 8. | MCL 600.5851(7)–(8) |

**Does the deadline change if the injured person is a child?** Yes. Under MCL 600.5851(1) a person who was under 18 when injured has until one year after turning 18 to sue, so a child’s negligence claim can be brought until age 19. Medical malpractice claims for children have a separate rule in 600.5851(7). Parents can and usually should bring the claim earlier; the evidence does not improve with age.

**What if I was injured by a city, county, or state vehicle or on government property?** The Governmental Tort Liability Act, MCL 691.1401 and following, limits when a government agency can be sued and imposes notice requirements that are much shorter than three years: 120 days for a highway or sidewalk defect under 691.1404, and six months for most claims against the state in the Court of Claims under 600.6431. A crash with a city bus, a fall on a public sidewalk, or a collision with a county snowplow should go to a lawyer within days, not months.

## Car accidents and no-fault

**Michigan is a no-fault state. What does that mean for my claim?** Two claims, not one. Your **first-party** or no-fault claim is against your own auto insurer (or a household member’s, or the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan if no policy applies) for medical expenses, 85 percent of lost wages for up to three years, replacement services at $20 a day, and attendant care, regardless of who caused the crash, under MCL 500.3107. Your **third-party** claim is against the at-fault driver for pain and suffering and economic losses that no-fault does not cover, and it requires a threshold injury under MCL 500.3135. Our [Michigan no-fault insurance guide](/resources/michigan-no-fault-guide) covers both in detail.

**What is the “threshold injury”?** Under MCL 500.3135(1), you may sue the at-fault driver for noneconomic damages only if you suffered death, serious impairment of body function, or permanent serious disfigurement. “Serious impairment of body function” is defined in 3135(5) as an objectively manifested impairment of an important body function that affects your general ability to lead your normal life, as interpreted in McCormick v Carrier (Michigan Supreme Court, 2010). It does not require permanence, and injuries that seemed minor at the scene often meet it once the medical record develops.

**Who pays my medical bills after a crash?** Your own no-fault insurer, up to the PIP medical level you selected when you bought the policy ($50,000 for Medicaid enrollees, $250,000, $500,000, or unlimited), after you file a written Application for No-Fault Benefits within one year of the crash. If no policy in your household applies, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan assigns an insurer, with medical benefits capped at $250,000 under MCL 500.3172. Your health insurer may be primary if you selected that coordination when buying the policy.

**What if the other driver had no insurance or fled?** Your no-fault benefits are unaffected; they come from your own policy. Your claim for pain and suffering against the driver is paid by your own **uninsured motorist coverage**, if you bought it, under the policy’s conditions, which typically include prompt notice to police and the insurer for a hit-and-run. Without UM coverage, the claim is worth only what the uninsured driver can personally pay. See our [uninsured and underinsured motorist page](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).

**Can I recover for damage to my car?** From your own collision coverage, less the deductible, regardless of fault; and from the at-fault driver under the **mini tort**, MCL 500.3135(3)(e), up to $3,000 for the deductible or unpaid repair cost, if the other driver was at least 50 percent at fault. Mini tort claims are usually paid by letter with the police report and two estimates.

**Should I talk to the other driver’s insurance company?** You are not required to, and you should not give a recorded statement, sign a medical authorization, or sign a release without advice. The adjuster’s job is to pay as little as the facts allow, and inconsistencies between your early statement and your later medical records are the most common reason a claim is discounted. Your own insurer is different: your policy requires cooperation with it, including a statement and, if the policy provides, an examination under oath.

**Do I have to attend the insurance company’s medical exam?** Your no-fault insurer may require you to submit to an examination by a physician of its choosing under MCL 500.3151, and refusing can suspend benefits. You may bring someone with you, you should not be asked about fault, and the examiner’s report can be challenged. Tell your lawyer the date as soon as it is scheduled.

## Fault and damages

**What if the accident was partly my fault?** Michigan uses modified comparative fault. Under MCL 600.2959 your damages are reduced by your percentage of fault, and under MCL 500.3135(2)(b) and 600.2959 a plaintiff more than 50 percent at fault recovers no pain-and-suffering damages at all (economic damages are still reduced proportionally). Fault is decided by the jury if the case is tried, which is why the evidence from the scene matters so much.

**What damages can I recover?** Economic damages: medical expenses not paid by no-fault or other insurance, lost wages and lost earning capacity, replacement services, and out-of-pocket costs. Noneconomic damages: pain and suffering, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of life, and disfigurement. In a wrongful death case, under MCL 600.2922(6), the family can recover medical and funeral expenses, the decedent’s conscious pain and suffering, and the loss of the decedent’s society and companionship. Michigan does not allow punitive damages; “exemplary” damages for intentional misconduct are rare.

**Are there caps on damages in Michigan?** Not in car, truck, premises, dog-bite, or product cases. Medical malpractice noneconomic damages are capped by MCL 600.1483 at $596,400 for 2026, or $1,065,000 for the most severe categories of injury, adjusted each year. Government defendants have the protections of the Governmental Tort Liability Act. No-fault medical benefits are limited by the PIP level the insured chose.

**What is my case worth?** No honest lawyer can answer that in the first call, and you should be cautious of one who does. The value depends on the injury and its documentation, the effect on your work and daily life, the strength of the fault evidence, your own share of fault, the insurance available to pay, and the county where a jury would hear it. In crash cases the at-fault driver’s liability limit, which can be as low as $50,000 per person under MCL 500.3009(1), is frequently the ceiling unless you carry underinsured motorist coverage.

**Can I recover if I was not wearing a seat belt?** Yes, but the recovery can be reduced. Under MCL 257.710e(8), failure to wear a seat belt may be considered evidence of negligence, and the reduction of damages for that reason is limited to 5 percent.

**What if the person who hurt me was drunk or on drugs?** It strengthens the fault case, and it can add defendants. Under Michigan’s dram shop act, MCL 436.1801, a bar or store that sold alcohol to a visibly intoxicated person or a minor can be liable for injuries the drunk driver causes, subject to a 120-day notice requirement. See our [drunk driving accident page](/personal-injury/drunk-driving-accidents). Separately, under MCL 600.2955a, a plaintiff who was more than 50 percent at fault and impaired by alcohol or drugs is barred from recovering.

## Fees and costs

**How much does a personal injury lawyer cost in Michigan?** Personal injury lawyers in Michigan work on contingency: you pay no fee unless the lawyer recovers money for you, and the fee is a percentage of the recovery. Michigan Court Rule 8.121 caps the contingency fee in personal injury and wrongful death cases at one-third of the net recovery after costs. ATLAW charges no fee unless we recover money for you. You pay nothing up front and nothing if there is no recovery. Case costs are advanced by the firm and deducted from the recovery first; the one-third fee is calculated on the remaining amount.

**What are case costs, and who pays them?** Filing fees, record charges, deposition transcripts, expert witness fees, and similar expenses. In most Michigan contingency agreements the firm advances them and is repaid from the recovery. A case that goes to trial with medical experts can carry significant costs; your fee agreement should say in writing who bears them if the case is lost. At ATLAW, if there is no recovery, you owe no fee and no costs.

**Is the first consultation free?** Yes. There is no charge to have ATLAW review your case, and no obligation. We welcome clients to our Dearborn office for a consultation. If you are unable to visit, home or hospital visits can be arranged as needed.

**Will I owe money if I lose?** No fee and no costs. If there is no recovery, you owe ATLAW nothing.

**How does the firm get paid for no-fault benefits it recovers?** Where a no-fault insurer unreasonably refuses or delays payment of overdue benefits, MCL 500.3148(1) permits a court to require the insurer to pay reasonable attorney fees, subject to the statute’s requirements. Overdue benefits may also accrue 12 percent simple interest under MCL 500.3142. Whether the insurer pays the attorney fees, and how any remaining fees or costs are handled, depends on the circumstances and the representation agreement.

## The process

**How long does a personal injury case take in Michigan?** From a few months to several years. A clear-liability crash with a policy-limits offer can settle in three to six months once treatment stabilizes. A litigated case in a Michigan circuit court typically takes 18 to 30 months from filing to trial, with most settling along the way at case evaluation, facilitation, or on the eve of trial. Medical malpractice cases, with their 182-day notice period and expert requirements, run longer. The single biggest factor the client controls is completing treatment, because a case cannot be valued until the medical picture is clear.

**Will I have to go to court?** Most cases settle without trial, but many are filed in court before they settle, because filing is what forces the insurer to produce its file and take depositions seriously. You may be deposed, attend an independent medical examination, and attend case evaluation or facilitation. Fewer than one in twenty personal injury cases is decided by a jury.

**What is case evaluation?** A Michigan procedure under MCR 2.403 in which a panel of three attorneys reviews the case and recommends a settlement figure, which each side accepts or rejects. Since the 2022 rule amendments, rejecting the evaluation no longer carries the automatic cost sanctions it once did, and courts increasingly send cases to facilitation (private mediation) instead.

**What is a deposition?** Sworn testimony given before trial, outside the courtroom, with a court reporter and the lawyers for both sides present. You will be asked about your background, the incident, your injuries, your treatment, and how the injury has affected your life. Your lawyer prepares you beforehand and attends. Honesty and consistency with your medical records matter more than anything else.

**Who decides my case if it goes to trial?** A jury of six in Michigan circuit court civil cases, with a verdict of five of the six sufficient (MCL 600.1352; MCR 2.514). The judge decides questions of law, including whether the threshold has been met when the facts are not in dispute.

**What happens to my medical bills while the case is pending?** In a car case, your no-fault insurer pays them as they come due. In other cases, your health insurance pays, and the health insurer (or Medicare or Medicaid) usually has a right to be repaid from the recovery. Part of the lawyer’s work at the end of a case is negotiating those liens down, which directly increases what the client receives.

## After the case

**Is my settlement taxable?** Compensation for physical injuries and the medical expenses, lost wages, and pain and suffering that flow from them is excluded from federal income tax under 26 USC 104(a)(2). Interest on a judgment and any portion for emotional distress unrelated to a physical injury are taxable. Michigan follows the federal treatment. Ask a tax professional about your specific settlement; we are not tax advisors.

**How is a settlement paid out?** The insurer sends the check to the firm’s trust account; the firm pays the costs, resolves the liens, deducts the fee under the agreement, and sends the client the balance with a written settlement statement showing every line. Ask for that statement before you sign the release. A structured settlement, paid over time, can be arranged for large recoveries and for minors, whose settlements require court approval under MCR 2.420.

**Can I reopen a case after I settle?** Almost never. A release is a contract that ends the claim for all injuries from the incident, known and unknown. That is why no release should be signed until the injuries are fully diagnosed and the no-fault and third-party pieces have both been evaluated.

**Can I fire my lawyer and hire a new one?** Yes, at any time. The first lawyer may have a right to reasonable compensation for work already performed or a lien against the eventual recovery, depending on the circumstances. Before switching attorneys, ask how any outstanding fees and case expenses will be addressed so you understand whether the change could affect your final recovery.

## Specific situations

**I was hurt at work. Can I sue my employer?** Generally no. Workers’ compensation under MCL 418.131 is the exclusive remedy against the employer, paying medical care and a portion of lost wages without regard to fault. You can sue a third party whose negligence caused the injury, such as the driver who hit you while you were working, a property owner, or the maker of a defective machine, and you can bring both claims at once. See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

**My family member died. Who can bring the claim?** The personal representative of the estate brings a wrongful death action under MCL 600.2922, and the proceeds are distributed by the probate court to the spouse, children, parents, grandparents, siblings, and others named in the statute according to their losses. See our [wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death).

**I fell in a store or on a sidewalk. Do I have a claim?** Possibly. Store owners owe customers a duty to keep the premises reasonably safe and to warn of hazards they knew or should have known about; since *Kandil-Elsayed* (2023), the obviousness of a hazard is considered in determining breach of duty and comparative fault, rather than automatically eliminating the property owner’s duty. Public sidewalks are governed by the Governmental Tort Liability Act, with its 120-day notice and the two-inch rule for sidewalk defects under MCL 691.1402a. Report the fall, photograph the hazard, and ask whether cameras recorded it the same day. See our [premises liability](/personal-injury/premises-liability) and [sidewalk injury](/personal-injury/sidewalk-injuries) pages.

**I was bitten by a dog. Does it matter if the dog had never bitten before?** No. Michigan’s dog bite statute, MCL 287.351, makes the owner liable for a bite on public property or while the victim was lawfully on private property, without proof of prior viciousness or negligence, unless the victim provoked the dog. See our [dog bite page](/personal-injury/dog-bites).

**I think my doctor made a mistake. How is that different?** Medical malpractice has its own deadlines (two years, with a six-month discovery rule and six-year outside limit), a mandatory 182-day notice of intent before suit (MCL 600.2912b), an affidavit of merit from a qualified expert (600.2912d), and the damage caps in 600.1483. Cases are expert-driven and expensive, and firms screen them carefully. See our [medical malpractice page](/personal-injury/medical-malpractice).

**I was a passenger. Who do I claim against?** Your no-fault benefits come from your own policy or a household member’s, or from the Assigned Claims Plan if none applies. Your pain-and-suffering claim is against whichever driver was at fault, including the driver of the car you were in. See our [passenger injury page](/personal-injury/passenger-injury).

**I was in an Uber or Lyft. Is that different?** The rideshare company’s $1 million liability policy applies while a trip is active, in addition to no-fault. Report the crash through the app and to police. See our [rideshare accident page](/personal-injury/rideshare-accidents).

**I am undocumented. Can I still make a claim?** Yes. Being undocumented does not automatically prevent you from pursuing a personal injury claim in Michigan. Eligibility for no-fault insurance benefits and recovery of lost wages depends on the applicable law, available coverage, and your individual circumstances. We can review these issues confidentially and explain the legal options available to you.
