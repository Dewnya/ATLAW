## Royal Oak, MI Motorcycle Accident Lawyer

A motorcycle crash in Royal Oak is governed by a rule many riders learn only after they are hurt: under Michigan no-fault, a motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle.” That definition decides:

- who pays your medical bills, and how much they pay
- whether you have a no-fault claim at all
- how the insurer will argue the helmet question

Royal Oak adds its own risks:

- **Downtown**, with tight streets, parked cars, pedestrians, and railroad crossings.
- **Three road agencies**, each with its own notice clock when the road itself caused the crash.

## Where Royal Oak motorcycle crashes happen

**The Woodward turnarounds.** Most left turns on Woodward happen at a Michigan-left turnaround: drivers pass the cross street, U-turn in the median, and come back. The rider crashes are:

- **Pulling out of the turnaround:** a driver leaving the turnaround into the near lanes without yielding to a motorcycle approaching at speed.
- **Moving left to reach it:** a driver changing lanes to the left to get to a turnaround, across a rider in the left lane (MCL 257.642(1)(a)).
- **Stopping short:** a driver who stops abruptly in a through lane after missing the turnaround.



**The left turn at the mile roads.** The recurring account in rider cases is a driver turning left across oncoming traffic who misjudges the speed of a motorcycle or never sees it. Under Michigan law, a driver must yield to oncoming traffic before turning left (MCL 257.650). Royal Oak produces that crash in two places every day:

- the signals on Eleven, Twelve, Thirteen, and Fourteen Mile, and on Crooks, Coolidge, Campbell, Main, and Rochester Road
- the driveways and side streets between them

Drivers pulling out from a side street across the lanes produce the mirror image of that crash.

**The Dream Cruise.** On cruise weekend, Woodward and the side streets fill with motorcycles, classic cars, and spectators. The crashes are:

- rear-end crashes in stop-and-go traffic
- lane changes into a rider
- drivers pulling out of parking lots and side streets into the cruise
- riders struck by drivers distracted by the crowd

Alcohol is part of many of them: 31 percent of Michigan’s 2024 rider deaths involved alcohol.

**Downtown and the railroad crossings.** Downtown’s streets bring several hazards for a rider:

- parked cars and opening doors
- drivers backing out of angled spaces
- pedestrians stepping out between cars
- gravel and sand at the corners

Railroad tracks cross several city streets at grade, and a front wheel caught at a rough or angled crossing can put a rider down. **[VERIFY: rail line and crossings]** Bars and restaurants that over-serve a driver who then hits a rider can be liable under the dram shop act. The notice is due within 120 days after you hire a lawyer, and suit within two years (MCL 436.1801(3)). See our [drunk driving accident page](/personal-injury/drunk-driving-accidents).

**I-696.** At the Woodward and Campbell interchanges, and near the I-75 interchange, drivers merging and changing lanes often fail to check for a motorcycle in the next lane. Trucks add blind spots and wind blast. See our [Royal Oak truck accident page](/personal-injury/truck-accidents/royal-oak).

**The road itself.** A single-vehicle crash caused by a pothole, a broken pavement seam, a rough railroad crossing, or gravel is a claim against the agency with jurisdiction, under the highway exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1402). In Royal Oak there are three possible defendants:

- MDOT for Woodward and I-696: written notice filed with the Court of Claims within 120 days (MCL 691.1404; *Champine v Department of Transportation*, Mich 2022).
- **The Road Commission for Oakland County for Ten, Twelve, and Fourteen Mile**: written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **The City of Royal Oak for the other streets, including Eleven and Thirteen Mile, downtown, and the residential streets**: written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).

A rough railroad crossing may be the railroad’s responsibility rather than the road agency’s. A defect that was readily apparent to an ordinary observer for 30 days or more is conclusively presumed known to the agency (MCL 691.1403). Notice to the wrong agency is not notice.

## Who pays a Royal Oak rider’s medical bills

**If a car or truck was involved, you have no-fault benefits, but not from your motorcycle insurer.** Under MCL 500.3114(5), your Personal Injury Protection benefits come, in order, from:

1. the insurer of the owner or registrant of the motor vehicle involved
2. the insurer of that vehicle’s operator
3. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s operator
4. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s owner or registrant

Priority follows the vehicles’ insurers, not who was at fault. “Involved” does not require contact; a car that cut you off is involved even if it never touched you. If no insurer in that chain exists, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays (MCL 500.3172), with its $250,000 medical cap, unless you owned the motorcycle and it was uninsured (MCL 500.3113(b)).

The benefits are the ones a driver would get under MCL 500.3107:

- medical expenses, up to the PIP limit on the policy that pays
- 85 percent of lost wages for up to three years, up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year
- $20 a day in replacement services
- attendant care
- mileage

Written notice is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

The first medical limit is set by a policy you did not choose. Since the 2019 reform, the PIP medical level on the policy first in priority pays first; if the driver who hit you chose $250,000, that policy pays up to $250,000, and a lower-priority policy with more coverage, such as your own car policy, may then pay the rest.

**If no car or truck was involved, no-fault does not apply.** Several common crashes produce no PIP claim:

- a slide on gravel or sand at a downtown corner or driveway
- a front wheel caught at a railroad crossing
- a pothole or broken pavement seam on a city street
- a low-speed drop in Dream Cruise traffic with no other vehicle involved

Your bills go to your health insurance and to any medical coverage you bought on the motorcycle policy. Any injury claim is against whoever caused the hazard, if anyone did.

**Your motorcycle.** Your own collision or comprehensive coverage pays for the bike. If another driver was at fault, you also have the mini tort claim against that driver for up to $3,000 (MCL 500.3135(3)(e)). If the insurer will not pay it, the mini tort claim is filed in the 44th District Court.

## Your claim against the driver who hit you

For pain and suffering and economic losses beyond no-fault, you can sue the at-fault driver if your injury meets the threshold: serious impairment of body function, permanent serious disfigurement, or death (MCL 500.3135). A serious impairment is an objectively manifested impairment of an important body function that affects your general ability to lead your normal life. It does not have to be permanent (*McCormick v Carrier*, 2010).

Motorcycle injuries often clear that threshold. Common examples include:

- fractures
- road rash requiring grafts
- torn ligaments
- [brain injuries](/personal-injury/brain-injury)
- [spinal cord injuries](/personal-injury/spinal-cord-injury)

You also recover excess economic damages above the PIP limit that applied to you (MCL 500.3135(3)(c)). You have three years to sue (MCL 600.5805(2)). A fatal crash is brought by the estate; see our [Royal Oak wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death).

**What the claim is worth depends on the policies.** The default liability limit is $250,000 per person, but some drivers on Woodward carry the $50,000 opt-down minimum (MCL 500.3009(5)) or nothing. The recovery above the other driver’s limit turns on your own **uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage**. That coverage must be on the motorcycle policy, because many car policies exclude the insured while riding an owned motorcycle. Your UIM insurer’s written consent is needed before you accept the at-fault driver’s limit. See our [uninsured motorist page](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).

**Comparative fault is the fight.** Michigan’s rule is modified comparative fault (MCL 500.3135(2)(b), 600.2959):

- **More than 50 percent at fault:** you recover no noneconomic damages.
- **50 percent or less:** your award is reduced by your share.
- **Exactly 50 percent:** you recover half.

Economic damages beyond no-fault are reduced by your share as well. Insurers argue speed, lane position, lane splitting (illegal in Michigan), and “came out of nowhere.” The evidence that answers them:

- the Royal Oak Police report and witnesses
- the other driver’s phone records
- downtown business, parking-structure, and gas-station cameras along Woodward and the mile roads
- the rider’s own camera

## The helmet question

Since 2012, Michigan has let riders 21 and older ride without a helmet if they meet two conditions (MCL 257.658):

- They carry at least $20,000 in first-party medical coverage.
- They have either held a motorcycle endorsement for two years or passed an approved safety course.

Riders under 21 must wear a helmet. If you met those conditions, riding without a helmet was legal, so it was not a traffic violation. In a head-injury case, the insurer will argue a helmet would have reduced the injury. That is a damages and causation argument for the jury, not a bar to the claim. In a leg or shoulder case, it is irrelevant.

## What to do after a Royal Oak motorcycle crash

1. **Get medical care the same day.** Corewell Health William Beaumont University Hospital, on Thirteen Mile at Woodward, is Oakland County’s Level I adult trauma center.
2. **Get the police report.** Crashes in the city are worked by the Royal Oak Police Department, and freeway crashes may be worked by the State Police. If a car caused the crash without making contact, make sure the report says so. **[VERIFY: report request process]**
3. **Identify every vehicle involved,** including the one that never touched you.
4. **Photograph everything:** the bike, the scene, the turnaround or intersection, the road surface, your gear, and your injuries. Do not repair or sell the bike.
5. **Read your policies.** Check your motorcycle policy for first-party medical, UM/UIM, collision, and comprehensive coverage, and your car policy’s PIP level.
6. **Apply for no-fault benefits with the right insurer within the first week.** Do not give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurer.
7. **If a road defect or a bar was involved, call within days.** The 120-day road-defect notice is already running, and the dram shop notice runs from the day you hire a lawyer.

## Frequently asked questions

**A driver pulled out of a Woodward turnaround and I went down. Do I have a claim?** Usually. A driver leaving a turnaround must yield to traffic on Woodward. The car is “involved” under no-fault whether or not it touched you, so its owner’s insurer is first in line for your medical bills. Get the plate and witnesses before the car is gone.

**I was hurt during the Dream Cruise. Is that different?** The rules are the same: the rider priority rule for your bills, and the threshold and comparative fault for the claim against the driver. What changes is the evidence. Spectators’ phones and the cameras along Woodward often captured the crash, so ask for names and videos at the scene.

**Does the at-fault driver’s insurance pay my medical bills?** Not because the driver was at fault. Under the rider priority rule, the insurer of the car involved pays first, whoever caused the crash. Fault matters for your separate claim for pain and suffering.

**I have my own car insurance. Doesn’t that cover me on the motorcycle?** Not first. If a car was involved, that car’s insurer is first in line. Your own car insurer is third, after the involved vehicle’s owner’s and operator’s insurers. A motorcycle policy itself does not carry Michigan PIP. If no car was involved, there is usually no PIP claim at all.

**My front wheel caught at a railroad crossing. Who is responsible?** It may be the railroad, which generally maintains the crossing surface, or the road agency for the approaches. The notice rules differ for each, so photograph the crossing and call quickly.

**I wasn’t wearing a helmet. Can I still recover?** Yes, if three conditions were met: - you were 21 or older - you carried the required $20,000 in medical coverage - you had either two years of endorsement or a safety course. The insurer can argue a helmet would have reduced a head injury. That goes to damages, not to whether you have a claim.

**How long do I have?** - One year to notify the responsible no-fault insurer. - Three years to sue the at-fault driver. - 120 days for a dram shop notice, and two years to sue a bar. - 120 days for a notice to the City or the Road Commission. - 120 days for a road-defect notice against MDOT.
