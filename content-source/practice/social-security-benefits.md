## Social Security Benefits Lawyer Michigan

In December 2025, 2,381,255 Michigan residents received a Social Security benefit, and another 247,605 received Supplemental Security Income. Among them were 280,455 disabled workers, 134,942 children, 122,832 widows, widowers, and parents, and 56,551 spouses. Those categories are the point of this page. Most people who call a Social Security lawyer are asking about one benefit, disability. A worker’s record often pays for several, and the ones that go unclaimed are usually the ones no one explained.

A disabled Michigan worker may be entitled to **Social Security Disability Insurance** on her own record, **Supplemental Security Income** if her benefit is low, benefits for her **children** and sometimes her **spouse** on her record, **Medicare** after 24 months, and **Medicaid** through SSI. Her adult son with a lifelong disability may be entitled to **childhood disability benefits** on her record when she becomes disabled or retires, without ever having worked. ## The benefits a disabled worker can claim

### Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI)

The benefit earned by working and paying Social Security taxes. You must be insured, generally 40 work credits with 20 earned in the ten years before disability (fewer under age 31), and you must meet the federal definition of disability: unable to engage in substantial gainful activity because of a medically determinable impairment expected to last 12 months or result in death. The benefit is your primary insurance amount, based on your lifetime earnings, averaging $1,630 a month in January 2026. SSDI has a five-month waiting period, pays up to 12 months of retroactive benefits before the application date, and brings Medicare 24 months after entitlement. Our [SSDI page](/disability/social-security-disability-insurance) covers insured status, the benefit calculation, and the waiting period in detail.

### Supplemental Security Income (SSI)

The need-based program for disabled, blind, or aged people with limited income and resources. The federal payment in 2026 is $994 a month for an individual and $1,491 for a couple, reduced dollar-for-dollar by most unearned income (including SSDI, after a $20 exclusion) and by half of earned income above $65. Countable resources must be under $2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple. SSI brings Medicaid in Michigan, has no waiting period, and pays no retroactive benefits before the application. Many Michigan claimants receive both: a small SSDI benefit topped up by SSI, which is why 78,964 Michigan SSI recipients also receive Social Security. Our [SSI page](/disability/supplemental-security-income) covers income and resource rules, the Michigan state supplement, and living-arrangement reductions.

### Benefits for your family on your record

When a worker receives SSDI, members of the family may receive **auxiliary benefits** on the same record, each generally up to 50 percent of the worker’s benefit, subject to a **family maximum** of roughly 150 to 180 percent of the worker’s benefit:

- **Children** under 18, or under 19 and still in high school full time, including biological, adopted, and dependent stepchildren.
- **A spouse** caring for the worker’s child under 16 or disabled, at any age; or a spouse age 62 or older.
- **A divorced spouse** married to the worker for at least ten years, age 62 or older and unmarried.

These benefits are not automatic. Eligible family members must be identified on the application or claim benefits separately, so it is important to ask about every potentially qualifying dependent. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: 20 CFR 404.330–404.352, 404.403]**

### Childhood disability benefits (disabled adult child)

An adult who became disabled before age 22 may receive benefits on a **parent’s** record when the parent becomes disabled, retires, or dies, without ever having worked, under 42 USC 402(d) and 20 CFR 404.350. The adult child must be unmarried (with exceptions for marriage to another Social Security beneficiary) and must meet the adult definition of disability. This benefit is often worth far more than SSI to an adult with a lifelong intellectual or developmental disability, because it is based on the parent’s earnings record and carries Medicare after 24 months, and it is routinely missed: a 35-year-old with Down syndrome receiving SSI becomes eligible for childhood disability benefits the month his father starts SSDI or retirement, and no one at Social Security is obligated to notice. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

### Disabled widow’s and widower’s benefits

A surviving spouse (or surviving divorced spouse after a ten-year marriage) who is disabled may receive benefits on the deceased worker’s record **from age 50**, rather than the usual 60, if the disability began before the worker’s death or within seven years after it, under 42 USC 402(e)–(f) and 20 CFR 404.335–404.336. The benefit is 71.5 percent of the worker’s primary insurance amount. The seven-year window, and the fact that the surviving spouse’s own work record may be thin, make this a benefit worth checking in every case involving a disabled person whose spouse has died. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

### Survivors’ benefits when the worker dies

If a disabled worker dies, the surviving spouse, children, and sometimes dependent parents may receive survivors’ benefits on the record, and a one-time $255 lump-sum death payment is available to an eligible spouse or child. A pending disability claim does not die with the claimant; a survivor or the estate may be substituted and paid benefits due through the month of death. Our [Compassionate Allowances page](/disability/compassionate-allowances) covers substitution of party.

### Medicare and Medicaid

**Medicare** begins 24 months after SSDI entitlement (immediately for ALS and end-stage renal disease), and the months of back pay count toward the 24. Part A is premium-free; Part B carries a monthly premium that is deducted from the benefit. **Medicaid** comes with SSI in Michigan and is also available to many SSDI recipients through Michigan’s Medicaid programs for the disabled, including Michigan’s Freedom to Work program for qualifying employed adults with disabilities ages 16 through 64, subject to income and other eligibility requirements. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current MDHHS program names and eligibility]**

### How disability and retirement interact

SSDI converts automatically to retirement benefits at full retirement age, in the same amount; nothing changes but the label. The years on disability are a **disability freeze** that keeps zero-earning years out of the retirement calculation. A worker between 62 and full retirement age who takes early retirement while a disability claim is pending receives the reduced retirement benefit and, if the disability claim is later approved, Social Security recalculates the applicable benefits. Early retirement taken before disability entitlement may still cause a reduction, depending on the timing. The order and timing of these claims affect the lifetime benefit, and they are a question for the attorney at the first meeting. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

## After approval: the rules that decide what you keep

### Working while on benefits

**SSDI** has a **trial work period**: nine months, not necessarily consecutive, within a rolling 60-month window, in which you may earn any amount; in 2026 a month counts toward the nine when earnings exceed $1,210. After the trial work period comes a **36-month extended period of eligibility** in which benefits are paid for any month earnings fall below the substantial gainful activity level ($1,690 in 2026) and suspended for months above it. If benefits end because of work and the disability later prevents work again, **expedited reinstatement** restores them without a new application for up to five years. The **Ticket to Work** program provides employment services and protects against a continuing disability review while you are using it. Impairment-related work expenses, subsidies, and unsuccessful work attempts can reduce countable earnings. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: 20 CFR 404.1592, 404.1592a, 404.1592c]**

**SSI has no trial work period; instead, earned income generally reduces the SSI payment by about one dollar for every two dollars of countable earnings after applicable exclusions. Work incentives, including section 1619(b), may help qualifying recipients retain Medicaid even when earnings make them ineligible for SSI cash payments.**. The student earned income exclusion, Plans to Achieve Self-Support, and Michigan’s Medicaid continuation under section 1619(b) protect SSI recipients who work. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

### Continuing disability reviews

Social Security periodically reviews whether a beneficiary is still disabled, under 20 CFR 404.1589–404.1594. The schedule depends on the expectation of improvement set at approval: **medical improvement expected**, reviewed in six to eighteen months; **medical improvement possible**, about every three years; **medical improvement not expected**, about every five to seven years. Benefits continue unless Social Security shows **medical improvement related to the ability to work**, so the question at a review is not whether you are disabled today but whether you have improved since the last favorable decision. The review begins with a mailed form (SSA-455 short form or SSA-454 long form); a long form or a request for a consultative examination signals a full review. A beneficiary whose benefits are stopped because SSA finds that the medical disability has ended generally has 60 days to appeal. Continued benefits may be available during qualifying medical cessation appeals if the beneficiary makes a timely election, generally within 10 days after receiving the notice. SSA ordinarily presumes receipt five days after the notice date. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

### Overpayments

Social Security pays more than it owes when earnings are not reported promptly, when a trial work period is miscounted, when SSI income or living arrangements change, or through its own error. An overpayment notice gives 60 days to request reconsideration (disputing the amount) or at any time to request a **waiver** (Form SSA-632) on the ground that you were not at fault and cannot afford to repay or repayment would be unfair. Since April 2025, Social Security’s default recovery for Title II overpayments has been **50 percent of the monthly benefit**, up from 10 percent, unless the beneficiary requests reconsideration, a waiver, or a lower rate; SSI recovery remains capped at 10 percent. Overpayments under $2,000 can often be waived administratively. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current default withholding rates and administrative waiver threshold]**

### Offsets

- **Workers’ compensation and public disability benefits.** SSDI may be reduced when combined qualifying benefits exceed the applicable federal limit, generally the higher of 80 percent of average current earnings or the total family benefit, under 20 CFR 404.408. A Michigan workers’ compensation settlement should account for Social Security’s offset and lump-sum proration rules. The terms of a redemption agreement may affect the offset calculation when consistent with applicable law and SSA requirements. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**
- **Pensions from non-covered employment.** The Social Security Fairness Act, signed January 5, 2025, repealed the Windfall Elimination Provision and the Government Pension Offset. Michigan workers who spent part of their careers in public employment that did not pay into Social Security, including some municipal, police, and fire positions, are no longer penalized, and qualifying beneficiaries may have received or be entitled to adjustments for benefits payable beginning January 2024. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**
- **Unemployment.** Receiving unemployment benefits does not legally bar SSDI, but the certification that you are able and available for work is evidence a judge may weigh against a disability claim.

### Taxes

Up to 85 percent of Social Security benefits are subject to federal income tax when combined income exceeds $25,000 for an individual or $32,000 for a couple; a large back-pay award can be allocated to the years it covers to limit the tax. **Michigan does not tax Social Security benefits**; they are deducted from Michigan taxable income under MCL 206.30. SSI is not taxable at all.

## Frequently asked questions about Social Security benefits in Michigan

**What is the difference between SSDI and SSI?** SSDI is earned through work and paid from your earnings record; SSI is need-based and paid regardless of work history. Both use the same definition of disability. Many Michigan claimants receive both.

**Can my children get benefits because I am disabled?** Yes. Children under 18 (or 19 if still in high school) can receive up to 50 percent of your benefit each, subject to the family maximum. They must be claimed; it is not automatic.

**My adult son has had a disability since birth and gets SSI. Is there anything more?** Possibly a great deal more. When you become disabled, retire, or die, he may be entitled to childhood disability benefits on your record, which are often higher than SSI and carry Medicare. Ask about it before you file your own claim.

**My husband died and I am disabled. I am 54. Can I get anything?** Disabled widow’s benefits may be available from age 50 on his record if your disability began before his death or within seven years after it, at 71.5 percent of his benefit.

**Can I work while receiving SSDI?** Yes, within the rules. The trial work period allows nine months of unlimited earnings; after that, benefits are paid in months under $1,690 (2026) and suspended in months over it, for 36 months. Report every month’s earnings to avoid an overpayment.

**How often will Social Security review my case?** Every six to eighteen months, three years, or five to seven years, depending on whether improvement was expected when you were approved. Benefits continue unless Social Security shows medical improvement related to your ability to work.

**I got an overpayment notice. What do I do?** Act within 60 days to request reconsideration if the amount is wrong, and request a waiver at any time if you were not at fault and cannot repay. Since 2025 Social Security withholds 50 percent of a Title II benefit by default unless you request a lower rate.

**I receive workers’ compensation. Will SSDI be reduced?** Possibly. Combined benefits are limited to 80 percent of your pre-disability average earnings. How a Michigan workers’ compensation settlement is written affects the size of the offset for the rest of your life.

**Are my benefits taxed?** Federally, up to 85 percent may be taxable above $25,000 (individual) or $32,000 (couple) in combined income. Michigan does not tax Social Security benefits. SSI is never taxed.

**What does a lawyer cost?** Nothing unless you win, and then the lesser of 25 percent of back pay or $9,200, approved and paid by Social Security. There is no fee on ongoing monthly benefits.
