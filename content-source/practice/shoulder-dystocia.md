## Shoulder Dystocia Lawyer Michigan

Shoulder dystocia is the obstetric emergency that happens after the baby’s head is born: the front shoulder lodges behind the mother’s pubic bone and the body does not follow. The umbilical cord is compressed in the birth canal, the baby cannot breathe, and the physician has a few minutes to free the shoulder using a trained sequence of maneuvers. Done correctly, most shoulder dystocias end with a healthy baby. Done incorrectly, or too slowly, they end with a torn brachial plexus, a fractured collarbone or arm, a brain injured by lack of oxygen, or a baby who does not survive, and often with serious injury to the mother.

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists puts the incidence at 0.2 to 3 percent of vaginal deliveries. Michigan recorded 99,420 births in 2024, about two-thirds of them vaginal, which means somewhere between a few hundred and roughly two thousand Michigan deliveries a year involve a shoulder dystocia. Most are resolved in under a minute by McRoberts positioning and suprapubic pressure and leave no trace but a line in the delivery note.

What distinguishes a shoulder dystocia case from a shoulder dystocia is what the physician and nurses did when the shoulder stuck. ACOG says plainly that shoulder dystocia cannot be reliably predicted or prevented, and defense lawyers quote that sentence in every case. It is true, and it is beside the point. The standard of care is not that the shoulder never sticks; it is that when it does, the team recognizes it, calls for help, works through the maneuvers in sequence, never pushes on the top of the uterus, never pulls hard on the baby’s head, and documents what it did and when. Every one of those steps leaves a record. This page explains what the standard required, the injuries to the baby and to the mother, how a Michigan case is proved and defended, the damages and 2026 caps, and the deadlines.

## Who is at risk, and what the standard required before labor

ACOG identifies the risk factors but warns that most shoulder dystocias occur in babies of normal weight to mothers with no risk factors, which is why routine cesarean for suspected large babies is not recommended. The factors that matter:

- **Macrosomia**: a large baby, especially over 4,500 grams (9 lb 15 oz). The risk of dystocia rises steeply with birth weight.
- **Maternal diabetes**, pre-existing or gestational, which produces babies with disproportionately large shoulders and trunks relative to the head.
- **A prior shoulder dystocia**, particularly one that caused a brachial plexus injury.
- **Operative vaginal delivery** with vacuum or forceps, and a prolonged second stage.
- **Maternal obesity and excessive weight gain**, through their effect on fetal size.

The standard of care before labor is not to predict the unpredictable; it is to act on the risks that are known. ACOG’s own guidance says a planned cesarean **should be offered** when the estimated fetal weight is 5,000 grams or more in a mother without diabetes or 4,500 grams or more in a mother with diabetes, and should be discussed with a mother whose prior baby had a shoulder dystocia with a brachial plexus injury. A diabetic mother carrying a ten-pound baby who was never told any of this has a claim that begins in the prenatal record, before the delivery room.

## The maneuvers, in the order they are trained

When the head delivers and the shoulder does not follow, the “turtle sign” (the head retracts against the perineum), the clock starts. Most Michigan hospitals train the sequence with the mnemonic HELPERR, and many run simulation drills, which ACOG recommends.

| Step | Maneuver | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Call for help** and note the time the head delivered | Brings a second physician, additional nurses, anesthesia, and the pediatric team; starts the documented interval |
| 2 | **Evaluate for episiotomy** | Creates room for the internal maneuvers; does not itself free the shoulder |
| 3 | **McRoberts maneuver** | The mother’s thighs are flexed sharply onto her abdomen, rotating the pelvis and flattening the sacrum; resolves about 40 percent of cases alone |
| 4 | **Suprapubic pressure** | An assistant presses downward and to the side just above the pubic bone to push the front shoulder under it. This is pressure on the pubic bone, not the uterus |
| 5 | **Delivery of the posterior arm** | The physician reaches along the back arm, flexes it, and sweeps it across the chest and out, reducing the shoulder diameter |
| 6 | **Rotational maneuvers** (Rubin II, Woods screw, reverse Woods) | Internal pressure on the shoulders rotates them into the wider oblique diameter of the pelvis |
| 7 | **Gaskin (all-fours) maneuver** | The mother rolls onto hands and knees, which changes pelvic dimensions and frees the posterior shoulder |
| 8 | **Last resorts** | Deliberate fracture of the clavicle; the Zavanelli maneuver (pushing the head back in and performing a cesarean); symphysiotomy |

ACOG does not require the steps in one fixed order after calling for help, and many physicians go to the posterior arm early because it has a high success rate. What the standard requires is that appropriate maneuvers be used promptly, with the team moving to another maneuver when one is unsuccessful.

## The two things that must never be done

The maneuvers are what the standard requires. Two actions are what it prohibits, and they are at the center of most shoulder dystocia lawsuits.

**Fundal pressure.** Pushing down on the top of the uterus drives the impacted shoulder harder against the pubic bone, increases the force on the brachial plexus, and can rupture the uterus. ACOG states it should not be used. It still happens, usually by a nurse acting on instinct or on a physician’s instruction, and it is frequently the mother or a family member who remembers it, because it is rarely charted.

**Excessive traction on the head.** Pulling the baby’s head downward and sideways to free the shoulder stretches the nerves of the brachial plexus on the side of the front shoulder. ACOG’s guidance is that the physician should avoid excessive lateral traction and should use the maneuvers instead of force. The 2014 ACOG task force report acknowledges that the forces of labor themselves can stretch the plexus, and hospitals rely on that finding in every case; the same report says the injury results from excess force, and a severe, permanent injury in a large baby after a long, documented dystocia is a pattern the records have to explain. ## How long is too long

While the shoulder is stuck, the cord is compressed and the baby is not getting oxygen. The baby’s blood becomes more acidic with each minute. Published studies have found that the risk of brain injury rises as the head-to-body interval lengthens, with permanent injury and death becoming substantially more likely beyond about five minutes, although a healthy baby at term can often tolerate a somewhat longer interval. ACOG does not set a hard limit; the standard is that the team move through the maneuvers without delay and that the interval be documented.

Two numbers in the delivery note therefore matter more than any other: the time the head delivered and the time the body delivered. A delivery note that omits them, or records a two-minute interval in a baby born with a pH of 6.9 and seizures, is itself evidence.

## What shoulder dystocia does to the mother

The mother’s injuries are often overlooked, and her claim is separate from the child’s with its own two-year deadline.

- **Postpartum hemorrhage**, the most common maternal complication, from uterine atony after the prolonged second stage and the maneuvers.
- **Third- and fourth-degree perineal tears** and the incontinence and fistula that follow if they are not recognized and repaired properly.
- **Uterine rupture**, particularly where fundal pressure was used.
- **Symphyseal separation and nerve injury** from the extreme hip flexion of a prolonged McRoberts position.
- **Bladder injury** and, in the case of a Zavanelli maneuver or symphysiotomy, the complications of those procedures.
- **Psychological injury**, which Michigan law recognizes as part of the mother’s non-economic damages.

A mother who required a hysterectomy because of negligently managed hemorrhage after shoulder dystocia may qualify for Michigan’s higher non-economic cap if the resulting permanent loss of a reproductive organ left her unable to procreate.

## Proving a Michigan shoulder dystocia case

Michigan requires proof of the standard of care, breach, causation more probable than not, and damages, MCL 600.2912a, through experts who practice the same specialty as the defendant, MCL 600.2169. In a shoulder dystocia case the records and the experts line up as follows.

**The records**, requested by name:

- The prenatal record: estimated fetal weights and ultrasounds, glucose testing, the mother’s weight gain, any prior shoulder dystocia, and whether a delivery-planning discussion was documented.
- The labor record and the electronic fetal monitoring archive, which shows the length of the second stage and whether vacuum or forceps were used.
- **The delivery note**, which ACOG says should document the time of delivery of the head and body, the maneuvers used and their sequence, which shoulder was anterior, who was present, the traction applied, the infant’s condition and cord gases, and the explanation given to the parents. A note that reads “shoulder dystocia, relieved with McRoberts and suprapubic pressure, infant to NICU” after a permanent injury is as telling as a detailed one.
- The nursing notes, which often record what the physician’s note omits, including fundal pressure, who was called and when, and the times.
- The newborn record: Apgar scores, cord gases, resuscitation, X-rays, the neurological exam, and NICU course.
- The hospital’s shoulder dystocia protocol and its simulation training records, subject to any applicable peer-review privilege.

**The experts**: a board-certified obstetrician on the physician’s standard of care; a labor and delivery nurse on the nursing standard, fundal pressure in particular; a maternal-fetal medicine specialist on the prenatal decisions; a pediatric neurologist or brachial plexus surgeon on the grade of the nerve injury and the force it implies; a neonatologist and pediatric neuroradiologist where there is a brain injury; and life care and economic experts.

**The defense**, in nearly every case, is that shoulder dystocia is unpredictable and that maternal forces, not the physician, injured the baby. The answer is not to deny either proposition but to show, from the records, that the known risks were not acted on, that the maneuvers were not performed or documented, that prohibited force was used, or that the interval was too long, and that the injury pattern is one that those failures produce.

## Who is liable

- **The delivering physician or midwife** and their practice, for the prenatal counseling, the decision to proceed vaginally, and the management of the dystocia itself.
- **The hospital**, for its nurses (fundal pressure, failure to call for help, failure to note times), for its training and protocols, and for a non-employee physician under *Grewe v Mt Clemens General Hospital* (1978) and *Markel v William Beaumont Hospital* (2022) where the family looked to the hospital for care.
- **Residents and the teaching hospital.** Shoulder dystocia is a procedure in which an unsupervised or inexperienced resident has produced many of the reported verdicts nationally; the attending physician’s presence and the hospital’s supervision policy are in issue.
- **State university hospitals, in the Court of Claims, where the claim or a qualifying notice of intention generally must be filed within six months, MCL 600.6431.**

Michigan retains joint and several liability in medical malpractice where the plaintiff was without fault, MCL 600.6304(6).

## What can be recovered

**For the child**: uncapped economic damages for surgery, therapy, equipment, future care, and lost earning capacity; non-economic damages under MCL 600.1483. For a brain injury meeting the statute’s terms, the higher cap, $1,065,000 for 2026, applies. For a brachial plexus injury, which is an injury to peripheral nerves, most cases fall under the lower cap, $596,400.

**For the mother**: medical expenses, lost wages, future treatment, and non-economic damages for pain, disfigurement, loss of function, and psychological injury, potentially under the higher cap where negligent care caused permanent loss of or damage to a reproductive organ resulting in an inability to procreate.

**For the family**: the father’s loss of consortium; in a death, the wrongful death act’s damages. Settlements for a minor require court approval under MCR 2.420 and are structured to preserve the child’s benefits.

## What to do after a shoulder dystocia

1. **Ask for the delivery note and the discharge summaries for mother and baby before you leave the hospital.** They are the hospital’s own first account.
2. **Request the complete records in writing**: the prenatal record, the labor record and electronic monitoring archive, the delivery and nursing notes, and the newborn record with X-rays and cord gases.
3. **Write down what you and anyone in the room remember**: how long the shoulder was stuck, who came in, whether anyone pushed on your abdomen, how the baby looked, what was said.
4. **If the baby’s arm is not moving, insist on a referral** to a pediatric brachial plexus program if there is no clear improvement by two months; the window for nerve surgery is three to nine months.
5. **Enroll in Early On** (1-800-327-5966), Michigan’s free early intervention program, for any child with a persistent injury.
6. **Call a lawyer when an injury persists past three months.** The mother’s two-year clock and a university hospital’s six-month notice run from the delivery; the child’s claim runs to the tenth birthday, but the records are only required to be kept seven years.

## Frequently asked questions about shoulder dystocia claims in Michigan

**Is shoulder dystocia itself malpractice?** No. It is an emergency that occurs in up to three percent of vaginal deliveries and cannot be reliably predicted. The question is how it was handled: whether the known risks were acted on before labor, whether the maneuvers were performed promptly and in sequence, whether fundal pressure or excessive traction was used, and how long the baby was without oxygen.

**The doctor said the baby was big and that this just happens. Is that the end of it?** No. If the baby was estimated at 5,000 grams or more, or 4,500 grams with diabetes, ACOG says a cesarean should have been offered, and the prenatal record shows whether that conversation happened. If the estimates were lower, the case turns on the management of the dystocia itself.

**My baby’s arm was limp at birth. Is that always a permanent injury?** No. Most brachial plexus injuries from shoulder dystocia are stretch injuries that recover within three months. About 15 percent persist beyond a year. A child with no biceps recovery by three months should be evaluated at a pediatric brachial plexus center.

**The nurse pushed on my stomach. Was that wrong?** Fundal pressure is prohibited in shoulder dystocia because it drives the shoulder harder into the bone and increases the force on the nerves. Suprapubic pressure, which is pressure just above the pubic bone, is a correct maneuver. Where the pressure was applied matters, and your account of it may be the only record.

**How long was too long?** There is no fixed limit, but the risk of brain injury rises as the head-to-body interval lengthens, and permanent injury becomes substantially more likely beyond about five minutes. The delivery note should record both times. If it does not, that omission is itself significant.

**My baby was fine but I hemorrhaged and needed a hysterectomy. Do I have a claim?** Possibly. Hemorrhage is the most common maternal complication of shoulder dystocia, and its management has a standard of care. A hysterectomy that resulted from mismanaged hemorrhage is the mother’s own malpractice claim, with a two-year deadline, and the loss of the ability to have children places it under Michigan’s higher cap.

**Can we see the hospital’s training records?** Michigan hospitals are expected to train for shoulder dystocia, and many run drills. Whether protocols and training records are discoverable depends on the hospital’s privilege claims, which the attorney will address. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

**How long do we have?** The child: until the tenth birthday for an injury before age eight. The mother and father: two years. A university hospital: six months’ notice. The 182-day notice of intent precedes every suit.

**What is a shoulder dystocia case worth in Michigan?** It depends on the injury. A permanent brachial plexus injury, a brain injury, or a maternal hysterectomy are each valued differently, and the caps apply differently to each. Economic damages are uncapped.
