## Why the first two weeks decide a Michigan truck case

Trucking companies are required to keep the records that show whether their driver was legal, but only for a limited time. Under federal rules, hours-of-service logs must be kept for six months (49 CFR 395.8). Driver vehicle inspection reports must be kept for three months (49 CFR 396.11). Once those windows close, the records can be destroyed lawfully, and companies with something to hide do not wait that long.

The first thing a truck accident lawyer does is send a **preservation letter** (sometimes called a spoliation letter) to the trucking company and its insurer. It legally obligates them to keep:

- The truck’s electronic logging device (ELD) data, which records every minute the driver was on duty
- The engine control module, or “black box,” which records speed, braking, and throttle in the seconds before the crash
- Dash camera and cab camera footage, which is often overwritten within days
- The driver’s qualification file, medical certificate, and training records
- Post-crash drug and alcohol test results
- Dispatch records, delivery schedules, and communications with the driver
- Maintenance and inspection records for the tractor and trailer
- The bill of lading and cargo weight tickets

If the company destroys evidence after receiving the letter, a Michigan court can instruct the jury to assume the evidence would have hurt the company’s case. That instruction alone has settled cases. Without the letter, the evidence simply disappears and there is no consequence.

The company’s insurer will also try to take your recorded statement in the first days, while you are medicated and before you know the extent of your injuries. You do not have to give one. Refer them to your lawyer.

## The federal rules that decide fault in a truck accident

Every commercial truck over 10,001 pounds in interstate commerce is regulated by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA), and Michigan has adopted those rules for intrastate carriers. When a driver or company breaks one of them, it is evidence of negligence. The rules that come up most often:

**Hours of service (49 CFR Part 395).** A property-carrying driver may drive no more than 11 hours after 10 consecutive hours off duty, within a 14-hour on-duty window, must take a 30-minute break after 8 hours of driving, and may not drive after 60 hours on duty in 7 days or 70 hours in 8 days. Since December 2017 these hours must be recorded by an electronic logging device, not a paper log. A driver who was over hours is a fatigued driver, and the ELD data proves it.

**Driver qualification (49 CFR Part 391).** The driver must hold a commercial driver’s license for the vehicle class, pass a medical exam every two years, and have a clean enough record for the carrier to hire them. The carrier must check the driver’s history before hiring and review it every year. A company that hired a driver with a suspended CDL or a history of crashes is liable for negligent hiring, separate from what the driver did.

**Drug and alcohol testing (49 CFR Part 382).** After a crash involving a death, or an injury or tow-away where the driver was cited, the carrier must test the driver for alcohol within 8 hours and for drugs within 32 hours. If the test was not done, the company has to explain why.

**Vehicle inspection and maintenance (49 CFR Part 396).** The driver must inspect the truck before every trip and report defects; the carrier must repair them before the truck goes out again, and must keep maintenance records. Brake failures and tire blowouts are almost always maintenance cases.

**Cargo securement (49 CFR Part 393).** Loads must be secured to specific standards. Shifted cargo causes rollovers and jackknifes; fallen cargo causes the crashes behind the truck.

**Minimum insurance (49 CFR Part 387).** $750,000 for general freight, $1 million for oil, and $5 million for hazardous materials. Many carriers carry more. This is why a truck case is worth pursuing fully even when a car case with the same injuries would be limited by a $50,000 policy.

**Michigan’s weight law.** Michigan allows trucks up to 164,000 pounds on eleven axles, the highest gross weight limit in the United States (the federal limit is 80,000). Heavier trucks take longer to stop and do more damage when they do not. Overweight tickets and scale records are evidence in a Michigan truck case in a way they are not elsewhere.

## Who can be held liable for a Michigan truck accident

A car accident usually has one defendant. A truck accident can have six, and the size of the recovery depends on finding all of them.

- **The driver**, for what they did behind the wheel: speeding, fatigue, distraction, impairment, following too closely.
- **The motor carrier** that employs the driver, both for the driver’s negligence and for its own: hiring, training, scheduling that required the driver to break hours-of-service rules, and failure to maintain the truck. Michigan treats a driver who leases their own truck to a carrier as the carrier’s employee for liability purposes, so “independent contractor” is rarely the defense it sounds like.
- **The owner of the tractor or trailer**, if different from the carrier.
- **The shipper** that loaded the cargo, if improper loading or an undisclosed hazardous load caused the crash.
- **The freight broker** that hired the carrier, if it hired a carrier with a known safety record it should have checked. Broker liability is contested in the courts, but it is pursued, and brokers carry their own insurance.
- **The maintenance contractor** that serviced the truck, and the **manufacturer** of a part that failed.
- **A government body**, if a road defect contributed. In Michigan that claim requires written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).

Each of these has its own insurance policy. A case against the driver alone is limited to one policy; a case against the driver, carrier, and shipper has three.

## How Michigan no-fault works when you were hit by a truck

Michigan’s no-fault law applies to truck crashes the same way it applies to car crashes, with one practical difference: the stakes.

**Your own auto insurer pays first**, regardless of fault, through Personal Injury Protection (PIP): medical expenses up to the coverage level you chose, 85% of lost wages up to $7,455 a month for crashes on or after October 1, 2026 (DIFS Bulletin 2026-20-INS), $20 a day in replacement services, and attendant care. You must notify your insurer in writing within one year (MCL 500.3145). If you were a pedestrian or cyclist with no policy of your own, the truck’s insurer may be responsible for your PIP benefits, which is one of the few situations where the commercial policy pays no-fault.

**You can sue the truck driver and the carrier** for pain and suffering if your injury is a serious impairment of body function (MCL 500.3135): objectively manifested, affecting an important body function, affecting your general ability to lead your normal life. Truck crash injuries almost always clear the threshold.

**Excess economic damages are where truck cases differ.** If your medical bills exceed your PIP coverage level, or your wage loss exceeds the monthly cap or continues past three years, you recover the excess from the at-fault party. In a car case, that recovery is often limited by a $50,000 minimum policy. In a truck case, the floor is $750,000. For a client with a spinal cord or brain injury and lifetime care costs, that difference is the case.

**Comparative fault** applies: if you were more than 50% at fault you cannot recover non-economic damages, and below that your award is reduced by your share. Trucking insurers assign fault to the car driver as a matter of routine. The ELD, black box, and dash camera data are how a lawyer answers that.

## Why trucks crash: the causes we see in metro Detroit cases

- **Fatigue and hours-of-service violations.** Drivers paid by the mile have an incentive to drive past the limit. The ELD proves whether they did.
- **Distraction.** Federal rules ban handheld phone use by commercial drivers (49 CFR 392.82), and Michigan’s 2023 hands-free law applies as well. Phone records are discoverable.
- **Speed and following distance.** A loaded semi at 65 mph needs about 525 feet to stop, nearly two football fields. Michigan’s truck speed limit on freeways is 65 mph.
- **Blind-spot and wide-turn collisions.** A tractor-trailer has blind spots on all four sides; right-turn “squeeze” crashes at intersections are common on Michigan Avenue, Telegraph, and Van Dyke.
- **Brake and tire failures.** Maintenance records and the post-crash inspection decide these.
- **Shifted or overweight cargo.** Weight tickets and the bill of lading are the evidence.
- **Impairment.** The post-crash test is mandatory; a missing test is itself evidence.
- **Weather.** Michigan winters do not excuse a truck driver; federal rules require drivers to reduce speed or stop in hazardous conditions (49 CFR 392.14).

## What to do after a truck accident in Michigan

1. **Get medical care, and keep going.** Truck crash injuries are often internal or neurological and show up over days. A gap in treatment is the insurer’s first argument.
2. **Get the police report.** The Michigan UD-10 report will identify the carrier, the USDOT number on the truck, and the insurer. Buy it from the State Police Traffic Crash Purchasing System for $10.
3. **Photograph the truck**, especially the USDOT and MC numbers on the cab door, the license plates on the tractor and trailer, the cargo, and any company name. Carriers change insurers and trucks change hands; the numbers are permanent.
4. **Do not talk to the trucking company’s insurer**, and do not sign anything. They may offer to “cover your medical bills” in exchange for a release. The release ends your claim.
5. **Notify your own auto insurer** in writing within days. Michigan no-fault benefits depend on it.
6. **Call a lawyer within the first week.** The preservation letter has to go out before the six-month and three-month record windows start closing, and before camera footage is overwritten.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan truck accident claims

**Who pays my medical bills after a truck accident in Michigan?** Your own auto insurer, first, through no-fault PIP benefits, regardless of who was at fault. If your bills exceed your PIP coverage level, you recover the excess from the at-fault trucking company. If you had no auto policy and no household member did, the truck’s insurer may be responsible for your PIP benefits.

**Can I sue the trucking company, or only the driver?** Both, and usually the company is the more important defendant. The company is liable for its driver’s negligence and for its own: hiring, training, scheduling, maintenance. Its insurance policy is the one that pays.

**What if the truck driver was an independent contractor?** Under federal leasing rules and Michigan law, a carrier is generally responsible for a driver operating under its authority, whether the driver is an employee or an owner-operator leased to it. The “independent contractor” defense rarely works in truck cases.

**How long do I have to file a truck accident lawsuit in Michigan?** Three years from the crash (MCL 600.5805). But the evidence that wins the case can be lawfully destroyed after three to six months, so the practical deadline for hiring a lawyer is weeks, not years. No-fault notice to your own insurer is due within one year.

**How much is a truck accident case worth?** It depends on the injury, the available insurance (at least $750,000 for a commercial carrier), how many parties are liable, and what the records show. No honest lawyer gives a number before reviewing the medical and carrier records.

**What is a black box, and can I get the data?** Most commercial trucks have an engine control module that records speed, braking, throttle, and sometimes steering for the seconds before a crash. The carrier controls it. A preservation letter requires them to keep it; a lawsuit lets your lawyer download it. Without the letter, the truck goes back into service and the data is overwritten.

**The trucking company’s insurer called and offered to pay my bills. Should I accept?** Not without a lawyer reviewing what you are signing. Early offers are made before the extent of the injury is known and come with a release that ends the claim. Your medical bills are already covered by no-fault; the offer is not generosity.

**Does Michigan’s no-fault law limit what I can recover from a trucking company?** It sets a threshold, not a cap. You can sue for pain and suffering if your injury is a serious impairment of body function, which truck crash injuries almost always are, and there is no cap on those damages. Excess economic losses are also recoverable.

**What if the truck was from out of state, or from Canada?** Federal rules apply to any carrier operating in interstate commerce, and a Michigan court has jurisdiction over a crash that happened here. Canadian carriers crossing at Detroit must carry U.S.-compliant insurance. The case proceeds in Michigan.

**Should I hire a Michigan truck accident lawyer or any personal injury lawyer?** Ask any lawyer you interview two questions: when will the preservation letter go out, and have they downloaded ELD data before. The answers tell you whether they have handled truck cases.
