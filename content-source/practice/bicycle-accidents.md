## Michigan Bicycle Accident Lawyer

In 2024, 1,790 bicyclists were involved in crashes with motor vehicles on Michigan roads. Twenty-nine died and 1,369 were injured, 196 of them seriously. Four of every five of those crashes happened in daylight, and the deadliest hour was 8 AM, the morning commute. These were not riders doing anything unusual; 17 of the 29 killed were going straight ahead when they were hit.

If you were hit by a car, truck, or bus while riding, Michigan law gives you more than most riders know. Your medical bills are paid by no-fault insurance even though you were on a bicycle, and even if you do not own a car. The driver who hit you was required to pass you with at least three feet of clearance. And a bicycle has the same right to the road as a car, which matters when the insurer argues that you should not have been there.

## Who pays a cyclist’s medical bills in Michigan

A bicycle is not a motor vehicle, so a bicycle crash with no car involved is outside the no-fault system. But a cyclist hit by a motor vehicle is a “non-occupant” injured in a motor vehicle accident, and Michigan’s no-fault law covers them (MCL 500.3105). Your medical expenses, 85% of lost wages (up to $7,455 a month for crashes on or after October 1, 2026), replacement services, and attendant care are paid as personal injury protection (PIP) benefits regardless of fault (MCL 500.3107). The insurer that pays is set by statute, in this order:

1. **Your own no-fault auto policy**, if you have one, even though you were on a bike.
2. **The policy of a spouse or a relative you live with** (MCL 500.3114(1)).
3. **The insurer of the owner of the vehicle that hit you**, then the insurer of its driver (MCL 500.3115(1)). Unlike passengers, cyclists and pedestrians without household coverage still claim from the striking vehicle’s insurer; the 2019 reform did not change that.
4. **The Michigan Assigned Claims Plan**, if the vehicle was uninsured or fled and is never identified (MCL 500.3172), with medical benefits capped at $250,000.

The claim must reach the responsible insurer in writing within one year of the crash (MCL 500.3145(1)). A cyclist with no car in the household is not uninsured; the law assigns an insurer. The error that costs cyclists is assuming no-fault does not apply to them and submitting bills to health insurance alone, until the year runs out. ## Michigan bicycle laws that decide fault

The second claim, for pain and suffering and the losses PIP does not cover, is against the at-fault driver, and it requires a serious impairment of body function, permanent serious disfigurement, or death (MCL 500.3135). Fault in a bicycle case is built from the Vehicle Code, and these are the sections that matter:

- **A bicycle is a vehicle with the rights of one.** A person riding a bicycle on a roadway has the rights and duties of a vehicle driver (MCL 257.657). A driver who “didn’t expect a bike there” is admitting the failure to keep a lookout that the law requires.
- **Three feet to pass.** Since 2018, a driver overtaking a bicycle must pass at a distance of at least three feet to the left, and may cross the center line to do so when safe (MCL 257.636). Ann Arbor, Dearborn, Grand Rapids, Kalamazoo, and several other Michigan cities require five feet by ordinance, and the League of Michigan Bicyclists’ position is that the state law does not preempt them. A sideswipe, a mirror strike, or a pass that forced the rider off the pavement is a violation.
- **Riding to the right, with exceptions.** A cyclist going slower than traffic must ride as near to the right as practicable, except when passing, preparing for a left turn, avoiding hazards such as parked cars, debris, or a surface that is unsafe, when the lane is too narrow to share, or when riding in a right-turn-only lane (MCL 257.660a). “Taking the lane” is lawful in each of those situations, and insurers that call it negligence are misreading the statute.
- **Lights and reflectors.** A bicycle ridden at night must have a white front lamp visible from 500 feet and a red rear reflector visible from 100 to 600 feet (MCL 257.662). A rider without them can be assigned a share of fault; a driver cannot use the absence of a light to excuse hitting a rider they should have seen with headlights.
- **Sidewalks and crosswalks.** Michigan law allows sidewalk riding unless a local ordinance prohibits it, and a cyclist on a sidewalk must yield to pedestrians and give an audible signal before passing (MCL 257.660). A cyclist riding across a crosswalk has the protections of a pedestrian in many cities’ ordinances, and the driver’s duty to yield at crosswalks applies. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: local ordinance variation]**
- **Dooring.** A driver or passenger who opens a car door into the path of a cyclist without checking is negligent under the general duty of care, and several cities prohibit it by ordinance. Dooring crashes in Detroit’s and Ann Arbor’s parked-car corridors are a recurring case type.
- **Phones.** Holding a phone while driving has been illegal since June 30, 2023 (MCL 257.602b). In 2024, 45 cyclists were hit by drivers recorded as distracted, three of them fatally; phone records are subpoenaed in bicycle cases for the same reason as in pedestrian cases.
- **Right hooks and left crosses.** A driver turning right across a bike lane, or left across the path of an oncoming cyclist, must yield to traffic proceeding straight, and a cyclist is traffic (MCL 257.650, 257.657).

The vehicle’s owner is liable with the driver when the driver had permission (MCL 257.401), and Michigan’s $50,000 minimum liability limit (MCL 500.3009) is why the owner’s policy, the employer’s policy, and your own underinsured motorist coverage are identified at the start.

## “You weren’t in the bike lane”: fault and the 50% rule

Comparative fault (MCL 600.2959) reduces your pain-and-suffering recovery by your percentage of fault and bars it above 50%. It does not touch your no-fault benefits. What insurers argue in bicycle cases, and the answer:

| The insurer says | The law |
| --- | --- |
| “The cyclist wasn’t in the bike lane” | Michigan has no statute requiring a cyclist to use a bike lane; MCL 257.660a’s exceptions allow leaving the right edge for hazards, turns, and narrow lanes. A bike lane blocked by a parked car or debris is a hazard |
| “No helmet” | Michigan has no bicycle helmet law for adults. A helmet does not prevent the crash; the argument goes to the head injury’s severity and is contested with medical evidence. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: e-bike helmet rule for riders under 18]** |
| “No lights” (night crash) | A real factor under MCL 257.662, weighed against the driver’s speed, headlights, and lookout |
| “The cyclist ran the stop sign” | Cyclists must obey signals and signs (MCL 257.657); the driver’s own speed and right-of-way are weighed against it. Video and the vehicle’s event data decide these |
| “The cyclist was on the sidewalk” | Lawful unless a local ordinance prohibits it; the driver entering or leaving a driveway must yield to sidewalk traffic |
| “The cyclist swerved” | Riders swerve to avoid potholes, grates, and opening doors; the cause of the swerve is the question |

## Hit-and-run bicycle crashes

A driver who leaves the scene of a crash that injures a cyclist commits a felony (MCL 257.617). The cyclist still has claims: no-fault benefits through a household policy or the Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3172), and uninsured motorist coverage on a household auto policy for pain and suffering, subject to the policy’s reporting and physical-contact conditions. Report the crash to police immediately, note the vehicle, and look for cameras along the route; bike-mounted and helmet cameras, which many commuters now use, have identified fleeing drivers. ## E-bikes and e-scooters

Michigan defines three classes of electric bicycle and treats them as bicycles, not motor vehicles (MCL 257.4, 257.13e). An e-bike rider hit by a car therefore has the same no-fault claim as a conventional cyclist. Class 3 e-bikes (assisted to 28 mph) carry age and helmet rules for younger riders. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current e-bike age and helmet provisions]** Shared electric scooters, which operate in Detroit, Ann Arbor, and Dearborn, are treated differently in the Vehicle Code, and a scooter rider’s no-fault coverage depends on how the device is classified and on the policies involved. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: e-scooter classification under the no-fault act]** Call before assuming a scooter crash is uncovered.

## Potholes, grates, and road defects

A cyclist thrown by a pothole, a longitudinal grate, or a broken pavement edge has a possible claim against the government that maintains the road, under the highway exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1402). The exception is narrow: it covers the improved portion of the highway designed for vehicular travel, the government must have had notice of the defect, and written notice of the injury must be given within 120 days (MCL 691.1404). Whether a bike lane or shoulder is “designed for vehicular travel” is a contested question. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current case law on bike lanes and shoulders under the highway exception]** Photograph the defect and the location the same day; repairs follow crashes quickly.

## What to do after a bicycle crash

1. **Get medical care**, even for “road rash and a sore shoulder.” Collarbone, wrist, and head injuries declare themselves later.
2. **Call police and get the report number.** Insist on a report even if the driver wants to “handle it”; the report records the driver’s insurance and the three-foot and right-of-way facts.
3. **Photograph everything**: the vehicle’s position, your bike, the road, the bike lane, the pothole or door, your injuries.
4. **Keep the bicycle and the helmet as they are.** Do not repair the bike. The damage pattern shows where and how you were struck. **[CONFIRM: the firm arranges storage or inspection of the bicycle]**
5. **Report to the auto insurer in your household**, even though you were on a bike. That starts the PIP claim and the one-year clock.
6. **Do not give the driver’s insurer a recorded statement** about your position in the lane, your lights, or your helmet until you have spoken to a lawyer.
7. **Call a bicycle accident lawyer** who knows MCL 257.636 and 257.660a; those two sections decide most liability disputes.

## What a Michigan bicycle accident case is worth

- **No-fault benefits**: medical care to the policy limit (or $250,000 through the MACP), wage loss, replacement services, attendant care, and in a death, survivor’s loss and funeral costs.
- **The claim against the driver and owner**: pain and suffering, loss of enjoyment of life, scarring, and economic losses PIP does not cover, reduced by any fault assigned to you.
- **Property damage**: the bicycle, which for a commuter or racer can be a four-figure loss, and gear. The mini-tort limit of $3,000 applies to vehicles; a bicycle is claimed as ordinary property damage against the at-fault driver.

Cyclists’ injuries are serious relative to the speeds involved because the rider absorbs the entire impact, which is why these cases often meet the threshold and why the policy limits, not liability, usually cap the recovery.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan bicycle accidents

**Does Michigan no-fault insurance cover bicyclists?** Yes, when a motor vehicle is involved. A cyclist hit by a car is a non-occupant covered by PIP benefits (MCL 500.3105), paid by your household policy, the striking vehicle’s insurer, or the Assigned Claims Plan, in that order (MCL 500.3114, 500.3115, 500.3172).

**I don’t own a car. Who pays my medical bills?** If no one in your household has auto insurance, the insurer of the vehicle that hit you (owner first, then driver). If that vehicle was uninsured or fled, the Assigned Claims Plan. You must give written notice within one year.

**What is Michigan’s three-foot passing law?** A driver overtaking a bicycle must pass at least three feet to the left (MCL 257.636). Ann Arbor and Dearborn require five feet by ordinance. A pass closer than that is a violation and evidence of fault.

**Do I have to ride in the bike lane?** No Michigan statute requires it. You must ride as far right as practicable when slower than traffic, with exceptions for hazards, turns, and lanes too narrow to share (MCL 257.660a). A blocked bike lane is a hazard.

**I wasn’t wearing a helmet. Does that hurt my case?** Michigan has no adult bicycle helmet law. The insurer may argue it made a head injury worse; it does not affect fault for the crash, and it never affects no-fault benefits.

**Can I sue the driver?** If your injury is a serious impairment of body function, permanent serious disfigurement, or death (MCL 500.3135). Fractures, head injuries, and injuries requiring surgery usually qualify.

**The driver fled. Do I have a claim?** Yes. No-fault benefits through a household policy or the Assigned Claims Plan, and uninsured motorist coverage on a household policy for pain and suffering, subject to the policy’s conditions. Report to police immediately.

**I crashed because of a pothole. Can I sue the city?** Possibly, under the highway exception (MCL 691.1402), with written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1404). The exception is narrow and the notice is strict; photograph the defect and call promptly.

**I was on an e-bike. Does that change anything?** Michigan classifies e-bikes as bicycles (MCL 257.13e), so the no-fault analysis is the same. Class 3 e-bikes have age and helmet rules for younger riders.

**Who pays for my bike?** The at-fault driver’s liability insurer, as property damage; the $3,000 mini-tort cap applies to motor vehicles, not bicycles. Keep the bike unrepaired until it has been photographed and valued.
