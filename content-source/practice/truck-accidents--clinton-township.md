Clinton Township’s freight moves on state highways. Groesbeck Highway (M-97) cuts diagonally across the township toward Mount Clemens, lined with industrial buildings, trucking yards, and logistics businesses. Gratiot Avenue (M-3) carries trucks between the I-94 corridor, Mount Clemens, and the northern suburbs. ## Where Clinton Township truck crashes happen

**Groesbeck Highway and the industrial frontage.** Groesbeck meets the mile roads at skewed angles, with industrial driveways, truck terminals, and yards between them. Tractor-trailers turn wide into driveways across two lanes, back into docks and yards from the highway, stop in a travel lane waiting to enter, and pull out into fast traffic where the angled intersections limit sight lines. A car that strikes the side or rear of a trailer can go under it; that is an **underride** crash, among the most lethal in trucking. Trailers must carry rear impact guards that meet federal standards (49 CFR 393.86), and a missing, damaged, or substandard guard is evidence against the carrier and the trailer’s owner. When a facility’s dock layout or schedule puts trucks in the road, the facility or its logistics provider may share responsibility with the carrier.

**Gratiot Avenue.** Trucks run Gratiot’s length between I-94, Mount Clemens, and Chesterfield, past dealerships and strip centers. Rear-end crashes at the signals and in the stacked turn lanes, trucks turning across traffic into commercial driveways, and lane-change crashes into a car in the blind spot on the right side of the cab.

**Hall Road and the retail deliveries.** The big-box stores, Partridge Creek, and the restaurants along Hall Road take tractor-trailer and box-truck deliveries daily. Hall Road’s Michigan-left crossovers are a particular hazard: a tractor-trailer cannot always make the turnaround in one movement, and a truck stalled across a crossover or swinging wide into the far lanes is struck by traffic that does not expect it.

**I-94.** The freeway crosses the township’s southeastern corner. Its truck crashes are rear-end and underride crashes in congestion, lane changes into a car in the blind spot, and merge crashes at the Harper Avenue interchange.

**Whose road it is: never the township’s.** Michigan townships do not own public roads. Groesbeck, Gratiot, Hall Road, and I-94 are MDOT’s; a road-defect claim against the State goes to the Court of Claims, with written notice filed there within 120 days (MCL 691.1404). The county roads, including Garfield, Romeo Plank, Harper, Moravian, Cass, Metro Parkway, and the mile roads, are the Macomb County Department of Roads’, with 120 days’ notice (MCL 691.1404).

## Who is responsible, and who pays

**The carrier, not just the driver.** The motor carrier is responsible for its drivers’ compliance with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations and is liable for a driver’s negligence in the course of employment. Michigan applies the federal rules to intrastate carriers (MCL 480.11a), so a local hauler running between Groesbeck yards and Macomb County plants is held to the same standards as an interstate carrier. The rules that decide truck cases most often:

| Rule | 49 CFR | What it means for the case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hours of service | Part 395 | 11 hours driving in a 14-hour window after 10 off; 60/70-hour weekly limits; recorded on an electronic logging device. Waiting time at a dock counts as on-duty time. |
| Driver qualification | Part 391 | CDL, medical certificate, driving record, background check; negligent hiring and retention are claims against the carrier. |
| Post-crash testing | Part 382 | Alcohol within 8 hours and drugs within 32 after a fatal crash, or an injury or tow-away crash with a citation. |
| Inspection and maintenance | Part 396 | Pre-trip inspections, defect reports, maintenance records; brake, tire, lighting, and reflective-tape cases are maintenance cases. |
| Equipment: rear impact guards, lighting, reflectors | Part 393 | Underride guards (393.86) and conspicuity marking; a trailer that cannot be seen at night or lacks a working guard is a defective trailer. |
| Handheld phone ban; hazardous conditions | 392.82, 392.14 | No handheld phone use; reduce speed or stop in snow, ice, and fog. |
| Minimum insurance | Part 387 | $750,000 general freight; $1 million oil; $5 million hazardous materials. |

**The owner of the truck or trailer.** In Michigan, a vehicle’s owner is liable for its negligent operation by someone driving with the owner’s consent (MCL 257.401). When the tractor, the trailer, and the driver belong to different companies, which is common in freight, each may be a defendant, although a company in the business of renting or leasing vehicles is generally shielded from owner liability by federal law (49 USC 30106).

**Michigan no-fault still applies to you.** Your own auto insurer pays your medical bills, 85 percent of lost wages up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year, replacement services, and attendant care regardless of fault (MCL 500.3107), with written notice due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). The Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays if no household policy applies (MCL 500.3172). Your claim against the driver and the carrier for pain and suffering and losses beyond no-fault requires a threshold injury (MCL 500.3135) and must be filed within three years (MCL 600.5805(2)). If you were more than 50 percent at fault, you cannot recover pain-and-suffering damages, and below that your share reduces them (MCL 500.3135(2)(b)). The difference from a car case is the money available: the carrier’s $750,000-plus policy, often with excess coverage above it. See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

**Other defendants.** The shipper or broker that loaded the trailer or hired an unsafe carrier; the facility or logistics provider whose dock operations contributed; the maintenance contractor; the manufacturer of a failed component or a defective guard ([product liability](/personal-injury/product-liability)); and, for a government truck (MDOT, the Department of Roads, Clinton Township, SMART), the agency under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405).

**If you were the truck driver.** Workers’ compensation pays your benefits, and your employer usually cannot be sued (MCL 418.131). If you were injured in your employer’s truck, no-fault benefits generally come from the insurer of that truck (MCL 500.3114(3)), coordinated with comp, which is paid first (MCL 500.3109(1)). You can also sue a third party, such as another driver or a facility whose dock operation caused the crash. See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

## The evidence, and how fast it disappears

Carriers must keep hours-of-service records for six months (49 CFR 395.8) and driver inspection reports for three months (49 CFR 396.11); after that the regulations no longer require them. MDOT freeway cameras, business and yard cameras along Groesbeck, Gratiot, and Hall Road, and dash cameras overwrite in days to weeks. The truck’s engine control module and event data recorder can be cleared when it is repaired and returned to service, and a damaged rear guard can be replaced before anyone measures it.

The first act in a Clinton Township truck case is a **preservation letter**: to the carrier, its insurer, the tractor and trailer owners, the shipper or broker, and, where the crash happened at or near a facility, the facility and its logistics provider. It demands the ELD and engine data, dash camera footage, the driver’s qualification file and post-crash test results, dispatch records and bills of lading, dock appointment and arrival logs, maintenance files, and the truck and trailer themselves for inspection, including the rear guard. Once a party knows a claim is coming, destroying that evidence exposes it to spoliation sanctions. 

## What to do after a Clinton Township truck crash

1. **Call 911 and get medical care the same day.** Henry Ford Macomb Hospital in the township and McLaren Macomb in Mount Clemens are both Level II trauma centers.
2. **Photograph the truck**: the cab, the trailer, the USDOT number and carrier name on the door, both plates, the load, the rear guard and reflective tape, and the driveway or yard if the truck was entering or leaving one.
3. **Get the police report.** Most township crashes are worked by the Clinton Township Police Department; crashes on I-94 may be worked by the Michigan State Police. A serious crash may produce a separate commercial vehicle inspection report. **[VERIFY: commercial vehicle enforcement; report request process]**
4. **Do not speak to the carrier’s insurer or its investigator.**
5. **Report to your own insurer and file the Application for No-Fault Benefits** within the first week.
6. **Call a lawyer within days**, so the preservation letter goes out before the records expire and the trailer is repaired.

## The courts and deadlines

Most Clinton Township truck cases are filed in the Sixteenth Judicial Circuit Court (Macomb County Circuit Court) at 40 N. Main Street in Mount Clemens. Cases against out-of-state carriers may be filed in or removed to the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan, which sits in Detroit. Claims against the State go to the Court of Claims; claims up to $25,000 go to the 41B District Court on Starks Drive. A fatal crash is brought by the estate through the Macomb County Probate Court; see our [Clinton Township wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death).

## Frequently asked questions

**How many truck accidents happen in Clinton Township?** The State does not publish truck crash figures by township. Countywide, 1,257 heavy trucks and buses were involved in Macomb County crashes in 2024, and statewide 15,888 crashes involved a heavy truck or bus.

**My car went under the back of a trailer on Groesbeck. Is that the truck’s fault?** It may be partly or wholly. Trailers must carry rear impact guards built to federal standards, and a guard that is missing, bent, or too high to stop a car is evidence against the carrier and the trailer’s owner. The trailer has to be inspected before it is repaired.

**A truck was stopped in the lane on Groesbeck waiting to turn into a yard, and I hit it. Is that my fault?** Not necessarily. Following too closely is usually the claim against the rear driver, but a truck stopped in a travel lane without warning, without lights, or as a routine practice of a facility’s dock schedule can carry fault too.

**I was rear-ended by a semi on Gratiot. Who pays?** Your own no-fault insurer pays your medical bills and lost wages first, regardless of fault. The carrier’s insurer pays for pain and suffering and losses beyond no-fault once a threshold injury is established.

**I drive a truck and was hurt in a crash in Clinton Township. What are my claims?** Workers’ compensation through your employer, no-fault benefits coordinated with comp, and a lawsuit against any third party who caused the crash.

**Why does the lawyer need to act within days?** Because the records expire: six months for hours-of-service logs, three months for inspection reports, days or weeks for camera footage, and the truck’s electronic data and the trailer’s damage can disappear on repair.

**Is a truck case worth more than a car case?** The injuries are usually worse and the insurance is usually larger: a $750,000 minimum for a general freight carrier, against $50,000 for a minimally insured car.
