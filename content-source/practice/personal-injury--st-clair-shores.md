If you were hurt in St. Clair Shores, or on Lake St. Clair, because someone else was careless, you have a claim, and its deadlines start the day you are hurt:

## Who the defendant is in St. Clair Shores

St. Clair Shores is a city with its own police and fire departments, sidewalks, sewers, parks, and buildings. Apart from I-94, its major roads belong to Macomb County or the City. Its housing includes condominiums and apartment complexes along Jefferson and Harper, its lakefront has marinas, restaurants, and bars, and its retail runs along Harper. Each of those changes who you claim against.

- **Landlords, apartment complexes, and condominium associations.** A landlord must keep the premises and common areas fit for their intended use and in reasonable repair, and comply with health and safety laws (MCL 554.139). That covers stairs, railings, walkways, parking lots, lighting, and snow and ice the landlord controls. In a condominium, the association usually controls the common elements, and a fall on a common walkway, stair, or dock is a claim against the association and its management company. See our [tenant injury page](/personal-injury/tenant-injuries).
- **Stores, restaurants, and parking lots.** A fall in a store, a restaurant, or a parking lot on Harper or Jefferson is a premises claim against the owner, the tenant, or both, depending on who controlled the area. Snow and ice are often handled by a contractor, which can be a separate defendant. Since *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil* (2023), an “open and obvious” hazard such as visible ice goes to comparative fault rather than ending the claim. The deadline is three years. See our [slip and fall](/personal-injury/slip-and-fall) and [premises liability](/personal-injury/premises-liability) pages.
- **Marinas, docks, and seawalls.** A marina owes its customers the duty of any business to keep its docks, walkways, ramps, and fuel areas reasonably safe. Loose or broken dock boards, missing railings or lighting, ice on the docks in spring and fall, and unsafe launch ramps are the usual claims. A fall from a private dock or seawall at a home is a claim against the homeowner, usually paid by the homeowner’s policy.
- **The bar that served the drunk driver.** A bar, restaurant, or store that served a visibly intoxicated person, or a minor, is liable for the injuries that person causes (MCL 436.1801). The claim needs written notice within 120 days after you hire a lawyer, the drunk driver named and kept in the suit, and suit within two years. The State Police recorded 55 alcohol-involved crashes in St. Clair Shores in 2024, about one crash in twenty-five. See our [drunk driving accident page](/personal-injury/drunk-driving-accidents).
- **Negligent security.** A tenant or customer assaulted at a complex, a bar, or a parking lot may have a claim against the owner if it knew of a specific risk and failed to respond reasonably.
- **The roads are split.** MDOT: I-94 and its ramps, with written notice of a road defect filed with the Court of Claims within 120 days (MCL 691.1404). Macomb County or the City: Jefferson, Harper, Greater Mack, Little Mack, and the mile roads, depending on the segment. The I-94 service drives may be MDOT’s or the local agency’s. The City of St. Clair Shores: the local streets.
- A claim against the county or the City requires written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1402, 691.1404), and the notice must go to the agency that has jurisdiction.
- **Sidewalks: the City, and possibly the adjacent owner.** The City must keep public sidewalks in reasonable repair (MCL 691.1402a). A fall on a broken public sidewalk is a claim against the City, with three limits: the City must have known, or should have known, of the defect at least 30 days before the injury; a defect of less than two inches is presumed not to be actionable; and written notice is due within 120 days.
- Even where an ordinance puts sidewalk repair on the adjacent owner, that duty runs to the City and does not by itself make the owner liable to a pedestrian (Levendoski v Geisenhaver, Mich 1965). See our [sidewalk injury page](/personal-injury/sidewalk-injuries).
- **The City’s parks and lakefront.** The City operates lakefront parks, including Blossom Heath and Veterans Memorial Park. An injury caused by a dangerous condition in a public building, which can include a pool building or a park structure, falls under the public building exception, which requires that the City knew, or should have known, of the defect and failed to fix it within a reasonable time, and written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1406). An injury elsewhere in a park, on a lawn, a path, or a launch ramp, generally falls outside every exception to governmental immunity.
- **Sewer backups and flooded basements.** Heavy storms have flooded basements in parts of Macomb County. When a sewer system the City owns or operates backs up because of a defect the City knew or should have known of and failed to fix in a reasonable time, the City can be liable for the property damage and any physical injury the backup caused (MCL 691.1416–691.1417). The claimant must give the City written notice within 45 days after discovering the damage (MCL 691.1419).
- **City buildings and schools.** The public building exception also covers City buildings and the buildings of the school districts that serve the city, including Lakeview, Lake Shore, and South Lake (MCL 691.1406).
- **Police and fire are the City’s.** A crash with a St. Clair Shores Police car or fire truck is a claim against the City, under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405). EMS treatment claims are limited to gross negligence or willful misconduct (MCL 333.20965).
- **Buses.** A crash with a SMART bus on Jefferson, Harper, or Nine Mile, or with a school bus, is a claim under the motor vehicle exception. See our [bus accident page](/personal-injury/bus-accidents).
- **Dog owners.** An owner is liable for a bite when the person bitten was lawfully present and did not provoke the dog (MCL 287.351). See our [dog bite page](/personal-injury/dog-bites).
- **Nursing homes.** A claim against a nursing home may be ordinary negligence, with a three-year deadline, or medical malpractice, with two years and a 182-day notice, depending on whether the conduct involved professional judgment. See our [St. Clair Shores nursing home abuse page](/personal-injury/nursing-home-abuse).

## Boating, jet-ski, and marina injuries on Lake St. Clair

A boating injury is not a car crash. Michigan no-fault does not apply to a boat, so there are no PIP benefits and no threshold. The claim is a negligence claim against whoever caused the injury, and two bodies of law can govern it.

**Michigan’s Marine Safety Act.** Part 801 of the Natural Resources and Environmental Protection Act (MCL 324.80101 and following) sets the operating rules on Michigan waters:

- speed and distance rules
- operating under the influence (MCL 324.80176)
- reporting a boating accident that causes injury or death (MCL 324.80134)
- age and safety-certificate requirements for younger operators and for personal watercraft
- the boat owner’s liability for injuries caused by the negligent operation of the boat by someone using it with the owner’s knowledge and consent (MCL 324.80157)

A violation of these rules is evidence of negligence.

**Federal maritime law.** Lake St. Clair is navigable water connected to the Great Lakes, so an injury involving a boat on the lake will often fall under federal admiralty jurisdiction. That can change three things:

- **Comparative fault.** General maritime law applies pure comparative fault: an injured person’s recovery is reduced by their share of fault but not barred, even above 50 percent. That differs from Michigan’s rule on land.
- **The deadline.** A maritime personal injury claim generally has three years (46 USC 30106).
- **Limitation of liability.** A boat owner can file a federal action to limit its liability to the value of the boat after the accident. The owner must file within six months of receiving written notice of a claim. If the owner files, the injured person must file a claim in that case by the court’s deadline or risk losing it.

Under the saving-to-suitors clause, a maritime injury claim can usually still be brought in state court, in the Sixteenth Circuit.

**Who pays.** A boat owner’s watercraft policy, or the watercraft coverage on a homeowner’s policy for a smaller boat, usually pays. Some boat owners carry little or no liability coverage, and some homeowner’s policies exclude larger boats. Your own health insurance pays your medical bills in the meantime. If the boat operator was drunk, the bar, restaurant, marina, or club that served them may be liable under the dram shop act, with the same 120-day notice.

**What to do after a boating injury.**

1. Get medical care, and make sure the accident is reported to the agency that responded on the water.
2. Get the boat’s registration number, the owner’s and operator’s names, and any witnesses on nearby boats or the shore.
3. Photograph the boat, the scene, and any equipment involved.
4. Do not sign anything from the boat owner’s insurer.
5. Call a lawyer quickly. The boat may be repaired or sold, and a limitation filing has its own deadlines.

**Electrical hazards at docks.** Faulty shore-power wiring at a dock or marina can energize the water around it. A swimmer can be shocked or drown, a risk called electric shock drowning. A marina or dock owner responsible for the wiring can be liable, as can the electrician who installed it. Swimming near docks with shore power is a known risk, and it should be reported to the marina or dock owner.

## Electrical injuries on land

An electrical injury on land is a claim against whoever controlled the source:

- the utility, for a downed or low line, or a line too close to a building or tree
- the landlord, for faulty wiring in a rental (MCL 554.139)
- the electrician or contractor, for its own negligent work
- the manufacturer or seller of a defective appliance, tool, or cord (MCL 600.2945–600.2949a); see our [product liability page](/personal-injury/product-liability)
- for a worker, workers’ compensation, plus a suit against a third party who is not the employer (MCL 418.131, 418.827); see our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation)

Burns can be deeper than they look, so get care right away and keep the product. Most of these claims allow three years (MCL 600.5805(2)).

## When the other driver has no insurance, or not enough

Some drivers carry only the $50,000 opt-down minimum (MCL 500.3009(5)), and some carry nothing.

- **Against an uninsured driver,** the claim for pain and suffering depends on your own **uninsured motorist coverage**. Without it, the claim is worth what the driver can personally pay. A hit-and-run is usually a UM claim, with short notice terms in the policy.
- **Against an underinsured driver,** your own **underinsured motorist coverage** pays the difference up to your UIM limit, if you follow the steps in order:
 1. The at-fault driver’s insurer offers its limit.
 2. You notify your own insurer and ask for its written consent to settle.
 3. Only then do you accept the limit and sign the release.
 4. The UIM claim then proceeds.

Accepting without consent can forfeit the UIM claim under most policies. After a drunk-driving crash, the claim against the bar is a separate source, and it has to be coordinated with the UIM claim.

If you have no auto insurance of your own and no household policy, your no-fault benefits may come from the Assigned Claims Plan, with its $250,000 medical cap. Two rules cut the other way:

- An owner who was required to insure a car and did not is generally barred from PIP benefits (MCL 500.3113(b)).
- A driver who was operating their own uninsured vehicle cannot recover pain-and-suffering damages, whoever was at fault (MCL 500.3135(2)(c)).

See our [uninsured motorist page](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).

## What a St. Clair Shores claim is worth, and what reduces it

A claim includes two kinds of damages:

- **Economic damages:** medical expenses, lost wages, lost earning capacity, and replacement services.
- **Noneconomic damages:** pain and suffering, loss of enjoyment of life, and disfigurement.

There is no cap except in medical malpractice. In an auto case, noneconomic damages require a threshold injury: death, serious impairment of body function, or permanent serious disfigurement (MCL 500.3135(1)). A serious impairment does not have to be permanent (*McCormick v Carrier*, 2010). Vehicle damage up to $3,000 can be recovered from the at-fault driver through the mini tort claim, without a threshold (MCL 500.3135(3)(e)). A premises, boating, electrical, dog bite, or dram shop case has no threshold.

On land, your share of fault reduces the recovery, and above 50 percent it bars noneconomic damages (MCL 600.2959; in auto cases, MCL 500.3135(2)(b)). At exactly 50 percent, you recover half. On the lake, where maritime law applies, fault reduces the recovery but does not bar it. Where more than one party caused the injury, each defendant generally pays only its own share of fault under Michigan law (MCL 600.2956, 600.6304). That is why the landlord, the association, the management company, the snow-removal contractor, the marina, and the bar all have to be identified and named. Michigan does not award punitive damages in ordinary injury cases; exemplary damages for humiliation or outrage are available only in narrow circumstances.

You cannot sue the at-fault party’s liability insurer directly in Michigan. The claim is against the driver, owner, or business, and its insurer defends and pays.

The practical limit is often insurance:

- **The at-fault driver’s limit** defaults to $250,000/$500,000 unless the driver chose lower limits, down to the $50,000/$100,000 minimum (MCL 500.3009(1), (5)).
- **Your UM and UIM coverage** fill the gap.
- **In a boating case,** the boat owner’s watercraft or homeowner’s policy pays, and some boats are insured for little or nothing.
- **In a premises, workplace, or nursing home case,** the commercial policy of the landlord, association, owner, marina, contractor, or operator pays, and those policies are often larger than an auto policy.
- **In a dram shop case,** the bar’s liquor liability policy adds to it.

For a worker with a third-party claim, the workers’ compensation insurer has a lien on the recovery (MCL 418.827). Medicare, Medicaid, and health plan liens are negotiated the same way.

## What it costs

Michigan caps contingency fees in injury cases at one-third of the net recovery (MCR 8.121). Case costs are advanced by the firm and deducted from the recovery first; the one-third fee is calculated on the remaining amount. You pay nothing up front and nothing if there is no recovery.

The agreement is in writing. Read how costs are handled if the case is lost, because that is where firms differ: filing fees, records, experts, and depositions. A promise that “you pay nothing unless we win” should say whether that includes costs, and whether “win” means the same thing as “settle.” A success rate quoted without saying which cases it counts tells you little, and neither does a recovery total that does not say when or what the cases were.

**How long do I have to file a personal injury claim in St. Clair Shores?** Three years for most claims. Several deadlines are much shorter: - 45 days to notify the City of a sewer backup - one year to notify your no-fault insurer after a crash - 120 days to notify Macomb County or the City of a road or sidewalk defect, or a public agency of a building defect - 120 days for a road-defect notice on I-94 - 120 days for a dram shop notice after you hire a lawyer, and two years to sue the bar - a 182-day notice and a two-year limit for malpractice - on the lake, the short deadlines in a boat owner’s limitation case, if one is filed

**I was hurt on someone’s boat on Lake St. Clair. Do I have a claim?** Usually, if the operator was careless: going too fast, operating while drunk, not keeping a lookout, or letting someone fall or be thrown. No-fault does not apply to boats, so the claim is against the operator and the owner, and is usually paid by the boat or homeowner’s policy. Federal maritime law may apply, which reduces your recovery by your share of fault but does not bar it.

**The boat owner’s insurer says the owner has filed to “limit liability.” What does that mean?** A boat owner can ask a federal court to limit its liability to the value of the boat after the accident. If that happens, you must file your claim in that case by the deadline the court sets, or you may lose it. Talk to a lawyer as soon as you receive notice of the filing.

**I fell on a dock at a marina. Who is responsible?** Usually the marina, which must keep its docks, walkways, and ramps reasonably safe for its customers. Photograph the dock, report the fall, and keep your slip or launch receipt. At a private home, the claim is against the homeowner. At a City park launch or dock, governmental immunity may bar the claim.

**I fell on the stairs in my condominium or apartment complex. Who is responsible?** Usually the association or the landlord, and its management or maintenance company, which must keep common areas in reasonable repair. Report the fall in writing, photograph the stairs and lighting, and keep any earlier complaints you made. Since 2023, the fact that a hazard was visible does not by itself end the claim.

**A drunk driver leaving the Nautical Mile hit me. Can I sue the bar?** Possibly. A bar that served a visibly intoxicated person can be liable for the injuries that person causes. Written notice must reach the bar within 120 days after you hire a lawyer, and the suit must be filed within two years.

**My basement flooded with sewage after a storm. Do I have a claim against the City?** Possibly, if the City’s sewer system backed up because of a defect it knew or should have known of and failed to fix. The written notice to the City is due within 45 days after you discovered the damage.

**Can I sue the other driver’s insurance company?** No. In Michigan, the claim is against the driver, and the driver’s insurer defends and pays. Your own no-fault insurer is a separate claim, for your medical bills and lost wages.

**What does a St. Clair Shores personal injury attorney charge?** Michigan caps the fee in injury cases at one-third of the net recovery, with no fee if there is no recovery. Case costs are advanced by the firm and deducted only from a recovery.

**Where would my St. Clair Shores case be filed?** Claims up to $25,000 go to the 40th District Court on Jefferson Avenue. Injury suits over $25,000 go to the Sixteenth Judicial Circuit Court in Mount Clemens. Claims against MDOT go to the Court of Claims. A boat owner’s limitation case is in federal court in Detroit.

**I searched “St. Clair” and found a lawyer page for St. Clair County. Is that the same place?** No. St. Clair Shores is in Macomb County, and its cases are heard in the 40th District Court and the Macomb County Circuit Court in Mount Clemens. St. Clair County is a separate county to the north, with its own courts in Port Huron.

**If I was partly at fault, do I still have a claim?** On land, yes, unless you were more than 50 percent at fault, which bars pain-and-suffering damages. Below that, your recovery is reduced by your share, and at exactly 50 percent you recover half. On the lake under maritime law, your share reduces the recovery but does not bar it. No-fault benefits are paid regardless of fault.

**Do I need a lawyer whose office is in St. Clair Shores?** No. What matters is that the lawyer knows the courts your case will be in, the agencies, insurers, and property owners involved, and will meet you where you are.
