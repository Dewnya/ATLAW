## Ypsilanti Motorcycle Accident Lawyer

A motorcycle crash in Ypsilanti is governed by a rule many riders learn only after they are hurt: under Michigan no-fault, a motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle.” That definition decides:

- who pays your medical bills, and how much they pay
- whether you have a no-fault claim at all
- how the insurer will argue the helmet question

## Where Ypsilanti motorcycle crashes happen

**Michigan Avenue and Washtenaw Avenue.** Michigan Avenue runs through downtown and east through the township as a four- and five-lane road past strip retail, gas stations, and motels. Washtenaw Avenue comes in from Ann Arbor past shopping centers and along the EMU campus. The rider crashes are:

- **A driver turning left** across the rider’s path into a driveway, a shopping center, or a side street. Under Michigan law, a driver must yield to oncoming traffic before turning left (MCL 257.650). This is the classic motorcycle crash: the driver “never saw” the bike or misjudged its speed.
- **A driver pulling out** of a driveway or parking lot into the rider’s lane (MCL 257.652).
- **A lane change** into a motorcycle in the next lane (MCL 257.642(1)(a)).
- **Rear-end crashes** at signals and in the turn lanes.

Michigan Avenue at Prospect Street had 20 injuries from 30 crashes in 2024, the most injuries of any intersection on Washtenaw County’s top-ten list.

**The downtown grid.** On Michigan Avenue, Huron, Hamilton, Washtenaw, Cross, and the streets around Depot Town, the crashes are:

- **Lane changes and turns** where one-way streets merge and split. Hamilton Street at Washtenaw Avenue had 132 crashes in 2024, first in the county.
- **Car doors.** A driver or passenger who opens a door into traffic is responsible for doing so only when it is reasonably safe.
- **Buses and delivery trucks** stopping, pulling out, or turning across a rider near the Ypsilanti Transit Center.
- Rail crossings and patched pavement, which are slick in the rain.

**Huron River Drive, Ford Lake, and the township roads.** Huron River Drive follows the river through the city and the township, and roads such as Grove, Textile, and Whittaker, and the roads around Ford Lake, carry riders out of town. The crashes there are different from the city’s:

- curves with trees and guardrails close to the road, and little or no shoulder
- gravel and sand washed into the corners, and leaves in the fall
- a driver crossing the center line on a curve, or pulling out from a stop sign at an unsignalized crossing
- deer, especially at dawn and dusk; the township recorded 62 deer-involved crashes in 2024

**I-94.** On I-94 through the township, the crashes are:

- merges at the Huron Street, Michigan Avenue, and Rawsonville Road interchanges
- lane changes into a motorcycle in a driver’s blind spot
- trucks, with larger blind spots, wind blast, and tire debris
- rear-end crashes in the slowdowns at the ramps and in work zones

See our [Ypsilanti truck accident page](/personal-injury/truck-accidents/ypsilanti).

**Drinking and riding.** 31 percent of Michigan’s 2024 rider deaths involved alcohol. The township recorded 36 alcohol-involved crashes of all kinds in 2024, and the city 13. A bar or restaurant that over-serves a driver who then hits a rider can be liable under the dram shop act. The notice is due within 120 days after you hire a lawyer, and suit within two years (MCL 436.1801(3)). See our [drunk driving accident page](/personal-injury/drunk-driving-accidents).

**The road itself.** A single-vehicle crash caused by a pothole, a broken pavement seam, or a drop-off in the traveled lanes is a claim against the agency with jurisdiction, under the highway exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1402). In Ypsilanti there are four possible road defendants:

- **The City of Ypsilanti** for city streets: written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **The Washtenaw County Road Commission** for township roads, including the township’s stretches of Huron River Drive, Grove, Textile, and Whittaker: written notice within 120 days. Townships do not own roads.
- **MDOT** for I-94, US-12, M-17, and state trunklines through the city: written notice filed with the Court of Claims within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **Eastern Michigan University** for its campus drives: also a State claim, with written notice filed with the Court of Claims within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).

A defect that was readily apparent to an ordinary observer for 30 days or more is conclusively presumed known to the agency (MCL 691.1403). A notice sent to the wrong agency does not protect the claim, which is why we confirm jurisdiction at the exact location first. Gravel spilled by a truck or tracked out of a construction site is a claim against the hauler or the contractor, not the road agency.

## Who pays an Ypsilanti rider’s medical bills

**If a car or truck was involved, you have no-fault benefits, but not from your motorcycle insurer, and not first from your own car insurer.** Under MCL 500.3114(5), your Personal Injury Protection benefits come, in order, from:

1. the insurer of the owner or registrant of the motor vehicle involved
2. the insurer of that vehicle’s operator
3. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s operator
4. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s owner or registrant

Priority follows the vehicles’ insurers, not who was at fault. A rider does not claim PIP from “the at-fault driver’s insurer” as such; the insurer of the vehicle involved is first in line whoever caused the crash. “Involved” does not require contact; a car that cut you off is involved even if it never touched you. If no insurer in that chain exists, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays (MCL 500.3172), with its $250,000 medical cap, unless you owned the motorcycle and it was uninsured (MCL 500.3113(b)). In a crash with an uninsured driver, and with no car policy of your own in the household, the Assigned Claims Plan is often where the claim goes.

The benefits are the ones a driver would get under MCL 500.3107:

- medical expenses, up to the PIP limit on the policy that pays
- 85 percent of lost wages for up to three years, up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year
- $20 a day in replacement services
- attendant care
- mileage

Written notice is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

**The medical limit is set by a policy you did not choose.** Since July 2, 2020, Michigan drivers have chosen a PIP medical level for each policy (MCL 500.3107c). The policy first in priority pays first, up to its PIP level. If the driver who hit you chose $250,000, or a lower level, a lower-priority policy with more coverage, such as your own car policy, may then pay the rest. This is why optional first-party medical coverage on a motorcycle policy matters.

**If no car or truck was involved, no-fault does not apply.** This is where a rider’s claim differs most from a driver’s. A driver who hits a deer on Huron River Drive has PIP benefits from their own policy. A rider who hits a deer, with no motor vehicle involved, generally has none. The same is true of:

- a slide on gravel, sand, or leaves in a corner
- a pothole, a pavement drop-off, a broken seam, or a rail crossing
- a low-speed drop in a parking lot

Your bills go to your health insurance and to any medical coverage you bought on the motorcycle policy. Any injury claim is against whoever caused the hazard, if anyone did: the road agency for a defect, or the hauler or contractor that left gravel on the road. A car or truck that forced you to swerve, even without contact, is a vehicle “involved” in the crash, and that changes the answer.

**Your motorcycle.** Your own collision or comprehensive coverage pays for the bike. The mini tort claim does not cover a motorcycle, because a motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle” under the no-fault act (*Nerat v Swacker*, 150 Mich App 61, 1986). That makes your own collision and comprehensive coverage the main source for the bike.

## EMU students who ride, and mopeds

Many riders in Ypsilanti are Eastern Michigan University students. The priority chain is the same, but steps 3 and 4 depend on whose car policy covers you:

- **A student still domiciled with a parent** may be covered by the parent’s car policy as the “motor vehicle insurer” of the operator. Whether a student living in Ypsilanti is still domiciled with a parent depends on the facts.
- **An out-of-state student** with a car policy from another state may still have Michigan no-fault benefits through that insurer, if the insurer is certified to write in Michigan (MCL 500.3163).
- **A student with no car policy in the household** depends on steps 1 and 2: the insurers of the car involved. If there are none, the Assigned Claims Plan.

**Mopeds.** A moped is not a motorcycle under the no-fault act, and the rules for a moped rider hit by a car are not the rider rule above. A moped rider is generally treated like a pedestrian or cyclist, with benefits from the household policy first and, if there is none, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3115(1)). Helmet and licensing rules for mopeds are different too.

## Your claim against the driver who hit you

For pain and suffering and economic losses beyond no-fault, you can sue the at-fault driver if your injury meets the threshold: serious impairment of body function, permanent serious disfigurement, or death (MCL 500.3135). A serious impairment is an objectively manifested impairment of an important body function that affects your general ability to lead your normal life. It does not have to be permanent (MCL 500.3135(5); *McCormick v Carrier*, 2010).

Motorcycle injuries often clear that threshold. Common examples include:

- fractures
- road rash requiring grafts
- torn ligaments
- [brain injuries](/personal-injury/brain-injury)
- [spinal cord injuries](/personal-injury/spinal-cord-injury)

You also recover excess economic damages above the PIP limit that applied to you, including lost wages above the monthly cap (MCL 500.3135(3)(c)). **You have three years to sue** (MCL 600.5805(2)). A fatal crash is brought by the estate through the Washtenaw County Probate Court. The wrongful death claim also has three years, with an extension for the estate’s personal representative (MCL 600.5852). See our [Ypsilanti wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death/ypsilanti).

**What the claim is worth depends on the policies.** Some drivers carry only the $50,000 opt-down minimum (MCL 500.3009(5)), and some carry nothing. The recovery above the other driver’s limit turns on your own **uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage**. That coverage must be on the motorcycle policy, because many car policies exclude the insured while riding an owned motorcycle. Your UIM insurer’s written consent is needed before you accept the at-fault driver’s limit. See our [uninsured motorist page](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).

**Comparative fault is the fight.** Michigan’s rule is modified comparative fault (MCL 500.3135(2)(b), 600.2959):

- **More than 50 percent at fault:** you recover no noneconomic damages.
- **50 percent or less:** your award is reduced by your share.
- **Exactly 50 percent:** you recover half.

Economic damages beyond no-fault are reduced by your share as well; they are not recovered “regardless of fault.” Insurers argue speed, lane position, lane splitting (illegal in Michigan), and “came out of nowhere.” The evidence that answers them:

- the crash report from the Ypsilanti Police Department in the city, the Sheriff’s Office in the township, EMU police on campus, or the State Police on I-94
- witnesses
- the other driver’s phone records
- gas station, store, and motel cameras along Michigan Avenue and Washtenaw Avenue, and doorbell cameras in the neighborhoods
- photographs of the intersection, the driveway, the curve, the gravel, or the road edge
- the vehicles’ event data recorders
- the rider’s own camera

## The helmet question

Since 2012, Michigan has let riders 21 and older ride without a helmet if they meet two conditions (MCL 257.658):

- They carry at least $20,000 in first-party medical coverage.
- They have either held a motorcycle endorsement for two years or passed an approved safety course.

Riders under 21 must wear a helmet, and a passenger has rules of their own. If you met those conditions, riding without a helmet was legal, so it was not a traffic violation. In a head-injury case, the insurer will argue a helmet would have reduced the injury. That is a damages and causation argument for the jury, not a bar to the claim. In a leg or shoulder case, it is irrelevant.

## When the vehicle that hit you was an EMU, TheRide, City, Sheriff, or Road Commission vehicle

**An Eastern Michigan University vehicle.** EMU is a constitutional state university. A crash with a University vehicle or an EMU police car is a claim against the State under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405). It goes to the Michigan Court of Claims, decided by a judge, and a verified notice, or the claim itself, must be filed with that court within six months of the crash (MCL 600.6431).

**A TheRide bus, a City police car or truck, a Sheriff’s patrol car, or a Road Commission truck.** These are claims under the same exception against the agency that owns the vehicle, in the ordinary courts. The township’s police services are provided by the Washtenaw County Sheriff’s Office, so a crash with a patrol car in the township is a claim against the county.

In each case the agency is the owner of the “motor vehicle involved,” so its insurer, or the agency itself if it is self-insured, is generally first in line for your no-fault benefits under the rider priority rule. An individual officer or employee is personally liable only for gross negligence (MCL 691.1407(2)). Dash camera, body camera, and dispatch records are overwritten on short schedules, so we request them by FOIA promptly.

## What to do after an Ypsilanti motorcycle crash

1. **Get medical care the same day.** Trinity Health Ann Arbor, which has an Ypsilanti mailing address but sits in Superior Township, and University of Michigan Health in Ann Arbor are Level I trauma centers.
2. **Get the police report,** and note which agency responded. It tells you whether the crash was in the city or the township. If a car caused the crash without making contact, make sure the report says so.
3. **Identify every vehicle involved,** including the one that never touched you, and write down the vehicle number of any bus or public vehicle.
4. **Photograph everything:** the bike, the scene, the intersection, driveway, or curve, the road surface and any gravel or debris, your gear, and your injuries. Do not repair or sell the bike.
5. **Read your policies.** Check your motorcycle policy for first-party medical, UM/UIM, collision, and comprehensive coverage, and your car policy’s PIP level, or your parent’s if you are a student.
6. **Apply for no-fault benefits with the right insurer promptly, if a motor vehicle was involved.** Do not give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurer.
7. **If a road defect, an EMU vehicle, or a bar was involved, call within days.** The road-defect and EMU notices are already running, and the dram shop notice runs from the day you hire a lawyer.

## Frequently asked questions


**How many motorcycle crashes happen in Ypsilanti?** The State’s county profile does not break out motorcycle crashes by community. Countywide, 105 motorcycles were involved in Washtenaw County crashes in 2024. Five riders were killed and 25 seriously injured.

**A driver turned left in front of me on Michigan Avenue. Who is at fault?** Usually the driver turning left, who must yield to oncoming traffic, including a motorcycle. Insurers will argue your speed, so gas station and store cameras and witnesses matter, and footage is overwritten within days.

**Does the at-fault driver’s insurance pay my medical bills?** Not because the driver was at fault. Under the rider priority rule, the insurer of the car involved pays first, whoever caused the crash. Fault matters for your separate claim for pain and suffering.

**I hit a deer on Huron River Drive. Does no-fault pay?** Usually not, if no car or truck was involved. A motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle” under no-fault, so a deer strike with no other vehicle generally produces no PIP claim. Your health insurance and any medical coverage on your motorcycle policy pay instead, and comprehensive coverage pays for the bike. If a car forced you off the road, or hit you after you went down, the answer changes.

**I’m an EMU student. Whose insurance pays?** If a car was involved, that car’s insurer is first in line. After that, the car policy covering you as the operator, which may be a parent’s policy if you are still domiciled with your parent, or an out-of-state policy if the insurer writes in Michigan. If none applies, the Assigned Claims Plan.

**I was riding a moped. Is that the same?** No. A moped is not a motorcycle under the no-fault act, so the rider priority rule does not apply. A moped rider hit by a car is generally treated like a pedestrian or cyclist.

**A pothole on a township road caused my crash. Who do I notify?** The Washtenaw County Road Commission, in writing within 120 days, not the township, which owns no roads. A city street is the City of Ypsilanti’s, and I-94, US-12, and M-17 are MDOT’s, with written notice filed in the Court of Claims within 120 days.

**How long do I have to sue after a motorcycle crash?** Three years against the at-fault driver (MCL 600.5805(2)), and three years for a wrongful death claim, with an extension for the estate. Shorter deadlines apply to other parts of the claim: one year to notify the no-fault insurer, 120 days for a notice to the City or the Road Commission, 120 days for a road-defect notice against MDOT or EMU, six months for a crash with an EMU vehicle, and 120 days for a dram shop notice, with two years to sue a bar.

**I wasn’t wearing a helmet. Can I still recover?** Yes, if three conditions were met: - you were 21 or older - you carried the required $20,000 in medical coverage - you had either two years of endorsement or a safety course. The insurer can argue a helmet would have reduced a head injury. That goes to damages, not to whether you have a claim.
