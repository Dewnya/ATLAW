## Pontiac, MI Medical Malpractice Lawyer

A medical malpractice claim in Michigan has rules no other injury claim has: a two-year deadline instead of three; a written Notice of Intent served on each provider at least 182 days before suit; a sworn Affidavit of Merit from a qualified expert filed with the complaint; strict rules on who that expert can be; and a cap on pain-and-suffering damages. A case that misses any one of them can be lost before its merits are heard. For a Pontiac patient the first question is who the defendant is. Pontiac has two hospitals: McLaren Oakland on North Perry Street and Trinity Health Oakland on Woodward Avenue, both Level II trauma centers on the State’s list, which treat patients from Pontiac and much of north Oakland County. The most serious injuries may be transferred to Corewell Health William Beaumont University Hospital in Royal Oak, the county’s Level I adult trauma center. Day-to-day care happens in the medical offices around both hospitals, in urgent care clinics, and in community health centers, where health-system-employed physicians practice alongside independent practices and separate physician groups. Each has its own legal entity, insurer, and notice address, and a federally funded clinic is sued under federal law on a different clock in a different court.

## The rules, by the numbers

| Rule | Figure | Statute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deadline to file suit | 2 years from the act or omission | MCL 600.5805(8) |
| Discovery rule | 6 months from when the claim was or should have been discovered; never more than 6 years after the act (exceptions for retained objects, fraud, reproductive injury) | MCL 600.5838a(2) |
| Notice of Intent before suit | 182 days; the provider must respond in writing within 154 days | MCL 600.2912b |
| Affidavit of Merit | Filed with the complaint | MCL 600.2912d |
| Defendant’s Affidavit of Meritorious Defense | Within 91 days after the answer | MCL 600.2912e |
| Expert qualification | Same specialty; same board certification if the defendant is certified; majority of professional time in that specialty in the year before | MCL 600.2169 |
| 2026 cap on noneconomic damages | $596,400 (standard) / $1,065,000 (catastrophic) | MCL 600.1483 |
| Child injured before age 8 | Deadline runs to the 10th birthday | MCL 600.5851(7) |
| Federally funded provider | Administrative claim (SF-95) within 2 years; suit after denial or 6 months | 28 USC 2401(b), 2675 |

Economic damages, medical bills, lost earnings, and the cost of future care, are not capped. The Affidavit of Merit itself is required by MCL 600.2912d; MCL 600.2169 decides who is qualified to sign it.

## What malpractice is, and is not

A bad outcome is not malpractice. Malpractice is a bad outcome caused by a provider who failed to do what a reasonably competent provider in the same specialty would have done in the same circumstances (MCL 600.2912a). You must prove the standard of care, a breach, that the breach more likely than not caused the injury, and damages. Michigan requires proof that the patient would have had a better outcome; a lost chance of a better outcome is compensable only where that chance was greater than 50 percent (MCL 600.2912a(2)). A qualified physician’s written review of the records decides, before anything is filed, whether a case exists. Many do not, and we say so.

## Pontiac’s providers, and who answers for each

**McLaren Oakland and Trinity Health Oakland.** The city’s two hospitals, both listed as Level II trauma centers, and their employed physicians, nurses, residents, and staff. Trinity Health Oakland was formerly St. Joseph Mercy Oakland, and McLaren Oakland was formerly known by other names; health-system names and ownership change, so the correct legal entity for the Notice of Intent must be identified from current corporate filings and from the name the hospital used at the time of care, not from the sign or the letterhead. Claims against either hospital and its employed providers are filed in the Sixth Judicial Circuit Court (Oakland County Circuit Court) in Pontiac. Both are teaching hospitals with residents, and a resident’s error is generally the hospital’s responsibility.

**Corewell Health William Beaumont University Hospital.** The Level I adult trauma center in Royal Oak, where the most serious injuries, strokes, and cardiac emergencies may be transferred from Pontiac. A transfer case can involve two hospitals, two sets of physicians, and the transport between them, and the question is which delay or decision caused the harm. It is also in Oakland County, so the same circuit court hears it.

**Emergency physicians and other contracted groups.** Many hospitals staff their emergency departments, anesthesia, and radiology through separate physician groups. The Notice of Intent must name the group as well as the hospital. Where a patient came to the hospital for care and reasonably believed the doctor worked for it, Michigan law can hold the hospital responsible under ostensible agency (*Grewe v Mt Clemens General Hospital*, 1978).

**Medical offices, urgent care, surgery centers, and imaging centers.** The primary-care and specialty practices around both hospitals, urgent care clinics, and outpatient surgery, dialysis, and imaging centers in and around the city. **[VERIFY: outpatient centers in the city]** A delayed diagnosis in an office practice, a missed fracture or infection at urgent care, and a surgical complication at an outpatient center are frequent claims, and the employer of each provider who touched the chart must be identified, because a health-system-employed physician and an independent physician in the same building are different defendants with different insurers. Michigan rules apply.

**Federally funded community health centers.** Pontiac has community health centers that receive federal Health Center Program funding, and many Pontiac residents get their primary, prenatal, and dental care there. Providers at a funded center can be deemed federal employees for malpractice purposes. A claim against one follows the Federal Tort Claims Act, not Michigan procedure: an administrative claim on Standard Form 95 to the Department of Health and Human Services within two years (28 USC 2401(b)), no Notice of Intent, and suit in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan, tried to a judge without a jury. Identifying a deemed clinic early is the difference between a timely claim and none.

**Veterans.** Veterans treated at the John D. Dingell VA Medical Center in Detroit or a VA outpatient clinic have an FTCA claim on the same path, and may also qualify for disability compensation under 38 USC 1151 where the injury was caused by VA carelessness, negligence, or error, or by an event that was not reasonably foreseeable; see our VA benefit claims page.

**EMS.** In Pontiac, the Waterford Regional Fire Department responds to medical calls under contract with the City, and a private ambulance company transports patients. Claims against EMS personnel, and against the agency that employs them, public or private, including a private ambulance company handling a transfer between hospitals, are limited by the Emergency Medical Services Act to gross negligence or willful misconduct (MCL 333.20965(1)).

**Nursing homes and assisted living.** Pontiac has one Medicare-certified nursing home, as well as assisted living and adult foster care homes (CMS data, October 2026) A nurse’s wound-care decision is malpractice; leaving a resident unsupervised against the care plan is usually negligence. See our [nursing home abuse page](/personal-injury/nursing-home-abuse).

**Care in the county jail.** The Oakland County Jail is on the county campus in Pontiac. A claim about medical care in a jail is usually not a Michigan malpractice case against the County, which has governmental immunity; it is a federal civil-rights claim for deliberate indifference to a serious medical need (42 USC 1983), often against the County and the private company contracted to provide jail medical care, with its own standards and deadlines. A state-law malpractice claim may also lie against the contracted medical provider and its staff, who do not share the County’s immunity.

**Workplace clinics.** Occupational clinics and employer medical departments that treat injured workers are a separate case: a claim against a provider employed by your own employer may be barred by workers’ compensation exclusivity, while an outside clinic or physician can be sued for malpractice. See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

## How a Pontiac malpractice case proceeds

1. **The records**, including the electronic audit trail, from every provider, requested promptly, under whatever name the provider used at the time of care.
2. **Expert review** by a physician in the defendant’s specialty, in writing, before anything is filed.
3. EMS (EMS Act) Serving a Notice of Intent on a federal provider while the federal two-year clock runs can lose the case.
4. **The Notice of Intent** (MCL 600.2912b), served at least 182 days before suit. A timely notice pauses the two-year period during the notice period (MCL 600.5856(c)). If the provider does not respond within 154 days, suit may be filed then.
5. **The complaint and Affidavit of Merit** (MCL 600.2912d); the defense then files its own affidavit (MCL 600.2912e).
6. **Discovery, case evaluation, facilitation**, and, for cases that do not settle, trial to a jury in the Sixth Circuit on Telegraph Road. Two to four years is typical.
7. **Resolution**, with minors’ and estates’ settlements approved by the court or the Oakland County Probate Court, and Medicare, Medicaid, and health plan liens resolved before distribution.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long do I have to sue a doctor or hospital in Pontiac?** Two years from the malpractice, with a six-month discovery rule capped at six years. Because the Notice of Intent must be served 182 days before suit and the expert review comes first, call well inside the first year.

**Is the Notice of Intent “six months” or 182 days?** 182 days, which is not the same as six calendar months. A notice served on the last day of a six-month count can be late. Count the days.

**I was sent home from urgent care and it turned out to be serious. Is that malpractice?** It can be, if a reasonably careful provider would have recognized the warning signs or ordered the test, and the delay changed the outcome. The urgent care operator, not the hospital system whose name may be on the building, is often the employer, so the right defendant has to be identified first.

**I was transferred from McLaren Oakland or Trinity Health Oakland to Royal Oak. Which hospital is responsible?** It depends on where the decision or delay that caused the harm happened: the first hospital’s diagnosis and decision to transfer, the transport, or the care after arrival. Each provider is a separate defendant with its own Notice of Intent.

**I’m a veteran and was treated at the VA. Is that different?** Yes. A claim against VA care follows the Federal Tort Claims Act: an administrative claim within two years, no Notice of Intent, and suit in federal court without a jury. A veteran may also qualify for disability compensation for an injury caused by VA error or an unforeseeable event.

**My care was at a community health center in Pontiac. Is that different?** It may be. If the center is federally funded and its providers are deemed federal employees, the claim is against the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act. We check the center’s status at the first call.

**Is there a cap on what I can recover?** On pain-and-suffering damages only: $596,400 in the standard case and $1,065,000 for the catastrophic permanent injuries the statute lists, in 2026, adjusted each year. Medical bills, lost earnings, and future care are not capped.

**My child was injured at birth. How long do we have?** A child injured before age eight has until the tenth birthday to file. The parents’ own claims are subject to the ordinary two-year period, so the family should not wait.
