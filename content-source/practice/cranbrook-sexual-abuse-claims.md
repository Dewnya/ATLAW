## Michigan’s deadlines for survivors’ civil claims

Michigan has no criminal statute of limitations for first-degree criminal sexual conduct (MCL 767.24), and long periods for other degrees, but the person accused in the Cranbrook letter is deceased, and a civil claim for money damages is governed by different and shorter rules.

**Current law (MCL 600.5851b, enacted 2018).** A person who was a minor when the criminal sexual conduct occurred may file a civil action at any time before turning 28, or within three years after discovering both the injury and its connection to the abuse, whichever is later. For abuse of an adult, Michigan generally provides a ten-year period for civil actions seeking damages sustained because of criminal sexual conduct (MCL 600.5805(6)), while other personal injury claims may be subject to the general three-year period.

**The problem for Cranbrook-era survivors.** The 2018 law extended deadlines going forward; it did not revive claims that had already expired, except for a narrow 90-day window in 2018 covering certain abuse that occurred after December 31, 1996, where the perpetrator had a qualifying first-degree criminal sexual conduct conviction and made specified admissions involving physician authority or purported medical treatment. A survivor abused in the 1950s, 1960s, or 1980s who is now in their 50s to 80s is, under current law, outside the limitations period unless another doctrine applies.

**Doctrines that can still apply.** - **Fraudulent concealment** (MCL 600.5855): if the defendant, including an institution, concealed the existence of the claim or the identity of a responsible party, the survivor has two years from when they discovered or reasonably should have discovered the claim or responsible party. Michigan courts generally require affirmative acts of concealment, not mere silence, although an established fiduciary duty to disclose can affect that analysis. The doctrine is argued case by case. - **Insanity tolling** (MCL 600.5851(1)): a survivor who was unable to comprehend their rights at the time the claim accrued, which some survivors establish through psychological evidence, may have the period extended. Michigan courts apply this narrowly. - Recently discovered injuries. A survivor's recent discovery of the connection between an injury and decades-old abuse does not, by itself, revive a claim that had already expired before the 2018 law took effect. The discovery rule in MCL 600.5851b does not apply retroactively to revive those claims.

None of these is automatic, and each depends on facts a lawyer must review before telling you whether a claim exists. A lawyer who promises a Cranbrook survivor a lawsuit without that review is not being candid.

## The pending change: Senate Bills 257–261

Michigan’s Senate passed a package in May 2025 that would change every rule above. As passed by the Senate (25–9, May 20, 2025) and summarized by the Senate Fiscal Agency on August 18, 2025:

- **Longer deadlines.** A survivor could sue within 10 years after the claim accrued, before age 42, or within 7 years of discovering the injury and its cause, whichever is latest (SB 257). Suits against a person convicted of the conduct could be brought at any time.
- **A one-year revival window.** For one year after the law takes effect, survivors whose claims had expired could file them, subject to a cap of $1.5 million per defendant per incident on revived claims.
- **Institutional accountability.** Public schools and universities would lose governmental immunity where they were negligent in hiring, supervising, or training an abuser, or knew or should have known of the conduct and failed to report it (SB 260). Private schools such as Cranbrook never had governmental immunity; the bill matters to them mainly through the revival window.
- **Court of Claims procedure** changes for claims against the state (SB 259, 261).

As of October 2026, the bills remain pending in the Michigan House Committee on Government Operations following their May 20, 2025 referral and have not become law. If the revival window opens, it will be one year long, and survivors who have gathered their records and spoken to a lawyer beforehand will be able to use it; those who wait for the news may not.

## How a claim against a private school is built

Cranbrook Educational Community is a private, nonprofit institution. A claim arising from abuse by a faculty member is brought against the institution on theories that do not require proving the school intended harm:

- **Negligent hiring, supervision, and retention**: that the school knew or should have known of the risk the employee posed and failed to act.
- **Negligent failure to protect students**, including failure to adopt or follow policies, respond to complaints, or report.
- **Fraudulent concealment or misrepresentation**, where an institution knew of allegations and concealed them from families or later victims.
- **Vicarious liability** for the employee’s acts is limited in Michigan because sexual abuse is outside the scope of employment; the negligence theories above are the usual path.
- **Claims against the abuser’s estate**, where the abuser is deceased, are possible in principle but limited by probate rules and the estate’s assets.

The evidence in a decades-old case is different from a recent one: the school’s own records of complaints, personnel files, and board minutes (obtained in litigation), the accounts of other alumni, the investigator’s findings if ever released, contemporaneous letters and diaries, and the survivor’s treatment history. Institutions’ insurance policies from the period, which can still respond to claims decades later, are identified through the institution’s records.

## What to do if you are a survivor

1. **Get support first.** Confidential, free, 24 hours: the National Sexual Assault Hotline, 1-800-656-4673 (RAINN), and Michigan’s Sexual Assault Hotline, 1-855-864-2374. Neither requires you to report or to make a claim.
2. **Write down what you remember**, including dates, places, names of others who were present or who were told, and keep it private.
3. **Gather what you have**: yearbooks, letters, photographs, diaries, prior disclosures to family, therapy records.
4. **Do not sign anything from the school or an insurer** before you have talked to a lawyer.
5. **Talk to a lawyer about the deadlines**, now rather than when the law changes. The analysis of whether a claim exists today, and would exist under the pending bills, is specific to your facts.
6. **Consider the Michigan Crime Victim Compensation program (MCL 18.351 and following), which can pay counseling and other expenses for victims of crime in some circumstances. Applications generally must be filed within five years of injury or discovery, and the crime generally must be reported to law enforcement, although waivers may apply. Eligibility for decades-old abuse depends on the circumstances.**

## What a survivor’s claim can recover

Michigan law allows a survivor to recover for the psychological injury and its consequences: the cost of therapy and treatment past and future, lost earnings and earning capacity where the abuse affected education and work, and non-economic damages for the harm itself. There is no cap on non-economic damages in a negligence claim against a private institution under current law; the pending bill’s $1.5 million cap applies only to claims revived through its window. Michigan does not award punitive damages. Many institutional cases resolve confidentially; some survivors value a public resolution and some value privacy, and the choice is the survivor’s.

**Has a lawsuit been filed against Cranbrook?** Not that we have found in the public record as of this writing. The school disclosed a graduate’s report in April 2022, retained an investigator, and asked alumni to come forward. **[CONFIRM: update if the firm knows otherwise]**

**The abuse happened in the 1960s. Can I still sue?** Under current Michigan law, usually not, unless a doctrine such as fraudulent concealment applies to your facts. The Senate-passed bills would open a one-year revival window if enacted. A lawyer can tell you where your facts fall under both.

**What is the statute of limitations for child sexual abuse in Michigan?** A survivor abused as a minor may sue before age 28 or within three years of discovering the injury and its cause, whichever is later (MCL 600.5851b). Pending bills would extend this to age 42, ten years, or seven years from discovery.

**Does it matter that the accused teacher is dead?** For a criminal case, yes; there is no one to prosecute. For a civil claim, the institution is the usual defendant, and its liability depends on what it knew and did, not on whether the abuser is alive.

**Can I sue a private school for a teacher’s abuse?** Yes, on negligence theories: negligent hiring, supervision, retention, failure to protect, and concealment. Private schools have no governmental immunity.

**Should I contact Cranbrook’s investigator?** You may. Understand that the investigator works for the school and that your statement is not privileged. Many survivors speak to a lawyer first; some ask the lawyer to communicate for them.

**Will my name be public?** Civil suits can be filed under initials in many circumstances, and settlements are often confidential. Whether and how your identity is protected is discussed before anything is filed.

**What if I was abused by another student, not a teacher?** The school’s duty to supervise and protect students applies; the analysis of the school’s knowledge and response is the same. Claims against the other person depend on their circumstances.
