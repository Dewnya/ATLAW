## Birth Asphyxia Lawyer Michigan

Birth asphyxia is the deprivation of oxygen and blood flow to a baby’s brain around the time of birth. When it is severe enough to injure the brain, the diagnosis is hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, HIE, and it is the single most important cause of birth-related cerebral palsy, epilepsy, and lifelong cognitive disability. HIE occurs in roughly one to three of every thousand live births in developed countries; with Michigan’s 99,420 births in 2024, that is on the order of a hundred to three hundred Michigan families a year.

Some of those injuries could not have been prevented. Many could, and the medicine of the last twenty years has made the difference provable. Fetal heart monitoring shows the fetus in distress, often for an hour or more before delivery. Cord blood gases measure, in numbers, how severe and how recent the oxygen deprivation was. MRI shows a pattern of injury that dates it. And therapeutic hypothermia, which must begin within six hours of birth, gives a hospital a second chance that it either takes or does not. This page explains how HIE is diagnosed and staged, how a Michigan case proves the injury happened during labor, the cooling standard, who is liable, and the deadlines, including the one that runs to the child’s tenth birthday.

## How birth asphyxia happens

Oxygen reaches the fetus through the mother’s blood, the placenta, and the umbilical cord. Anything that interrupts that chain for long enough injures the brain. The causes, and the standard-of-care question each raises:

| Cause | What it is | The question in the case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Umbilical cord compression or prolapse** | The cord is squeezed by contractions or the fetal head, or drops through the cervix ahead of the baby | Was the variable-deceleration pattern recognized? Was a prolapse managed as the emergency it is (manual elevation, immediate cesarean)? |
| **Placental abruption** | The placenta separates from the uterine wall before delivery | Were bleeding, pain, and the tracing recognized? How fast was delivery? |
| **Uterine rupture** | The uterus tears, often along a prior cesarean scar during attempted vaginal birth after cesarean | Was the mother an appropriate candidate? Was the sudden fetal bradycardia acted on within minutes? |
| **Uterine hyperstimulation (tachysystole)** | Contractions too frequent to allow the placenta to re-oxygenate between them, usually from oxytocin or misoprostol | Was the drug reduced or stopped when contractions exceeded five in ten minutes or the tracing deteriorated? |
| **Prolonged or obstructed labor; shoulder dystocia** | Hours of pushing against an obstruction; the head delivered and the shoulders stuck | Was the labor curve followed? Was the dystocia managed within the minutes available? |
| **Maternal hypotension** | A drop in the mother’s blood pressure, often after epidural placement | Was blood pressure monitored and treated? |
| **Infection (chorioamnionitis)** | Infection of the membranes and fluid | Was fever recognized and treated? Was delivery expedited? |
| **Post-term pregnancy and placental insufficiency** | The placenta fails to support the fetus | Was antenatal testing done and acted on? Was induction timed appropriately? |
| **Delayed neonatal resuscitation** | The baby is born depressed and resuscitation is slow or inadequate | Was a team trained in neonatal resuscitation present? Were the steps followed in sequence and on time? |

## Diagnosing and staging HIE

HIE is diagnosed from what happened at birth and how the newborn behaves afterward:

- **Apgar scores** at one, five, and ten minutes. Persistently low scores (5 or less at five and ten minutes) are one marker of an acute event.
- **Umbilical cord blood gases**, drawn from the cord at delivery. An arterial pH below 7.0 and a base deficit of 12 mmol/L or more indicate significant metabolic acidosis. A hospital that fails to draw cord gases in a depressed newborn has lost the most objective evidence of timing; the omission itself is a departure from standard practice in many settings.
- **The Sarnat staging** of encephalopathy in the first days: Stage 1 (mild: hyperalert, jittery, normal tone, no seizures), Stage 2 (moderate: lethargic, low tone, weak reflexes, seizures common), Stage 3 (severe: stuporous or comatose, flaccid, absent reflexes, frequent seizures). Moderate and severe encephalopathy are the stages treated with cooling.
- **Multi-organ involvement**: kidney, liver, heart, and blood-clotting abnormalities that accompany a global hypoxic event.
- **Neuroimaging**: head ultrasound early, and MRI, usually at days 3 to 7 (or after rewarming), which shows the pattern of injury. Injury to the deep gray matter (basal ganglia and thalami) is associated with an acute, profound event; a watershed or cortical pattern with a prolonged, partial one. The pattern and its evolution on imaging are how experts date the injury.
- **EEG**, including amplitude-integrated EEG, for seizures and background activity.

## Therapeutic hypothermia: the six-hour standard

Since the mid-2000s, cooling a newborn with moderate or severe HIE to about 33.5°C for 72 hours, beginning within six hours of birth, has been the standard treatment. It reduces death and major disability; the benefit falls as the start is delayed, and after six hours it is largely lost. The eligibility criteria used by most programs: gestational age of 36 weeks or more; evidence of an acute perinatal event with a cord or early blood gas pH of 7.0 or less or a base deficit of 16 or more (or a less severe gas with a sentinel event, a low 10-minute Apgar, or prolonged ventilation); and moderate or severe encephalopathy on examination or EEG. **[ATTORNEY CHECK / MEDICAL CONSULTANT: criteria as stated]**

Cooling is available at specialized neonatal intensive care units in southeast Michigan, including Children's Hospital of Michigan and the University of Michigan's C.S. Mott Children's Hospital. A community hospital that delivers a depressed newborn and has no cooling program must recognize the candidate, begin passive cooling, and arrange transfer so that active cooling starts inside the window. A baby who met the criteria and was not cooled, or was cooled at hour eight, may have a claim that stands on its own, apart from what happened in labor, if the delay fell below the standard of care and caused a compensable injury. Michigan law distinguishes traditional malpractice claims, which require proof that negligence more probably than not caused the injury, from claims alleging a lost opportunity for a better outcome, which are governed by MCL 600.2912a(2). The cooling trials' data may be relevant to causation in either type of claim.

## Proving the injury happened during labor

The defense in nearly every HIE case is the same: the brain injury occurred before labor, from an infection, a genetic or metabolic condition, a placental problem that developed over weeks, or a stroke in utero, and nothing the labor team did or failed to do caused it. The answer is built from the criteria that obstetric and pediatric professional bodies themselves use to decide whether neonatal encephalopathy is consistent with an acute intrapartum event:

1. **Neonatal signs of an acute event**: Apgar of 5 or less at five and ten minutes; cord arterial pH below 7.0 or base deficit of 12 or more; MRI or MR spectroscopy showing acute injury consistent with hypoxia-ischemia; multisystem organ failure.
2. **Type and timing of contributing factors**: a sentinel event (uterine rupture, abruption, cord prolapse, amniotic fluid embolism, maternal collapse, fetal hemorrhage); a fetal heart rate pattern that was normal on admission and converted to a Category III pattern (absent variability with recurrent late or variable decelerations or bradycardia); the timing and pattern of the brain injury on imaging; and the absence of another explanation.
3. **Exclusion of other causes**: placental pathology, infection workup, genetic and metabolic testing.

Those criteria are applied by experts in maternal-fetal medicine, neonatology, pediatric neurology, and neuroradiology. The single most important document is the complete fetal monitoring strip, which records, minute by minute, whether the fetus was oxygenated when labor began and when it stopped being so, and what the staff did about it. The nurse’s documented interpretation, the times the physician was notified, the oxytocin rate, and the decision-to-incision interval for the cesarean convert the strip into a timeline. Hospitals must retain records for at least seven years (MCL 333.20175), but strips are archived separately and are sometimes incomplete; a written preservation request goes out as soon as a family suspects a problem. **[CONFIRM: the firm’s preservation practice]**

## What the child will need, and what Michigan law pays for

A child with moderate or severe HIE may have cerebral palsy, epilepsy, feeding and swallowing problems, vision and hearing impairment, and cognitive disability, and may need therapies, equipment, medications, surgeries, attendant care, and supported living for life. Michigan law pays for that through economic damages, which are not capped: a life care plan prepared from the treating physicians’ prognoses, lost earning capacity over a working life projected by a vocational economist, and the mother’s own losses. Non-economic damages for the child are capped at the brain-injury tier, $1,065,000 in 2026 (MCL 600.1483), and the mother has her own non-economic claim within the cap.

Public programs help now and are protected later: Michigan’s Early On program from birth to three, Children’s Special Health Care Services, Medicaid, and special education from age three. A settlement is placed in a special needs trust and structured annuities, approved by the court (MCR 2.420), so that the child keeps those benefits. ## Frequently asked questions about birth asphyxia and HIE claims in Michigan

**What is the difference between birth asphyxia and HIE?** Birth asphyxia is the oxygen deprivation; HIE (hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy) is the brain injury it causes, diagnosed from Apgar scores, cord gases, the newborn’s examination, and imaging.

**Was my baby’s HIE preventable?** That depends on what the fetal monitoring strip showed and when, how the team responded, how fast the cesarean was, and whether cooling started within six hours. Experts answer the question from the records; the hospital’s explanation is not the last word.

**What is therapeutic hypothermia and why does six hours matter?** Cooling the newborn to about 33.5°C for 72 hours reduces death and disability from moderate and severe HIE, but only if it begins within about six hours of birth. A hospital without a cooling program must identify the candidate and transfer in time.

**The hospital says the injury happened before labor. Is that the end?** No. That is the standard defense. Professional criteria for an acute intrapartum event (Apgars, cord gases, MRI pattern and timing, a sentinel event or a deteriorating tracing, exclusion of other causes) are what experts apply, and the complete strip usually answers the question.

**How long do we have to sue in Michigan?** For the child, until the 10th birthday if the injury occurred before age 8 (MCL 600.5851(7)); for the mother, two years. A 182-day notice of intent precedes suit.

**Is there a cap on damages?** Non-economic damages are capped at $1,065,000 in 2026 for the brain-injury tier. Lifetime care and lost earning capacity are not capped and are the largest part of an HIE recovery.

**Will a settlement affect my child’s Medicaid or SSI?** Not if structured through a special needs trust and court-approved annuities, which is standard in these cases.

**Can we sue the hospital if the doctor was not an employee?** Often yes, under Michigan’s ostensible agency rule (Grewe; Markel v Beaumont), and the hospital is directly liable for its nurses, its policies, and its cooling and transfer protocols.

**Our baby died. Is there a claim?** A neonatal death from preventable HIE is a wrongful death claim brought by the estate’s personal representative under the malpractice procedure, with a two-year period and a saving provision.

**What does it cost?** Nothing up front. HIE cases require several medical experts; the firm advances those costs and recovers them only from a settlement or verdict.
