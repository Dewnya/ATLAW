## Michigan’s hands-free law

Michigan’s hands-free driving law (MCL 257.602b, effective June 30, 2023) prohibits a driver from holding or using a mobile electronic device while operating a vehicle on a public road, including while stopped at a light. “Using” covers making or receiving a call, sending or reading a text or email, watching or recording video, using social media, and entering information into an app or navigation system by hand.

What is allowed: hands-free use through voice commands or a single touch to start or end a call, a device mounted on the dash or windshield, and use to call 911 or report an emergency. Commercial vehicle and school bus drivers are held to a stricter standard.

Penalties (MCL 257.602b(6)): a first violation is a $100 fine and/or 16 hours of community service; a second or later violation is $250 and/or 24 hours. The fines double if the driver was in a crash at the time. Three violations in three years require a driver improvement course. For commercial drivers the fines are $200 and $500.

**Kelsey’s Law** (MCL 257.602c) goes further for teen drivers: a driver on a Level 1 or Level 2 graduated license may not use a phone at all while driving, hands-free or not.

The law changed Michigan from a state that banned only texting (the 2010 law) to a state that bans holding the phone. That matters for an injury case: before 2023, a lawyer had to prove the driver was texting as opposed to dialing. Now, holding the phone is enough.

## How a hands-free violation proves your case

In a negligence case, you have to prove the other driver failed to use reasonable care. Michigan treats the violation of a safety statute as evidence of negligence: when a driver breaks a law designed to prevent exactly the kind of harm that occurred, the jury may find negligence from the violation itself. A driver cited under the hands-free law, or shown by phone records to have been using the phone at the moment of the crash, has very little to argue about on fault. The case then turns on the injury and its value.

The evidence, in the order a lawyer pursues it:

1. **The crash report (UD-10).** Michigan officers record a distraction code and the type (cell phone, other electronic device, passenger, activity inside or outside the vehicle). A hands-free citation issued at the scene goes on the report.
2. **The other driver’s statements**, to the officer, to witnesses, and in the first insurance call. “I only looked down for a second” is an admission.
3. **A preservation letter** to the driver and their insurer, sent within days, demanding that the phone and its contents not be wiped, replaced, or reset.
4. **Phone records by subpoena.** Once a lawsuit is filed, the carrier’s records (calls, texts, data sessions, with timestamps) are obtained by subpoena and matched to the crash time from the report, the 911 call, and the vehicle’s event data recorder. The carrier keeps the records; the driver cannot delete them. App-level data (social media, navigation, streaming) can be obtained from the device itself by forensic examination, which the preservation letter protects.
5. **Vehicle data and infotainment logs**, which in most vehicles since the mid-2010s record Bluetooth connections, calls placed through the vehicle, and in some cases text messages.
6. **Dash cameras and traffic cameras**, and in metro Detroit, the city’s Project Green Light cameras at participating businesses, which are overwritten on short cycles.

A driver who denies using the phone and is then confronted with carrier records showing a data session at the moment of impact does not usually go to trial.

## How Michigan no-fault applies to a distracted driving crash

Distracted driving changes who is at fault; it does not change how no-fault works. Your own auto insurer pays your medical bills and 85% of your lost wages (up to $7,455 a month for crashes on or after October 1, 2026) regardless of fault, with written notice required within one year (MCL 500.3145). To recover for pain and suffering and for economic losses beyond no-fault, you sue the distracted driver, and you may if your injury is a serious impairment of body function (MCL 500.3135). The driver’s violation makes the fault element of that lawsuit close to certain, which in practice means a higher settlement than the same injury with disputed fault.

Comparative fault still applies: if you were more than 50% at fault you cannot recover non-economic damages. A distracted driver’s insurer will look for distraction on your side too, which is a reason not to volunteer your own phone use to an adjuster.

If the distracted driver was working (a delivery driver, a rideshare driver, a company vehicle), the employer may be liable as well, and commercial drivers are subject to the stricter federal and Michigan rules. If the driver was a teen on a graduated license, Kelsey’s Law applies and the parents’ policy is the source of recovery.

## What to do after a crash with a distracted driver

1. **Tell the officer what you saw.** If you saw the driver on the phone, say so, and ask that it be recorded. If the driver admits it, tell the officer that too.
2. **Get witness names.** A passenger in another car who saw the driver looking down is the best evidence there is before the phone records arrive.
3. **Note the exact time** of the crash, from your own phone or the 911 call. The records are matched to the minute.
4. **Photograph the other driver’s phone** if it is visible (in a hand, in a lap, on the seat, with a screen lit).
5. **Get medical care the same day** and notify your own insurer in writing.
6. **Do not give a recorded statement** to the other driver’s insurer, and do not discuss your own phone use with anyone but your lawyer.
7. **Call a lawyer within days.** The preservation letter has to reach the driver before the phone is replaced or reset, and camera footage is overwritten within weeks.

## What a distracted driving case is worth

The violation makes fault clear; the value comes from the injury:

- **The injury** and whether it clears the serious-impairment threshold.
- **Economic losses beyond no-fault**: medical bills above your PIP level, wages above the cap or beyond three years, future care.
- **The driver’s policy limits** (Michigan minimum $50,000 per person) and your own UM/UIM coverage.
- **The strength of the distraction evidence.** A case with carrier records and a citation settles at a different number than a case with a suspicion.
- **Employer liability**, when the driver was working.

Michigan does not award punitive damages, so the driver’s conduct does not add a separate penalty, but a jury that hears a driver was on social media at the moment of a crash evaluates the injured person’s pain and suffering accordingly.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan distracted driving accidents

**Is it illegal to hold a phone while driving in Michigan?** Yes, since June 30, 2023 (MCL 257.602b). Holding or using a phone for calls, texts, video, social media, or manual navigation entry is a civil infraction: $100 and/or 16 hours of community service for a first offense, $250 and/or 24 hours after that, doubled if the driver was in a crash.

**How do I prove the other driver was on their phone?** The crash report’s distraction code, the driver’s statements, witnesses, and, once suit is filed, the carrier’s call and data records obtained by subpoena and matched to the crash time. A preservation letter sent early keeps the phone itself from being wiped.

**Can I get the other driver’s phone records myself?** No. Carriers release records only to the account holder or under subpoena. A lawyer obtains them after filing the lawsuit.

**Does a hands-free ticket mean the driver is automatically at fault?** Not automatically, but close. Violation of a safety law is evidence of negligence in Michigan, and a ticket plus records showing use at the moment of the crash leaves little to dispute.

**What if the driver was a teenager?** Kelsey’s Law (MCL 257.602c) bars drivers on Level 1 and 2 graduated licenses from any phone use, including hands-free. The teen’s parents’ policy covers the claim.

**What if the distracted driver was working?** The employer may be liable for a driver using a phone on the job, and commercial drivers face stricter rules. Delivery, rideshare, and company-vehicle crashes are investigated for employer liability.

**Does no-fault still pay my bills if the other driver was texting?** Yes. Your own insurer pays PIP regardless of fault. The distracted driver is liable for pain and suffering and for losses no-fault does not cover.

**Can I get punitive damages?** Michigan does not allow punitive damages in negligence cases. The driver’s conduct bears on the jury’s assessment of your non-economic damages, but there is no separate penalty award.

**How long do I have?** One year to notify your no-fault insurer, three years to sue the driver. The phone and camera evidence should be preserved within days.
