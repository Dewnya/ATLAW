## Southfield Slip and Fall Lawyer

For twenty years a fall on someone else’s property in Michigan was close to unwinnable, because a hazard that was “open and obvious” ended the case before it began. That rule is gone. Since the Michigan Supreme Court’s July 2023 decision in *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil*, a Southfield fall case turns on who had the duty to keep the place safe, what they knew about the hazard and when, what they did about it, and how much of the fall was your fault. ## Where Southfield falls happen, and who is responsible

**Office towers and office parks.** Southfield has a large stock of office space, along Northwestern Highway, at the Town Center, and in the parks off Telegraph and Evergreen. A fall in a lobby, a restroom, a parking deck, or an icy plaza usually involves several companies: the owner, a property management company, a janitorial or snow-removal contractor, and sometimes the tenant whose suite you were visiting. Each points to the others. The contracts decide who controlled the spot where you fell, so we request the management agreement, the cleaning and snow contracts, and the work orders along with the footage. A visitor on business is an invitee, owed the full duty of care: a property owner or possessor must exercise reasonable care under the circumstances, which may include inspecting for hazards, correcting dangerous conditions, or providing appropriate warnings.

**If you fell at work in one of those buildings,** your employer’s workers’ compensation covers the injury and your employer cannot be sued (MCL 418.131). But the building owner, the management company, or the contractor that left the floor wet is a third party, and a negligence claim against them proceeds alongside the comp claim. Comp’s lien is repaid from that recovery under MCL 418.827. See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

**Apartments and rental housing.** Southfield has a large rental housing stock, from older garden complexes to high-rises. Landlords must keep the premises and common areas fit for their intended use and in reasonable repair (MCL 554.139): broken stairs, missing handrails, dark stairwells, and failed lighting in parking lots are the usual claims. A lot with an inch or two of snow is still “fit” for parking under *Allison v AEW Capital* (2008), so tenant snow cases depend on whether the accumulation actually prevented use; the general negligence claim after *Kandil-Elsayed* is a separate route. A lease clause waiving the landlord’s liability for its own negligence is void. See our [tenant injury page](/personal-injury/tenant-injuries).

**Stores, restaurants, and retail.** Grocery stores, pharmacies, and restaurants along Telegraph, Greenfield, Eight Mile, and Twelve Mile: spills, mopped floors without signs, produce on the floor, ice at entrances, and potholes in shared lots. The case is decided on **notice**: actual (an employee saw or caused it) or constructive (it existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it). The lease decides whether the store or the shopping-center owner maintained the lot.

**City sidewalks.** A claim against the City of Southfield for a sidewalk defect proceeds under the highway exception (MCL 691.1402). The two-inch presumption is rebuttable but must be rebutted with evidence (MCL 691.1402a), so measure and photograph the defect with a ruler before it is patched. Written notice stating the exact location, the defect, the injury, and the witnesses must be served within 120 days (MCL 691.1404). The State’s and the Road Commission’s duty under the highway exception stops at the roadway and does not include sidewalks, so a sidewalk along Telegraph or Twelve Mile is generally a City claim, not an MDOT or Road Commission claim (MCL 691.1402(1)).

**Schools, the college, and City buildings.** Southfield Public Schools and Oakland Community College are governmental agencies; a fall caused by a dangerous or defective condition of one of their buildings proceeds under the public building exception, which requires the agency to have known of the defect and failed to fix it, with written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1406). The same rule applies to City Hall, the public library, and the City’s recreation buildings. Lawrence Technological University is private; ordinary premises law applies. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: confirm OCC campus and agency status]**

**Hospitals and nursing homes.** A visitor’s fall at Henry Ford Providence Southfield Hospital (formerly Ascension Providence) is a premises claim. A patient’s fall, or a resident’s fall in one of Southfield’s five CMS-certified nursing homes, may be premises, negligence, or medical malpractice, and which one decides the deadline and the notice requirements (*Bryant v Oakpointe Villa*, 2004). ## A parking-lot fall can be a no-fault claim

If you fell while getting into or out of your own car, or were hurt by property you were lifting onto or lowering from it, Michigan’s no-fault law may cover your medical bills and lost wages even though no crash occurred. An injury from a parked vehicle is covered when the person was “occupying, entering into, or alighting from” it, or was injured by property being lifted onto or lowered from it during loading or unloading (MCL 500.3106(1)). That claim runs on the no-fault notice rules: written notice to the auto insurer within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). It sits alongside any premises claim against the lot’s owner for the ice or the pothole. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

## The law after *Kandil-Elsayed*

Under the old rule (*Lugo v Ameritech*, 2001), an owner owed no duty to protect visitors from a danger an average person would have noticed, and insurers denied fall claims on that basis. In *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil* (Michigan Supreme Court, July 28, 2023), the Court held that whether a hazard was open and obvious no longer decides whether a duty existed. It is now a factor in whether the owner breached the duty of reasonable care, and in how much of the fault belongs to the person who fell; both are questions for a jury. That does not make every fall a case. A visible hazard still matters: if the jury assigns you some fault, your damages are reduced by that share, and above half you recover no pain-and-suffering damages, only reduced economic losses (MCL 600.2959).

Duty also depends on why you were there. A customer or business visitor is an invitee, owed inspection and repair; a social guest is a licensee, owed a warning of hidden dangers the owner knows about; a trespasser is owed protection from willful and wanton misconduct. Mixing these up, as some summaries do, overstates or understates the claim.

## Snow and ice

An owner must take reasonable steps, within a reasonable time after snow or ice accumulates, to make walkways and entrances safe. What is reasonable depends on the property: an office building open at 8 a.m. after an overnight storm is expected to have treated its entrances and its deck. The law does not require an owner to keep every surface bare at every moment of a storm. Black ice from refreezing meltwater, a leaking downspout, a clogged drain, or snow piled where it melts across the walk can be a condition the owner created, and those cases are stronger than natural accumulation.

On public sidewalks, Southfield’s ordinance requires the occupant of the adjoining property, or the owner if it is unoccupied, to clear snow and ice, and a sidewalk left uncleared for 24 consecutive hours is a violation (Southfield Code Secs. 4.75, 4.76). Photograph the ice, the path, the alternatives you had, and the weather the same day.

## What to do after a fall in Southfield

1. **Get medical care the same day** and tell the provider where and how you fell.
2. **Photograph the hazard immediately**, from several angles, with something for scale, before it is mopped, salted, or patched. Photograph your shoes.
3. **Report the fall** to the manager, the building’s security desk, the landlord, or the City, and get the incident report number. Do not give a recorded statement to any insurer.
4. **Get witness names**, including employees and security officers, and note the time; it matters for constructive notice.
5. **Measure a sidewalk defect** with a ruler in the photograph.
6. **Keep the shoes** unworn and unwashed.
7. **Call a lawyer within days.** Footage may be overwritten in days; if the City, the schools, or the college may be involved, the 120-day notice is already running.

## Frequently asked questions

**I slipped on ice outside my office building in Southfield. Can I sue?** Possibly, and not your employer. If you were at work, workers’ compensation covers the injury, and you may also have a claim against the building owner, the management company, or the snow-removal contractor responsible for the entrance.

**The hazard was obvious. Do I still have a case?** Possibly. Since 2023, an obvious hazard no longer ends the case. The questions are whether the owner should have fixed or treated it, whether you had a realistic alternative, and how much of the fault is yours.

**I fell on a broken sidewalk. Who is responsible?** Usually the City, which must have known or should have known of the defect, with a presumption that defects under two inches are reasonable repair. Written notice must reach the City within 120 days.

**I fell getting out of my car in a parking lot. Is that a car accident claim?** It may be. No-fault can cover injuries from entering or leaving a parked vehicle, separate from any claim against the lot’s owner.

**How long do I have?** Three years to sue a private owner, 120 days for written notice to the City, the schools, or the college, and one year to notify a no-fault insurer. The footage disappears far sooner.
