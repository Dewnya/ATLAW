## Detroit Workers’ Compensation Lawyer

A worker hurt on the job in Detroit is owed benefits under Michigan’s Workers’ Disability Compensation Act regardless of who was at fault: medical care in full, 80 percent of after-tax wages while unable to work, retraining, and scheduled payments for the loss of a body part. What makes Detroit different is who is on the other side. The city’s major employers, the automakers, the hospital systems, and the City of Detroit itself, are self-insured and administer claims through their own adjusters and third-party administrators, with their own clinics for the first 28 days of treatment. They know the Act in detail, and they deny claims in the places where it is narrow: the definition of disability, pre-existing conditions, the reasonable-employment rule, and the 90-day notice. The claim can be won at every one of those points, with the right evidence, in front of a magistrate at the agency’s Detroit hearing office.

## Where Detroit workers are hurt, and who handles the claim

Repetitive-motion and lifting injuries, crush and amputation injuries from presses and robotics, falls, and chemical exposures. The automakers are self-insured and have plant medical departments; the first 28 days of care are typically there. Union contracts add grievance rights and sickness-and-accident benefits that interact with comp. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: S&A coordination under MCL 418.354]**

**The hospitals.** The Detroit Medical Center, Henry Ford Health, and the nursing homes: patient-handling back and shoulder injuries, needlesticks and exposures, assaults by patients, and slips in clinical areas. The health systems are self-insured, and the injured worker’s treating clinic is often the employer’s own occupational health department.

**The City of Detroit.** Police, fire, EMS, DDOT drivers and mechanics, Public Works, and Water and Sewerage. The City is self-insured. Firefighters have a statutory presumption that certain cancers arose from employment (MCL 418.405). Police and firefighters also have duty-disability and pension provisions under the City’s retirement systems and bargaining agreements that run alongside comp and must be coordinated. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: 418.405 presumption; City duty-disability coordination]**

**Construction.** Downtown and Midtown projects, the Gordie Howe bridge approaches, the I-94 and I-375 reconstructions, and demolition across the neighborhoods. Falls, struck-by, electrocution, trench collapses, and workers struck by traffic in work zones (see our [pedestrian accident page](/personal-injury/pedestrian-accidents)). On a multi-employer site the injured worker’s claim is against their own employer’s comp insurer, and the general contractor, other subcontractors, and the property owner are potential third-party defendants whose negligence can be sued for. The MIOSHA investigation file and citations are the first evidence requested.

**Warehousing, delivery, and logistics.** The distribution centers along I-94 and at the former State Fairgrounds, package delivery, and freight handling at the intermodal yards. Lifting injuries and, for drivers, motor vehicle crashes on the job, which bring Michigan no-fault into the claim; see below.

**Casinos, stadiums, restaurants, and retail.** Repetitive-motion, slip and fall, burns, and lifting injuries in high-turnover workforces where injuries often go unreported past the 90 days.

**Temporary and staffing workers.** A worker placed by a staffing agency is the agency’s employee for comp purposes, and the host employer may also be treated as an employer entitled to the exclusive-remedy bar. ## What a Detroit worker is owed

**Medical care.** All reasonable and necessary treatment for the work injury, for as long as needed, with no deductible or co-pay (MCL 418.315): surgery, therapy, prescriptions, prosthetics, mileage, and attendant care at home, capped at 56 hours a week for family-provided care. For the first 28 days the employer chooses the doctor; after that you may treat with your own physician on notice to the employer (MCL 418.315(1)). An employer’s clinic that releases you to full duty on day 20 is a reason to see your own doctor on day 29.

**Wage loss.** Eighty percent of your after-tax average weekly wage, computed from the highest 39 of the 52 weeks before the injury and adjusted for filing status and dependents (MCL 418.351, 418.371), capped at the state maximum. Partial wage loss if you return at lower pay (MCL 418.361(1)). Benefits begin after seven days and are paid from day one if the disability lasts two weeks (MCL 418.311).

**Specific loss.** A scheduled number of weeks for the loss of a body part or its use, regardless of wage loss: 215 weeks for a hand, 162 for a foot or an eye, among others (MCL 418.361(2)). Total and permanent disability pays for at least 800 weeks and often for life (MCL 418.361(3)).

**Vocational rehabilitation** (MCL 418.319) and **death benefits** to dependents for 500 weeks plus burial (MCL 418.321, 418.345); a death case also raises the third-party wrongful death claim described on our [Detroit wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death/detroit).

**When the injury is permanent.** A Detroit worker whose injury keeps them from any work for a year or more may also qualify for Social Security Disability, which is coordinated with comp benefits and has its own application and appeal process; see our [Detroit Social Security Disability page](/disability/social-security-disability/detroit). Employer-provided short- and long-term disability plans pay alongside comp under their own terms; see our [long term disability page](/disability/short-long-term-disability). A work injury that causes a [brain injury](/personal-injury/brain-injury) or [spinal cord injury](/personal-injury/spinal-cord-injury) is a total-and-permanent-disability claim and, where a third party is at fault, a catastrophic injury suit. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: SSD/comp offset under 42 USC 424a]**

## Where Detroit claims are denied, and how they are won

**“Disability.”** Since 2011, disability means a limitation in wage-earning capacity in work suitable to your qualifications and training, considering all jobs you could perform (MCL 418.301(4)). The adjuster’s vocational expert will list jobs you supposedly could do. To keep full benefits you must show a good-faith search for work within your restrictions (MCL 418.301(5)); a written job-search log is the evidence.

**Pre-existing conditions.** A work injury that aggravates a prior back, knee, or shoulder condition is compensable only if the aggravation is medically distinguishable and work contributed in a significant manner (MCL 418.301(2)). The pre- and post-injury medical records decide it.

**Reasonable employment.** Refusing a job within your restrictions without good reason can reduce or end benefits (MCL 418.301(9)). Whether the job was real and reasonable is contested constantly.

**The IME.** The insurer can send you to its doctor (MCL 418.385), and a report saying you have recovered is the usual basis for cutting benefits off. Your treating physician’s records are the answer.

**Late notice.** The 90-day notice runs from the injury or from when you knew it was work-related (MCL 418.381(1)). Verbal notice counts but written notice is provable; a supervisor’s “we’ll handle it” is not a claim.

**Retaliation.** Firing or disciplining a worker for filing a claim is prohibited (MCL 418.301(13)) and is a separate lawsuit with its own damages.

## The hearing: Cadillac Place

When a Detroit employer or insurer denies a claim or stops paying, the worker files an Application for Mediation or Hearing (Form WC-104A) with the Michigan Workers’ Disability Compensation Agency. Disputed claims go first to mediation and then to a magistrate, who hears testimony and medical evidence and issues a written decision. Hearings for Wayne County workers are held at the agency’s Detroit hearing office in Cadillac Place, 3026 West Grand Boulevard. **[VERIFY address and current hearing-site assignments]** Either side can appeal to the Michigan Compensation Appellate Commission and then, by leave, to the Court of Appeals. A contested claim typically takes 12 to 18 months to decision, which is why most disputed claims resolve by **redemption**: a lump-sum settlement approved by the magistrate that closes the claim, including future medical care. Whether to accept one depends on what treatment you will need, and we say so before you sign.

## The lawsuit comp does not bar

Workers’ compensation is your exclusive remedy against your employer (MCL 418.131); you cannot sue the employer for pain and suffering except for a deliberate intent to injure. You can sue **anyone else** whose negligence caused the injury (MCL 418.827), and that suit recovers what comp does not: pain and suffering, full lost wages, and future losses. In Detroit the third party is often:

- **The other driver**, when the injury is a crash on the job. A DDOT driver, a delivery driver, a home health aide between patients. Michigan no-fault applies to the crash, and workers’ compensation is primary for the medical and wage benefits it covers, with no-fault paying the difference (MCL 500.3109(1)); the pain-and-suffering claim against the at-fault driver proceeds under MCL 500.3135. See our [Detroit car accident page](/personal-injury/auto-accidents/detroit) and [Detroit truck accident page](/personal-injury/truck-accidents/detroit). **[ATTORNEY CHECK: 3109 coordination]**
- **The general contractor, another subcontractor, or the property owner** on a construction or industrial site.
- **The manufacturer** of a defective press, lift, saw, or scaffold; see our [product liability page](/personal-injury/product-liability).
- **A property owner** whose hazard caused a fall while you were working; see our [Detroit slip and fall page](/personal-injury/slip-and-fall/detroit).
- **A dog owner**, for delivery and utility workers (MCL 287.351); see our [Detroit dog bite page](/personal-injury/dog-bites/detroit).

The comp insurer has a lien on the third-party recovery for the benefits it paid (MCL 418.827(5)), reduced by its share of attorney fees and costs. Negotiating that lien is part of the work and often adds more to what the worker keeps than the fee costs. The third-party suit is filed in the Third Judicial Circuit Court and must be filed within three years (MCL 600.5805(2)).

## What to do after a work injury in Detroit

1. **Report it in writing the same day**, to your supervisor and to HR, and keep a copy. Ninety days is the legal limit; the day of is the practical one.
2. **Get treatment**, at the employer’s clinic for the first 28 days if required, and tell every provider exactly how the injury happened at work.
3. **Count 28 days.** On day 29, if you are not satisfied with the employer’s doctor, see your own and notify the employer in writing.
4. **Keep your pay records** and, once you are released with restrictions, a written log of every job you apply for.
5. **Do not give a recorded statement** to the adjuster without advice, and do not sign a redemption agreement without understanding that it ends your medical coverage.
6. **If another company’s employee, a driver, a product, or a property hazard was involved, call within days.** The third-party evidence disappears on the same schedule as in any injury case.
7. **If a fatality or serious injury occurred, request the MIOSHA file** when it closes.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long do I have to report a work injury in Detroit?** Ninety days from the injury, or from when you knew it was work-related, to notify your employer, and two years to file a claim. Report it in writing the day it happens.

**My employer’s clinic says I can go back to work. Can I see my own doctor?** After the first 28 days, yes, with written notice to your employer. For the first 28 days the employer chooses. Your own doctor’s records are what answer the insurer’s doctor at a hearing.

**I work at a Detroit plant and was hurt. Is the plant’s medical department my only option?** For the first 28 days, generally yes. After that you may treat with your own physician. Your union contract may also provide sickness-and-accident benefits and grievance rights that run alongside the comp claim.

**I was in a crash while driving for work in Detroit. Who pays?** Workers’ compensation pays your medical and wage-loss benefits first, your no-fault insurer pays what comp does not cover, and the at-fault driver is liable for pain and suffering if you have a threshold injury. All three claims run at once, with different deadlines.

**Can I sue my employer?** No, except for a deliberate intent to injure. Workers’ comp is the exclusive remedy against the employer. You can sue any other company or person whose negligence caused the injury.

**What is a redemption, and should I take one?** A lump-sum settlement approved by a magistrate that closes the claim, including future medical care. Whether to accept depends on what treatment you will need and whether the lump sum covers it. Do not sign one without advice.

**Can I be fired for filing a workers’ comp claim?** Not lawfully. Retaliation is prohibited and is a separate lawsuit with its own damages.

**What does a Detroit workers’ comp lawyer cost?** Nothing up front. Fees are limited by state rule to a percentage of the benefits recovered and must be approved by the magistrate. You owe nothing if no benefits are recovered. **[CONFIRM]**

**I am undocumented. Can I still get benefits?** Medical benefits, yes. Wage-loss benefits are more complicated under current Michigan law. Call before you speak to the adjuster. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: keep or remove]**
