## Taylor, MI Truck Accident Lawyer

Taylor sits where Downriver’s freight routes meet.

- I-75 crosses the city’s south side, carrying trucks between Detroit, the Downriver plants and rail yards, and Toledo. It has interchanges at Telegraph, Eureka, and Allen/Northline.
- **I-94** crosses the north side toward Detroit Metropolitan Airport’s cargo operations, with interchanges at Ecorse, Telegraph, and Pelham.
- **Telegraph Road (US-24)** carries trucks the length of the city between the two interstates.
- **Eureka Road** brings tractor-trailers and box trucks to the loading docks of the Southland retail corridor, while delivery vans fan out into the subdivisions.

In 2024, according to the Michigan State Police, 3,916 heavy trucks and buses were involved in crashes in Wayne County, nearly twice Oakland County’s 2,081. Statewide, 110 people were killed and 3,613 injured in heavy truck and bus crashes. The State’s county profile does not break out truck crashes by city.

A truck crash is not a car crash with a larger vehicle:

- **The rules are federal.** The driver and the carrier are governed by federal safety regulations, and a violation is evidence of negligence.
- **The insurance is larger.** The carrier must carry $750,000 or more in insurance.
- **The records expire.** The records that prove the case, the driver’s electronic logs and the truck’s engine data, can be lawfully discarded within months.
- **The other side moves fast.** The carrier’s insurer will have an investigator on the case within days.

## Where Taylor truck crashes happen

**I-75 and I-94.** On the freeways, the typical truck crashes are:

- rear-end and underride crashes in congestion and in the backups at the interchanges
- lane changes into a car in the blind spot on the right side of the cab
- merge crashes on the ramps
- crashes caused by driver fatigue on long runs

A car that strikes the side or rear of a trailer can go under it. That is an **underride** crash, among the deadliest in trucking. Trailers must carry rear impact guards that meet federal standards (49 CFR 393.86), and a missing, damaged, or substandard guard is evidence against the carrier and the trailer’s owner.

Freeway work zones add crashes with trucks that cannot slow in time for a lane closure. In a work zone, the contractor can share responsibility.

**Telegraph Road.** Telegraph carries tractor-trailers between I-94 and I-75 and on to Monroe County, through signals at Van Born, Ecorse, Goddard, Northline, and Eureka. Eureka at Telegraph had 68 crashes and 36 injuries in 2024, the fifth highest crash count in Wayne County. The truck crashes on Telegraph are:

- wide right turns that catch a car on the inside of the turn
- left turns across traffic by a truck too long to clear the intersection before the light changes
- rear-end crashes by trucks that could not stop for a changing signal

On designated Michigan routes, heavy trucks can weigh up to 164,000 pounds, about twice the federal Interstate limit, and they need far more distance to stop.

**Eureka Road and the retail docks.** Stores, malls, and restaurants along Eureka are supplied by tractor-trailers and box trucks. Those trucks:

- turn into service drives across traffic
- back into loading docks from the road
- park on the shoulder while they wait for a dock

When a property’s dock layout or delivery schedule puts trucks in the road, the property owner or tenant may share responsibility with the carrier. See our [premises liability page](/personal-injury/premises-liability).

**Delivery vans and box trucks in the neighborhoods.** Package and parcel vans, appliance and furniture trucks, and garbage trucks work the subdivision streets and the mile roads. The crashes are backing crashes, crashes with vans stopped in the lane, and right-turn crashes that catch a cyclist or pedestrian. Delivery vans are often run by contracted delivery companies rather than by the retailer on the package. The van’s operator, its insurer, and the contract between them have to be identified. See our [pedestrian accident page](/personal-injury/pedestrian-accidents) and our [bicycle accident page](/personal-injury/bicycle-accidents).

**Lost and spilled loads.** Cargo, equipment, or debris that falls from a truck is a violation of the federal cargo securement rules (49 CFR 393.100 and following) and of Michigan law (MCL 257.720). The carrier and the company that loaded the truck can both be responsible.

**Whose road it is.** Taylor is a city, so the answer has three parts:

- **MDOT** owns I-75, I-94, and Telegraph. A road-defect claim against the State goes to the Court of Claims, with written notice filed there within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- Wayne County owns Eureka, Allen, and other mile roads.
- **The City of Taylor** owns the subdivision streets.

A claim against Wayne County or the City requires written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1402, 691.1404).

## Who is responsible, and who pays

**The carrier, not just the driver.** The motor carrier is responsible for its drivers’ compliance with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations. It is liable for a driver’s negligence in the course of employment. Michigan applies the federal rules to intrastate carriers (MCL 480.11a), so a local hauler that never leaves Wayne County is held to the same standards as an interstate carrier. These are the rules that decide truck cases most often:

| Rule | 49 CFR | What it means for the case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hours of service | Part 395 | 11 hours driving in a 14-hour window after 10 hours off; 60/70-hour weekly limits; recorded on an electronic logging device. Some short-haul drivers are exempt from logs but not from the limits. |
| Driver qualification | Part 391 | CDL, medical certificate, driving record, background check. Negligent hiring and retention are claims against the carrier. |
| Post-crash testing | Part 382 | Alcohol within 8 hours and drugs within 32 hours after a fatal crash, or after an injury or tow-away crash with a citation. |
| Inspection and maintenance | Part 396 | Pre-trip inspections, defect reports, maintenance records. Brake, tire, lift-axle, lighting, and reflective-tape cases are maintenance cases. |
| Cargo securement | 393.100–393.136 | Loads must be contained or secured so they cannot shift or fall. A spilled load is evidence of a violation. |
| Equipment: rear impact guards, lighting, reflectors | Part 393 | Underride guards (393.86) and conspicuity marking. A trailer that cannot be seen at night or lacks a working guard is a defective trailer. |
| Handheld phone ban; hazardous conditions | 392.82, 392.14 | No handheld phone use. Reduce speed or stop in snow, ice, and fog. |
| Minimum insurance | Part 387 | $750,000 general freight; $1 million oil; $5 million hazardous materials. |

**The owner of the truck or trailer.** In Michigan, a vehicle’s owner is liable for its negligent operation by someone driving with the owner’s consent (MCL 257.401). In freight and in airport and rail drayage, the tractor, the trailer, and the driver often belong to different companies, and each may be a defendant, although a company in the business of renting or leasing vehicles is generally shielded from owner liability by federal law (49 USC 30106).

**Michigan no-fault still applies to you.** Your own auto insurer pays these benefits regardless of fault (MCL 500.3107):

- your medical bills
- 85 percent of lost wages, up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year
- replacement services
- attendant care

Written notice is due to your insurer within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). The Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays if no household policy applies (MCL 500.3172).

Your claim against the driver and the carrier covers pain and suffering and losses beyond no-fault. It requires a threshold injury (MCL 500.3135) and must be filed within three years (MCL 600.5805(2)).

Michigan’s comparative fault rule is modified, not pure. If you were more than 50 percent at fault, you cannot recover pain-and-suffering damages, and below that your share reduces them (MCL 500.3135(2)(b); MCL 600.2959). The difference from a car case is the money available: the carrier’s policy of $750,000 or more, often with excess coverage above it. See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

**Other defendants.**

- the shipper or broker that loaded the truck or hired an unsafe carrier
- the store, warehouse, or terminal whose dock or yard operations contributed
- the maintenance contractor
- the manufacturer of a failed component or a defective guard ([product liability](/personal-injury/product-liability))
- for a government truck (MDOT, Wayne County, the City of Taylor, SMART, or a school bus), the agency, under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405)

**If you were the truck driver.** Workers’ compensation pays your benefits, and your employer usually cannot be sued (MCL 418.131). If you were injured in your employer’s truck, no-fault benefits generally come from the insurer of that truck (MCL 500.3114(3)), coordinated with comp, which is paid first (MCL 500.3109(1)). You can also sue a third party, such as another driver or a business whose dock or yard caused the crash. See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

## The evidence, and how fast it disappears

The federal record-keeping rules run out fast:

- Carriers must keep hours-of-service records for six months (49 CFR 395.8).
- They must keep driver inspection reports for three months (49 CFR 396.11).

After that, the regulations no longer require them. Other evidence goes faster still:

- Dock and yard cameras, business cameras along Telegraph and Eureka, MDOT freeway cameras, and dash cameras overwrite in days to weeks.
- The truck’s engine control module and event data recorder can be cleared when it is repaired and returned to service.
- A spilled load is swept up within hours.
- A damaged rear guard can be replaced before anyone measures it.

The first act in a Taylor truck case is a **preservation letter**. It goes to the carrier, its insurer, the tractor and trailer owners, the shipper or broker, and, where the crash happened at a dock or yard, the property owner and tenant. It demands:

- the ELD and engine data, and dash camera footage
- the driver’s qualification file and post-crash test results
- dispatch records, bills of lading, and dock appointment logs
- maintenance files
- the truck and trailer themselves, for inspection of the brakes, lift axles, load securement, and rear guard

Once a party knows a claim is coming, destroying that evidence exposes it to spoliation sanctions.

## What to do after a Taylor truck crash

1. **Call 911 and get medical care the same day.** Corewell Health Taylor, on Telegraph, has an emergency department. The most serious injuries often go to Corewell Health Dearborn or Corewell Health Trenton (Level II), or the Level I trauma centers in Detroit, Detroit Receiving Hospital and Henry Ford Hospital.
2. **Photograph the truck.** Get the cab and trailer, the USDOT number and carrier name on the door, both plates, the load and anything that fell from it, the rear guard and reflective tape, and the dock or service drive if the truck was entering or leaving one.
3. **Get the police report.** Crashes on city streets are worked by the Taylor Police Department. Freeway crashes may be worked by the Michigan State Police, and a serious crash may produce a separate commercial vehicle inspection report from the State Police.
4. **Do not speak to the carrier’s insurer or its investigator.**
5. **Report to your own insurer and file the Application for No-Fault Benefits** within the first week.
6. **Call a lawyer within days,** so the preservation letter goes out before the records expire and the truck is repaired.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many truck accidents happen in Taylor?**  **[The State’s county profile does not break out truck crashes by city. Countywide, Wayne County had 3,916 heavy trucks and buses involved in crashes in 2024, nearly twice Oakland County’s 2,081**. Statewide, 15,888 crashes involved a heavy truck or bus.

**A semi turned right on Telegraph and crushed the side of my car. Whose fault is that?** Usually the truck driver’s. A driver who swings wide to make a right turn must do so safely and must yield to traffic beside the truck.

**A box truck was backing into a store’s dock from Eureka and I hit it. Is that my fault?** Not necessarily. A truck backing across a travel lane must do so safely. Where the store’s dock layout routinely puts trucks in the road, the property owner or tenant may share fault.

**A delivery van hit me. Who is responsible?** The van’s driver and the company that employs the driver. That is often a contracted delivery company rather than the retailer whose name is on the package, and that company’s commercial auto policy is usually the coverage. Get the van’s company name and plate.

**My car went under the back of a trailer on I-75. Is that the truck’s fault?** It may be, partly or wholly. Trailers must carry rear impact guards built to federal standards. A guard that is missing, bent, or too high to stop a car is evidence against the carrier and the trailer’s owner. The trailer has to be inspected before it is repaired.

**I was partly at fault. Can I still recover?** Yes, if you were 50 percent at fault or less. Michigan’s rule is modified comparative fault, not pure: your recovery is reduced by your share, and above 50 percent you lose the claim for pain and suffering. No-fault benefits are paid regardless.

**I drive a truck and was hurt in a crash in Taylor. What are my claims?** Workers’ compensation through your employer, no-fault benefits coordinated with comp, and a lawsuit against any third party who caused the crash.

**Why does the lawyer need to act within days?** Because the records expire. Hours-of-service logs must be kept for six months and inspection reports for three. Camera footage lasts days or weeks, and the truck’s electronic data and the trailer’s damage can disappear on repair.
