## Social Security Disability Appeal Lawyer Michigan

If you applied for Social Security disability in Michigan and were denied, you are in the majority. In fiscal year 2025, the Social Security Administration approved about 36 percent of initial applications nationally and denied the rest. At the next step, reconsideration, it approved about 16 percent. Then, at the hearing before an administrative law judge, the approval rate rose to about half, and in Michigan’s seven hearing offices it ran higher than that over the past year. The system is built so that the people who persist and prepare win, and the people who stop at the first denial do not.

The denial letter in your hand gives you 60 days to appeal. That deadline governs everything on this page. What you do inside it, which appeal you file, what evidence you add, and how you prepare for the hearing, determines whether you receive benefits and how many months of back pay come with them.

## The four levels of appeal, and the deadline at each

Every denial notice from Social Security tells you which level you are at and how to appeal. The deadline is the same at every level: **60 days from the date you receive the notice**, and Social Security presumes you received it five days after the date printed on it unless you can show otherwise. Missing the deadline usually means starting over with a new application and losing the months of back pay the earlier application protected.

| Level | What it is | Deadline | Form | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Reconsideration** | A fresh review of the file by a different examiner and medical consultant at Michigan’s Disability Determination Service, the state agency that decides claims for SSA | 60 days from receipt of the initial denial | SSA-561, with SSA-3441 (disability report, appeal) and SSA-827 (medical release) | Paper review; no hearing; about 16 percent approved nationally; a chance to add new medical evidence. Michigan eliminated this step from 1999 to 2019 as a test state and reinstated it in October 2019 |
| **2. Hearing before an administrative law judge** | Your first chance to appear before a decision-maker, testify, present witnesses, and have a lawyer question the experts | 60 days from receipt of the reconsideration denial | HA-501 | Held at one of Michigan’s seven hearing offices, by video, or by phone; a vocational expert and sometimes a medical expert testify; about half of decisions nationally are favorable; the wait in Michigan is about seven to eight months |
| **3. Appeals Council** | Review of the judge’s decision for legal error, lack of substantial evidence, or abuse of discretion | 60 days from receipt of the judge’s decision | HA-520 | Paper review in Falls Church, Virginia; most requests are denied; about 15 percent of outcomes are remands back to a judge for a new hearing; the Council can also review a favorable decision on its own motion |
| **4. Federal court** | A civil action in the United States District Court for the Eastern or Western District of Michigan | 60 days from receipt of the Appeals Council’s notice | Complaint filed by an attorney | The court reviews the record for legal error and substantial evidence; it almost never awards benefits directly, but about two-thirds of decisions are remands for a new hearing |

**If you missed the deadline.** Social Security may extend it for **good cause** under 20 CFR 404.911: serious illness, a death in the family, records destroyed by fire, not receiving the notice, a misunderstanding caused by SSA’s own information, or limited English or education that prevented you from understanding what was required. The request must be in writing and explain the reason. Good cause is not automatic, and the alternative, a new application, forfeits the earlier protective filing date. Call before you refile.

**Reopening.** A prior denied application can sometimes be reopened within 12 months for any reason, or within four years for good cause (two years for SSI), under 20 CFR 404.988 and 416.1488. Reopening may preserve an earlier application period and potential past-due benefits, but it is discretionary and is not guaranteed.

## The five-step test, and where claims are lost

Every disability decision, from the state examiner to the federal judge, follows the same five steps set out in 20 CFR 404.1520. Understanding where your claim was denied tells you what the appeal has to fix. The Listings are covered on our [conditions that qualify](about:blank) page, and the evidence that wins at each step on our [how to win your disability claim](about:blank) page.

| Step | The question | How claims are lost here | What fixes it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Are you working?** | Are you performing “substantial gainful activity,” which in 2026 means earning more than SSA’s monthly threshold (adjusted annually)? | Working part-time above the threshold | Reduce hours below the threshold or show the work was an unsuccessful work attempt or subsidized |
| **2. Is your impairment severe?** | Does a medically determinable impairment significantly limit basic work activities, and has it lasted or will it last 12 months or result in death? | Thin medical records; a diagnosis without documented functional limits; no 12-month duration | Treatment records, objective testing, and a longitudinal history |
| **3. Does it meet or equal a Listing?** | Does the impairment meet the specific criteria of SSA’s Listing of Impairments (20 CFR Part 404, Subpart P, Appendix 1), the “Blue Book”? | The records do not document every element of the listing, even when the condition is plainly severe | A physician’s statement addressing each listing criterion with supporting test results |
| **4. Can you do your past work?** | Given your residual functional capacity (RFC), what you can still do despite your limitations, can you perform any job you held in the past five years? | SSA’s RFC overstates what you can do; your past work is described too generally | Detailed function-by-function medical opinions; accurate description of past job demands |
| **5. Can you do any other work?** | Considering RFC, age, education, and work experience, is there other work in the national economy you can do? | The vocational expert names jobs you can supposedly perform; the “grid rules” are misapplied | Cross-examination of the vocational expert; correct application of the grid rules, which favor claimants 50 and older limited to sedentary or light work |

Two rule changes matter for appeals filed now. Since June 2024, SSA looks back only **five years** for past relevant work at step four, not fifteen, and disregards jobs that lasted less than 30 days, under 20 CFR 404.1560 and 404.1565 as amended. And for claims filed after March 27, 2017, SSA no longer gives controlling weight to a treating physician’s opinion; under 20 CFR 404.1520c every medical opinion is weighed for its supportability and consistency, which means an opinion from your own doctor must be backed by the records and explain itself.

## What the medical evidence has to show

Claims are won on records, and a denial at reconsideration is most often a records problem rather than a merits problem.

- **A longitudinal treatment history** from the alleged onset date forward. Gaps in treatment are read against you. If cost or lack of insurance caused a gap, that must be explained and documented; SSR 16-3p requires the judge to consider the reasons.
- **Objective findings**: imaging, laboratory results, pulmonary function tests, cardiac testing, neuropsychological testing, and physical examination findings that correspond to the limitations you describe.
- **Function-by-function opinions** from treating sources, physical or mental, that state what you can do in an eight-hour workday: how long you can sit, stand, and walk; how much you can lift; how often you would be off task or absent; whether you can tolerate ordinary workplace stress. A conclusory letter that you are “disabled” carries little weight; a detailed, supported RFC statement carries a great deal.
- **Mental health records**, where applicable, with documented symptoms, treatment response, and the four areas of mental functioning the Listings use.
- **Consistency.** Your statements to your doctors, your function reports to SSA, and your testimony at the hearing should describe the same person. Judges read all three.
- **The five-day rule.** Under 20 CFR 404.935, evidence must be submitted or identified at least five business days before the hearing, or the judge may refuse it. A lawyer’s job in the months before the hearing is to obtain and submit everything by that date.

Social Security has a duty to develop the record, including ordering a consultative examination at its expense under 20 CFR 404.1519a when the evidence is insufficient, but a consultative examiner who sees you once for twenty minutes rarely helps a claim; your own records do.

## What happens at the hearing

The hearing is informal by courtroom standards, usually lasts 45 minutes to an hour, and is recorded. In Michigan it may be held in person, by telephone, by video at an SSA facility, or by online video. You may object in writing to telephone or agency-video appearances within 30 days after receiving SSA’s notice of hearing options. If you timely object to both, SSA generally schedules an in-person hearing, subject to limited exceptions. Online video requires your agreement. Present are the judge, a hearing reporter, you, your representative, and, by phone or video, a vocational expert and sometimes a medical expert.

The judge questions you about your work history, your medical conditions, your daily activities, and why you cannot work. Your representative questions you to bring out the limitations the records support. The vocational expert then testifies, in response to hypothetical questions from the judge describing a person with certain limitations, whether jobs exist that such a person could do. **The cross-examination of the vocational expert is often where the case is won**: a representative who can show that the judge’s hypothetical omitted a documented limitation, that the jobs named are obsolete or inconsistent with the Dictionary of Occupational Titles, or that an additional limitation (being off task 15 percent of the day, missing two days a month) would eliminate all work, has changed the outcome.

Decisions are mailed, usually one to three months after the hearing. A **fully favorable** decision awards benefits from your alleged onset date; a **partially favorable** decision finds you disabled from a later date and may be worth appealing in itself; an **unfavorable** decision starts the 60-day Appeals Council clock.

## Back pay, and why the appeal preserves it

Benefits are paid from your **established onset date**, subject to a five-month waiting period for SSDI, and SSDI back pay can reach back up to **12 months before your application date**. Because the appeal keeps your original application alive, a claimant denied in 2025 who wins at a hearing in 2027 is paid for the whole period. A claimant who gave up and refiled in 2026 lost a year or more. Back pay is paid as a lump sum for SSDI and, for SSI, in up to three installments. Medicare eligibility begins 24 months after SSDI entitlement, which the back-pay period counts toward.

## What to do today if you were denied

1. **Find the date on the denial notice** and count 65 days. That is your realistic deadline, and the appeal should be filed well before it.
2. **Do not file a new application** unless a lawyer has told you to. Appealing preserves your filing date and your back pay; reapplying usually does not.
3. **File the appeal online** at ssa.gov/apply/appeal or on paper, and keep the confirmation. A lawyer can file it for you once you have signed the appointment form, SSA-1696.
4. **Request your file.** You are entitled to the complete claims file, including the examiner’s explanation and the consulting physicians’ assessments, which show exactly why you were denied.
5. **Keep treating, and tell your doctors about every limitation.** The records created between now and the hearing are the ones the judge reads most carefully.
6. **Write down your work history for the past five years**, job by job, with the physical and mental demands of each.
7. **Call a lawyer before the reconsideration appeal is filed, not after it is denied.** The evidence added at reconsideration is in the file for the hearing, and the hearing request is where the wait begins.

## Frequently asked questions about disability appeals in Michigan

**I was denied. Should I appeal or reapply?** Appeal, in nearly every case. The appeal keeps your original filing date, which protects your back pay, and the approval rate rises sharply at the hearing level. Reapplying starts the clock over and, if the facts have not changed, usually produces the same denial.

**How long do I have?** Sixty days from receiving the notice, and SSA presumes you received it five days after the date on it. If you missed it, a good-cause extension may be available under 20 CFR 404.911, but call immediately.

**How long will the whole process take in Michigan?** Reconsideration typically takes several months. After a hearing request, Michigan’s hearing offices have averaged about seven to eight months to a decision, somewhat faster than the national average. A decision then takes one to three months to arrive. From initial application to a hearing decision, two years is common.

**What are my chances at the hearing?** Nationally about half of judge decisions are favorable; in Michigan’s offices the rate over the past year ran from the low fifties to the mid-sixties depending on the office. Your chances depend most on the medical evidence and on whether the vocational testimony is tested.

**Do I have to go to the hearing in person?** No. Michigan hearings are held in person, by video, or by phone. You may object to video or phone and request in person. We discuss which format suits your case.

**What is the difference between SSDI and SSI, and does it change the appeal?** SSDI is based on your work record; SSI is need-based. The appeal process and the five-step test are the same. The deadlines, forms, and fee rules are the same. Our [SSDI](about:blank) and [SSI](about:blank) pages explain eligibility for each.

**My doctor says I am disabled. Why was I denied?** Because since 2017 a doctor’s conclusion is not controlling; what matters is whether the records support specific functional limitations. A letter saying you are “disabled” is weighed for its supportability and consistency and often carries little weight on its own. A detailed opinion about what you can and cannot do in a workday, tied to the records, is what the judge needs.

**I am over 50. Does that help?** Often, yes. SSA’s medical-vocational guidelines, the grid rules, may direct a finding of disabled for certain claimants age 50 and older, depending on their residual functional capacity, education, work experience, and transferable skills. Age categories are applied at the time of the decision, and SSA may apply the next category in borderline situations.

**What does the lawyer cost?** Nothing unless you win, and then the lesser of 25 percent of your back pay or $9,200, paid by SSA from the back pay. There is no fee on your ongoing benefits. Record-retrieval costs are **[CONFIRM]**.

**Can I get my back pay if I win?** Yes. Back pay runs from your established onset date (subject to SSDI’s five-month waiting period) and can reach up to 12 months before your application date for SSDI. Winning on appeal rather than on a new application is what preserves it.
