## Troy, MI Medical Malpractice Lawyer

A medical malpractice claim in Michigan has rules no other injury claim has: a two-year deadline instead of three; a written Notice of Intent served on each provider at least 182 days before suit; a sworn Affidavit of Merit from a qualified expert filed with the complaint; strict rules on who that expert can be; and a cap on pain-and-suffering damages. A case that misses any one of them can be lost before its merits are heard. In Troy the first question is who the defendant is. The city’s hospital on Dequindre has changed names, several health systems run outpatient and specialty centers on Big Beaver and Livernois, and the medical office buildings around the hospital campus hold independent practices alongside health-system-employed physicians. Each has its own legal entity, insurer, and notice address, and a federally funded clinic is sued under federal law on a different clock in a different court.

## The rules, by the numbers

| Rule | Figure | Statute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deadline to file suit | 2 years from the act or omission | MCL 600.5805(8) |
| Discovery rule | 6 months from when the claim was or should have been discovered; never more than 6 years after the act (exceptions for retained objects, fraud, reproductive injury) | MCL 600.5838a(2) |
| Notice of Intent before suit | 182 days; the provider must respond in writing within 154 days | MCL 600.2912b |
| Affidavit of Merit | Filed with the complaint | MCL 600.2912d |
| Defendant’s Affidavit of Meritorious Defense | Within 91 days after the answer | MCL 600.2912e |
| Expert qualification | Same specialty; same board certification if the defendant is certified; majority of professional time in that specialty in the year before | MCL 600.2169 |
| 2026 cap on noneconomic damages | $596,400 (standard) / $1,065,000 (catastrophic) | MCL 600.1483 |
| Child injured before age 8 | Deadline runs to the 10th birthday | MCL 600.5851(7) |
| Federally funded provider | Administrative claim (SF-95) within 2 years; suit after denial or 6 months | 28 USC 2401(b), 2675 |

Economic damages, medical bills, lost earnings, and the cost of future care, are not capped. For a patient whose career has been cut short, the uncapped lost-earnings claim is often the larger part of the case.

## What malpractice is, and is not

A bad outcome is not malpractice. Malpractice is a bad outcome caused by a provider who failed to do what a reasonably competent provider in the same specialty would have done in the same circumstances (MCL 600.2912a). You must prove the standard of care, a breach, that the breach more likely than not caused the injury, and damages. Michigan requires proof that the patient would have had a better outcome; a lost chance of a better outcome is compensable only where that chance was greater than 50 percent (MCL 600.2912a(2)). A qualified physician’s written review of the records decides, before anything is filed, whether a case exists. Many do not, and we say so.

## Troy’s providers, and who answers for each

**Corewell Health Troy Hospital.** The hospital on Dequindre was Beaumont Hospital, Troy, and became Corewell Health Troy Hospital after the Beaumont and Spectrum Health merger that formed Corewell Health. **[VERIFY dates]** Many patients’ records still use the Beaumont name. For a claim, the name matters in one way: the correct legal entity for the Notice of Intent must be identified from current corporate filings, not from the sign or the letterhead on an older record. Claims against the hospital and its employed providers are generally filed in the Sixth Judicial Circuit Court in Pontiac.

**Emergency physicians and other contracted groups.** Many hospitals staff their emergency departments, anesthesia, and radiology through separate physician groups. The Notice of Intent must name the group as well as the hospital. Where a patient came to the hospital for care and reasonably believed the doctor worked for it, Michigan law can hold the hospital responsible under ostensible agency, the rule established in *Grewe v Mt Clemens General Hospital* (1978).

**Outpatient and specialty centers.** Several health systems operate outpatient medical centers, specialty clinics, imaging, and ambulatory surgery in Troy, including Henry Ford Health’s Troy medical center and a Children’s Hospital of Michigan specialty center. **[VERIFY: current Troy outpatient and specialty centers and their operating entities]** An outpatient center’s providers may be employed by the health system, by a separate physician group, or by a joint-venture entity, and each is a different defendant. A child seen at a Troy specialty center may have been treated by physicians employed by a different entity from the Detroit hospital whose name is on the building.

**Medical office buildings, surgery centers, and imaging centers.** The medical office buildings around the hospital campus and along Big Beaver and elsewhere in the city hold primary-care and specialty practices, and Troy has outpatient surgery, endoscopy, and imaging centers and urgent care clinics. A delayed diagnosis in an office practice and a surgical complication at an outpatient center are frequent claims, and the employer of each provider who touched the chart must be identified, because a health-system-employed physician and an independent physician in the same building are different defendants with different insurers. Michigan rules apply.

**The hospitals Troy patients use outside the city.** Corewell Health William Beaumont University Hospital in Royal Oak, a Level I trauma center, is in Oakland County, so claims there are also generally filed in the Sixth Circuit. Hospitals across Dequindre in Macomb County, and the Detroit hospitals, are in other circuits. Venue follows where the malpractice occurred and where the defendants are (MCL 600.1629); a case against providers in two counties can raise a choice of courts.

**Federally funded community health centers.** Health centers that receive federal Health Center Program funding can have their providers deemed federal employees for malpractice purposes. A claim against one follows the Federal Tort Claims Act, not Michigan procedure: an administrative claim on Standard Form 95 to the Department of Health and Human Services within two years (28 USC 2401(b)), no Notice of Intent, and suit in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan, tried to a judge without a jury. Identifying a deemed clinic early is the difference between a timely claim and none.

**Veterans.** Troy veterans treated at the John D. Dingell VA Medical Center in Detroit or a VA outpatient clinic have an FTCA claim on the same path, and may also qualify for disability compensation under 38 USC 1151 where the injury was caused by VA carelessness, negligence, or error, or by an event that was not reasonably foreseeable; see our [VA benefit claims page](about:blank).

**EMS.** Claims against EMS personnel, and against the ambulance service that employs them, public or private, are limited by the Emergency Medical Services Act to gross negligence or willful misconduct (MCL 333.20965(1)). Troy’s ambulance service has been provided by Star EMS, a private company, since early 2026.

**Nursing homes and assisted living.** Troy has two Medicare-certified nursing homes and several assisted living and memory care communities, and their claims are sometimes malpractice and sometimes ordinary negligence; which one it is decides whether the Notice of Intent, the two-year period, and the cap apply. A nurse’s wound-care decision is malpractice; leaving a resident unsupervised against the care plan is usually negligence. See our [nursing home abuse page](/personal-injury/nursing-home-abuse).

**Workplace clinics.** Occupational clinics and employer medical departments that treat injured workers are a separate case: a claim against a provider employed by your own employer may be barred by workers’ compensation exclusivity, while an outside clinic or physician can be sued for malpractice. See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

## How a Troy malpractice case proceeds

1. **The records**, including the electronic audit trail, from every provider, requested in the first week, under whatever name the provider used at the time of care.
2. **Expert review** by a physician in the defendant’s specialty, in writing, before anything is filed.
3. **Identify the defendants and the forum**: hospital entity, physician group, outpatient center operator, surgery center, or independent practice (Sixth Circuit for Oakland County care); deemed clinic or VA (SF-95 and the federal court); public EMS (governmental immunity). Serving a Notice of Intent on a federal provider while the federal two-year clock runs can lose the case.
4. **The Notice of Intent** (MCL 600.2912b), served at least 182 days before suit. A timely notice pauses the two-year period during the notice period (MCL 600.5856(c)). If the provider does not respond within 154 days, suit may be filed then.
5. **The complaint and Affidavit of Merit** (MCL 600.2912d); the defense then files its own affidavit (MCL 600.2912e).
6. **Discovery, case evaluation, facilitation**, and, for cases that do not settle, trial to a jury in Pontiac. Two to four years is typical.
7. **Resolution**, with minors’ and estates’ settlements approved by the court and Medicare, Medicaid, and health plan liens resolved before distribution.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long do I have to sue a Troy doctor or hospital?** Two years from the malpractice, with a six-month discovery rule capped at six years. Because the Notice of Intent must be served 182 days before suit and the expert review comes first, call well inside the first year.

**My records say Beaumont Troy. Does the name change affect my claim?** Not the claim itself, but it affects who is named. The Notice of Intent must go to the legal entity responsible for the care, identified from current filings rather than the name on the record.

**My child was treated at a specialty center in Troy with a Detroit hospital’s name on it. Who is the defendant?** The entity that employed the physicians and staff who treated your child, which may not be the hospital whose name is on the building. That is identified from the records and corporate filings before the Notice of Intent goes out.

**My doctor’s office is in a medical building next to the hospital. Is the hospital responsible?** Not necessarily. Many physicians in those buildings are independent or belong to separate groups. The physician’s employer must be identified, and the hospital is responsible only for its own employees and, in some emergency settings, under ostensible agency.

**My care was at a community health center. Is that different?** It may be. If the center is federally funded and its providers are deemed federal employees, the claim is against the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act: an administrative claim within two years, no Notice of Intent, and suit in federal court without a jury. We check the center’s status at the first call.

**Is there a cap on what I can recover?** On pain-and-suffering damages only: $596,400 in the standard case and $1,065,000 for the catastrophic permanent injuries the statute lists, in 2026, adjusted each year. Medical bills, lost earnings, and future care are not capped.

**My child was injured at birth. How long do we have?** A child injured before age eight has until the tenth birthday to file. The parents’ own claims are subject to the ordinary two-year period, so the family should not wait.
