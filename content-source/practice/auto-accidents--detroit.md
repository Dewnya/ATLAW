## Detroit Car Accident Lawyer

A car crash in Detroit is a Michigan no-fault claim first and a Detroit claim second. The no-fault part is the same everywhere in the state: an insurer determined under Michigan’s no-fault priority rules may pay covered medical expenses and wage-loss benefits regardless of fault. A claim for pain and suffering against the at-fault driver generally requires an injury meeting Michigan’s statutory threshold. The Detroit part is what this page is about. The driver who hit you may be uninsured or carry only the minimum liability coverage. The vehicle is more likely to belong to the City: a DDOT bus, a police car, a Public Works truck. The crash is more likely to involve a pedestrian; Detroit accounted for 420 of Wayne County’s 637 pedestrian-involved crashes in 2024. The road may be the City’s, the County’s, or the State’s, and the notice goes to a different office for each. And the case will be in the Third Circuit or the 36th District, which run differently from the suburban courts.

## Who pays after a Detroit car crash

A Detroit car crash may involve two separate claims: a no-fault benefits claim and a claim against a responsible driver.

**Your no-fault (PIP) claim, against your own insurer.** Under MCL 500.3107, an applicable no-fault insurer may pay covered medical expenses up to the available PIP medical limit, if medical coverage applies. Coverage depends on the injured person’s insurance priority, eligibility, available medical coverage, and statutory exclusions. Benefits also include 85 percent of eligible lost income for up to three years (capped at $7,455 per 30-day period for accidents occurring from October 1, 2026, through September 30, 2027), replacement services at $20 a day, and attendant care, regardless of who caused the crash. If you have no policy, the order is your spouse’s or a resident relative’s policy (MCL 500.3114); if none exists, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3172), which assigns an insurer and caps medical benefits at $250,000. The insurer must receive **written notice within one year** of the crash (MCL 500.3145(1)) and must pay each bill within 30 days of reasonable proof or owe 12 percent interest (MCL 500.3142). Our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance) covers the application, the benefits, and the denials.

**Your claim against the at-fault driver.** For pain and suffering, you generally must establish death, serious impairment of body function, or permanent serious disfigurement under MCL 500.3135. Certain economic losses beyond available no-fault benefits may be recoverable separately under MCL 500.3135(3)(c), without the same injury threshold. “Serious impairment” means an objectively manifested impairment of an important body function that affects your general ability to lead your normal life, as the Michigan Supreme Court defined it in *McCormick v Carrier* (2010) and the Legislature codified in 2019; it does not require permanence. The deadline is three years (MCL 600.5805(2)). Noneconomic damages are reduced by your share of fault and generally barred if you are found more than 50 percent at fault. Economic damages require a separate comparative-fault analysis. For vehicle damage, the **mini tort** under 3135(3)(e) recovers up to $3,000 from the at-fault driver for damage your own collision coverage does not pay; it is filed in the 36th District Court if the driver’s insurer will not pay it.

**Where the two claims meet.** Under Michigan’s reformed no-fault law, available PIP medical options include unlimited coverage, $500,000, $250,000, $250,000 with qualifying exclusions, $50,000 for eligible Medicaid households, and medical PIP opt-out for qualifying Medicare households. A Detroit driver who chose $50,000 or $250,000 to afford the policy can exhaust it in a single hospitalization. Qualifying medical expenses above an applicable PIP medical limit may be recoverable from a legally responsible at-fault driver as excess economic damages under MCL 500.3135(3)(c). **[ATTORNEY CHECK: excess allowable expenses under 3135(3)(c)]**

## What is different about a Detroit car accident claim

**The other driver is more likely to be uninsured.** Michigan’s 2019 no-fault reform was driven largely by Detroit’s premiums and its uninsured rate; before the reform, state regulators estimated that about 60 percent of Detroit drivers were driving without insurance. Michigan’s 2019 no-fault reform introduced lower-cost coverage options and was followed by a statewide decrease in uninsured driving. In Detroit, identifying available liability, PIP, and UM/UIM coverage remains especially important. **[CONFIRM: DIFS figure and wording]** For your claim this means three things. An injured person without an applicable PIP insurer may need to seek benefits through the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan. Michigan permits drivers to select bodily-injury liability limits as low as $50,000 per person and $100,000 per accident, rather than the default $250,000/$500,000 limits. And your own **uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage** is more often the real recovery; we read every policy in the household before we value a Detroit claim. See our [uninsured motorist page](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).

**Hit-and-run crashes are more common.** When the other driver leaves the scene, identifying available coverage can be difficult. An eligible injured person may still pursue applicable no-fault benefits, subject to Michigan’s priority rules and exclusions. Your injury claim depends on your uninsured motorist coverage, and UM policies carry their own conditions: some policies require prompt police reporting, written notice within a specified period, or evidence of physical contact with the unidentified vehicle. Report the crash to Detroit Police the same day, get the report number, and notify your insurer in writing that week. Whether your policy requires contact, and what its notice period is, depends on the policy, not on a statute. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

**The other vehicle may belong to the City.** DDOT buses (and SMART buses on the suburban routes into downtown), Detroit Police, Fire, and EMS vehicles, Department of Public Works trucks, and Detroit Water and Sewerage vehicles. A crash involving a government-owned vehicle may fall under the Governmental Tort Liability Act’s motor vehicle exception, MCL 691.1405, when the statutory requirements are met. Available DDOT video, police camera footage, dispatch records, and Project Green Light recordings should be identified and preservation requested promptly. Access procedures and retention periods vary by the record and the entity holding it. **The road itself may be the defendant, and the notice goes to a different office for each road.** A crash caused by a qualifying defect in the traveled roadway may implicate the highway exception to governmental immunity. Missing signs, malfunctioning signals, lighting, and roadway design require separate legal analysis. On MDOT-maintained freeways and state trunklines, a qualifying highway-defect claim generally requires written notice within 120 days, filed in the Court of Claims under MCL 691.1404. Some Detroit roads remain under Wayne County jurisdiction. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: road jurisdiction within Detroit; which roads are county]** The highway exception covers the traveled portion of the roadbed, not the surrounding area, and the notice must state the exact location, the nature of the defect, the injury, and the known witnesses. Notice to the wrong agency is not notice. On Belle Isle, a state park since 2014, road and premises claims are against the Michigan Department of Natural Resources in the Court of Claims.

**The other driver may be from Ontario or out of state.** The Ambassador Bridge, the Detroit-Windsor Tunnel, and I-75 and I-94 bring Ontario, Ohio, and other out-of-state drivers through the city daily. A nonresident generally cannot recover Michigan PIP benefits unless they own a motor vehicle registered and insured in Michigan (MCL 500.3113(c)).

**Rental cars and rideshare vehicles are common downtown and near the airport.** Coverage after a rental-car crash depends on applicable Michigan law, the renter’s insurance, the rental company’s statutory obligations, and the available policy terms. Michigan law requires at least $1 million in liability coverage during a prearranged rideshare trip, with different minimum coverage requirements while the driver is logged on and waiting for a request (MCL 257.2123). **The pedestrian cases.** Four hundred twenty pedestrian-involved crashes in the city in 2024. A pedestrian struck by a car is covered by no-fault through their own or a household policy first, then the insurer of the vehicle’s owner, then its operator, then the Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3115), and has the same threshold claim against the driver. Michigan’s comparative-fault rule applies to pedestrians, and fault may be disputed based on the crosswalk, signal phase, visibility, and other circumstances. ## What to do after a Detroit car crash

1. **Stop, call 911 if anyone is hurt, and call police.** If no officer responds, go to the precinct and file the report; do not let the crash go unreported.
2. **Photograph everything before the scene clears:** the vehicles, the plates, the other driver’s license and insurance card, the signals and signs, the road surface, and any camera on a nearby business or Green Light location.
3. **Get the names and numbers of witnesses.** In Detroit, witnesses leave quickly.
4. **Get medical care the same day** and tell the provider it was a car crash. A gap between the crash and the first visit is the insurer’s first argument.
5. **Report to your own insurer and request the Application for No-Fault Benefits.** File it within the first week, not the eleventh month.
6. **Read your declarations page for PIP level, UM/UIM, and collision coverage.** In Detroit this is the step that decides what the claim is worth.
7. **Do not give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurer**, sign a medical authorization, or sign a release.
8. **Stay off social media** about the crash and your activities.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many car accidents happen in Detroit each year?**  In 2024, Detroit recorded 24,321 traffic crashes, with 8,872 people injured and 104 killed.

**The driver who hit me had no insurance. Can I still recover anything?** Your no-fault benefits are unaffected: your own insurer, a household member’s, or the Assigned Claims Plan pays your medical bills and lost wages. Your pain-and-suffering claim depends on whether you carry uninsured motorist coverage; without it, the claim is worth only what the uninsured driver can personally pay, which in most cases is very little.

**The other driver left the scene. What now?** Report it to Detroit Police today and get the report number. Your no-fault claim proceeds as usual. Your injury claim is an uninsured motorist claim under your own policy, and the policy sets the conditions: prompt police report, written notice, and sometimes proof of contact. Call us before the notice period runs.

**I was hit by a DDOT bus. Is that handled like any other crash?** No. DDOT is a City of Detroit department, so the claim is against the City under the Governmental Tort Liability Act’s motor vehicle exception, with the Act’s procedures and defenses. The bus video is the central evidence in the case; request it immediately.

**Who is responsible for a pothole crash on a Detroit street?** The agency with jurisdiction over that road: the City for neighborhood streets, MDOT for the freeways and the trunkline arterials such as Woodward, Gratiot, Grand River, Michigan Avenue, and Telegraph, and Wayne County for some roads. Written notice is due within 120 days to the City or County and within six months to the State, and notice to the wrong agency does not count.

**Where would my Detroit car accident case be filed?** Injury suits over $25,000 go to the Third Judicial Circuit Court at the Coleman A. Young Municipal Center. Mini tort and other claims under $25,000 go to the 36th District Court on Madison Street.

**I chose a $250,000 PIP limit. What happens when my medical bills pass it?** Bills above your PIP limit become part of your claim against the at-fault driver as excess economic damages, in addition to pain and suffering. If the at-fault driver carries only the minimum, your own underinsured motorist coverage is the next source. Your health insurer may cover the excess and then assert a lien on your recovery.

**I was a passenger in a car with no insurance. Who pays my bills?** Your own policy if you have one, then a household member’s; if none, the Assigned Claims Plan, capped at $250,000 for medical benefits. Since 2019, uninsured passengers no longer claim from the vehicle owner’s insurer. See our [passenger injury page](/personal-injury/passenger-injury).

**How long do I have?** One year to give your no-fault insurer written notice, three years to sue the at-fault driver, 120 days for a notice to the City or a bar, and six months for a notice to the State.

**Do I need a lawyer for a Detroit fender-bender?** Not if no one was hurt and the insurer is paying. Call if anyone was injured, the other driver was uninsured or fled, a city vehicle was involved, the police report is wrong or missing, or an insurer has denied or delayed benefits.
