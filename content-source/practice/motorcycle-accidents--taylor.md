## Where Taylor motorcycle crashes happen

**The left turn on Telegraph and Eureka.** The recurring account in rider cases is a driver turning left across oncoming traffic who misjudges the speed of a motorcycle or does not see it at all. Under Michigan law, a driver must yield to oncoming traffic before turning left (MCL 257.650).

Telegraph’s signals at Van Born, Ecorse, Goddard, Northline, and Eureka produce that crash every day. So do Eureka’s driveways and signals between Telegraph and I-75. Drivers pulling out of a shopping-center driveway across the lanes produce the mirror image of the same crash. On a road this wide, a left-turning driver has to judge a gap across several lanes of oncoming traffic, and a motorcycle in the far lane is the easiest vehicle to miss.

**I-75, I-94, and the interchanges.** At the I-75 interchanges at Telegraph, Eureka, and Allen/Northline, and the I-94 interchanges at Ecorse, Telegraph, and Pelham, drivers merging and changing lanes for an exit often fail to check for a motorcycle in the next lane. Rear-end crashes in stop-and-go traffic throw a rider in a way a car would absorb. Tractor-trailers on both freeways add blind spots and wind blast. See our [Taylor truck accident page](/personal-injury/truck-accidents/taylor).

**The mile roads and subdivision streets.** On Goddard, Northline, Van Born, Ecorse, Pelham, Beech Daly, Allen, and Pardee, cars pull out from side streets and subdivision entrances. At uncontrolled subdivision intersections, a driver who rolls through a stop sign or fails to yield catches a rider crossing through.

**The road itself.** A single-vehicle crash caused by a pothole, a broken concrete joint, a pavement drop-off, or gravel is a claim against the agency with jurisdiction, under the highway exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1402). Taylor is a city, so there are three possible defendants:

- **MDOT** for Telegraph, I-75, and I-94: written notice filed with the Court of Claims within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **Wayne County for Eureka, Allen, and other mile roads**: written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **The City of Taylor** for the subdivision streets: written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).

A defect that was readily apparent to an ordinary observer for 30 days or more is conclusively presumed known to the agency (MCL 691.1403). Notice to the wrong agency is not notice. A defect that was readily apparent to an ordinary observer for 30 days or more is conclusively presumed known to the agency (MCL 691.1403).

**Bars and restaurants.** 31 percent of Michigan’s 2024 rider deaths involved alcohol. A bar that over-served the driver who hit a rider can be liable under the dram shop act. Written notice is due within 120 days after you hire a lawyer, and suit within two years (MCL 436.1801(3)). See our [drunk driving accident page](/personal-injury/drunk-driving-accidents).

## Who pays a Taylor rider’s medical bills

**If a car or truck was involved, you have no-fault benefits, but not from your motorcycle insurer.** Under MCL 500.3114(5), your Personal Injury Protection benefits come, in order, from:

1. the insurer of the owner or registrant of the motor vehicle involved
2. the insurer of that vehicle’s operator
3. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s operator
4. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s owner or registrant

Priority follows the vehicles’ insurers, not who was at fault. “Involved” does not require contact: a car that cut you off is involved even if it never touched you. If no insurer in that chain exists, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays (MCL 500.3172), with its $250,000 medical cap, unless you owned the motorcycle and it was uninsured (MCL 500.3113(b)).

The benefits are the ones a driver would get under MCL 500.3107:

- medical expenses, up to the PIP limit on the policy that pays
- 85 percent of lost wages for up to three years, up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year
- $20 a day in replacement services
- attendant care
- mileage

Written notice is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

The first medical limit is set by a policy you did not choose. Since the 2019 reform, the PIP medical level on the policy first in priority pays first; if the driver who hit you chose $250,000, that policy pays up to $250,000, and a lower-priority policy with more coverage, such as your own car policy, may then pay the rest.

**If no car or truck was involved, no-fault does not apply.** A single-vehicle crash on a pothole, a broken joint, or gravel produces no PIP claim. Your bills go to your health insurance and to any medical coverage you bought on the motorcycle policy. Your injury claim is against whoever caused the hazard.

**Your motorcycle.** Damage to the bike is covered by your own collision coverage, or by the mini tort claim against the at-fault driver for up to $3,000 (MCL 500.3135(3)(e)). If the insurer will not pay the mini tort claim, it is filed in the 23rd District Court in Taylor.

## Your claim against the driver who hit you

For pain and suffering, and for economic losses beyond no-fault, you can sue the at-fault driver if your injury meets the threshold: serious impairment of body function, permanent serious disfigurement, or death (MCL 500.3135). A serious impairment is an objectively manifested impairment of an important body function that affects your general ability to lead your normal life. It does not have to be permanent (*McCormick v Carrier*, 2010).

Motorcycle injuries clear that threshold more often than car injuries do. Common examples are fractures, road rash requiring grafts, torn ligaments, [brain injuries](/personal-injury/brain-injury), and [spinal cord injuries](/personal-injury/spinal-cord-injury). You also recover excess economic damages above the PIP limit that applied to you (MCL 500.3135(3)(c)).

You have three years to sue (MCL 600.5805(2)). A fatal crash is brought by the estate; see our [Taylor wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death).

**What the claim is worth depends on the other driver’s policy.** Many drivers carry the $50,000 opt-down minimum (MCL 500.3009(5)), and some carry nothing. The recovery then turns on your own **uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage**, which must be on the motorcycle policy. Many car policies exclude the insured while riding an owned motorcycle. See our [uninsured motorist page](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).

**Comparative fault is the fight.** Michigan’s rule is modified comparative fault:

- **More than 50 percent at fault:** you recover no noneconomic damages.
- **50 percent or less:** your award is reduced by your share. At exactly 50 percent, you recover half.

The rule is in MCL 500.3135(2)(b) and 600.2959. Economic damages beyond no-fault are reduced by your share as well. Insurers argue speed, lane position, lane splitting (illegal in Michigan), and “came out of nowhere.” The evidence that answers those arguments includes:

- the police report and witnesses
- the other driver’s phone records
- store and parking-lot cameras along Telegraph and Eureka
- the rider’s own camera

## The helmet question

Since 2012, Michigan has let riders 21 and older ride without a helmet if they meet two conditions (MCL 257.658):

- They carry at least $20,000 in first-party medical coverage.
- They have either held a motorcycle endorsement for two years or passed an approved safety course.

Riders under 21 must wear a helmet. If you met those conditions, riding without a helmet was legal, so it was not a traffic violation. In a head-injury case, the insurer will argue a helmet would have reduced the injury. That is a damages and causation argument for the jury, not a bar to the claim. In a leg or shoulder case, it is irrelevant.

## What to do after a Taylor motorcycle crash

1. **Get medical care the same day.** Corewell Health Taylor, on Telegraph, has an emergency department. The most serious injuries often go to Corewell Health Dearborn or Corewell Health Trenton (Level II), or the Level I trauma centers in Detroit, Detroit Receiving Hospital and Henry Ford Hospital.
2. **Get the police report.** Crashes in the city are worked by the Taylor Police Department, and freeway crashes may be worked by the State Police. If a car caused the crash without making contact, make sure the report says so. **[VERIFY: report request process]**
3. **Identify every vehicle involved,** including the one that never touched you.
4. **Photograph the bike, the scene, the road surface, your gear, and your injuries.** Do not repair or sell the bike.
5. **Read your motorcycle policy** for first-party medical, UM/UIM, and collision coverage, and check your car policy’s PIP level.
6. **Apply for no-fault benefits with the right insurer within the first week.** Do not give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurer.
7. **If a road defect was involved, call within days.** The 120-day notice is already running.

## Frequently asked questions

**A car turned left in front of me on Telegraph and I went down. Do I have a claim?** Yes. A driver turning left must yield to oncoming traffic. The car is “involved” under no-fault whether or not it touched you, so its owner’s insurer is first in line for your medical bills. Get the plate and the witnesses before the car is gone.

**Does the at-fault driver’s insurance pay my medical bills?** Not because the driver was at fault. Under the rider priority rule, the insurer of the car involved pays first, whoever caused the crash. Fault matters for your separate claim for pain and suffering.

**I hit a pothole on Goddard Road. Do I notify the City of Taylor?** Probably not. Taylor’s mile roads include Wayne County roads such as Eureka and Allen, so confirm who has jurisdiction over that stretch of Goddard before the notice goes out. Written notice is due within 120 days. It must state the exact location, the defect, the injury, and the witnesses. Subdivision streets belong to the City, with the same 120 days. Telegraph and the freeways belong to MDOT, with written notice filed with the Court of Claims within 120 days.

**Are motorcycles covered by no-fault?** A motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle” under the no-fault act, so a rider who crashes alone has no PIP claim. A rider hit by a car or truck does have one, paid first by that vehicle’s insurer under the priority rule.

**I wasn’t wearing a helmet. Can I still recover?** Yes, if you were 21 or older, carried the required $20,000 in medical coverage, and had either two years of endorsement or a safety course. The insurer can argue a helmet would have reduced a head injury, but that goes to damages, not to whether you have a claim.

**How long do I have?** - One year to notify the responsible no-fault insurer. - Three years to sue the at-fault driver. - Two years to sue a bar. - 120 days for a notice to Wayne County or the City. - 120 days for a road-defect notice against MDOT..
