## The two-level appeal, and how to handle it

ERISA’s claims regulation permits a plan to require **two levels** of mandatory appeal before suit, provided each level complies with the regulation’s deadlines and the claimant is told the rules. ERISA also permits voluntary review after the plan’s required appeal procedures have been exhausted. Under 29 CFR 2560.503-1(c)(3), a qualifying voluntary appeal cannot be required before filing suit, applicable limitations periods are tolled while the voluntary review is pending, and the plan must provide sufficient information for the claimant to make an informed decision. The plan may not charge fees or costs for participating in that voluntary process.: the plan may not require it before suit, the limitation period for suing must be tolled while a voluntary appeal is pending, the claimant must be told it is voluntary and what effect it has, and the plan may not use the claimant’s decision to skip it against the claimant later. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: 29 CFR 2560.503-1(c)(3)(iv)]**

Lincoln’s letters commonly offer a second review. The questions, in order:

1. **Is the second level mandatory or voluntary?** The denial letter and the plan document say. If mandatory, it must be completed and suit cannot be filed until it is; the record stays open through the second decision. If voluntary, suit may be filed after the first appeal is denied, and the second level is a choice.
2. **If voluntary, is it worth taking?** A voluntary second appeal is another chance to add evidence to the record before court, and the limitation period is tolled while it is pending. It also costs months. It is usually worth taking when there is significant evidence still to add, and not when the record is complete and the insurer’s position is fixed.
3. **What does the contractual limitation period say?** Lincoln’s policies, like most, set a period for filing suit measured from when proof of claim was due. Under *Heimeshoff*, it is enforceable. The period is calculated at the first denial, and the appeal strategy is built around it.

Whichever path is taken, the first appeal is built as if it were the only one: the complete record, the treating-physician responses, the vocational evidence, the procedural objections. A thin first appeal that relies on the second to fix it is a gamble on the insurer’s generosity.

## The ERISA rules Lincoln must follow

- The **denial letter** must state the specific reasons and plan provisions, describe what would perfect the claim, explain the appeal procedure including whether any further level is mandatory or voluntary, state the right to sue, and, under the 2018 amendments, explain why Lincoln disagrees with the treating physicians and with any Social Security award.
- **At least 180 days** to file the first appeal.
- **The complete claim file**, free of charge on request, including the peer reviews, the transferable skills analysis, the surveillance, and the internal guidelines relied on.
- **New evidence** developed during the appeal must be disclosed with time to respond before the appeal is decided.
- The appeal decided by **someone new**, consulting a health professional not involved in the initial denial, within **45 days** plus one 45-day extension at each mandatory level.
- Violations that are more than minor cost Lincoln its deference: deemed exhaustion and de novo review under 29 CFR 2560.503-1(l)(2).

## Building the appeal against Lincoln

The appeal is the trial. The federal judge reads the file as Lincoln closed it and, in most cases, asks only whether Lincoln’s decision was arbitrary and capricious.

**Proving the disability**

1. Obtain and read the complete claim file. Identify the peer reviewer and specialty, the occupational classification Lincoln applied, the transferable skills analysis and its assumed capacities, and every record Lincoln did not have.
2. Update the medical record through the appeal date.
3. Obtain treating-specialist opinions that answer the peer reviewer point by point and state function-by-function restrictions against the policy’s definition of disability, own occupation as generally performed or any occupation at the earnings threshold.
4. Add objective testing where the condition allows.
5. Obtain a vocational assessment addressing the occupation as the policy defines it and each occupation in the transferable skills analysis.
6. Include the Social Security decision and its evidence.
7. Add statements from family, former supervisors, and coworkers.

**Proving the process was unreasonable**

1. Document the reviewer’s specialty mismatch, the treating opinions ignored, and Lincoln’s unexercised right to examine.
2. Compare the denial letter to the record.
3. Document every procedural failure, including any failure to explain whether the second level was mandatory or voluntary.
4. Set out the structural conflict of interest under *Glenn*.
5. Write the argument tying the evidence to the policy language and the law.

## If the appeal is denied

Suit is filed under 29 USC 1132(a)(1)(B) in federal court, in Michigan the Eastern District (Detroit, Ann Arbor, Flint, Bay City) or Western District (Grand Rapids, Kalamazoo, Lansing, Marquette), on the administrative record. If the plan grants Lincoln discretion, the standard is arbitrary and capricious with the conflict of interest weighed under *Glenn*; if Lincoln forfeited deference through procedural violations, de novo. Remedies are the benefits owed with interest and, in the court’s discretion, attorney fees to a claimant who achieves some success on the merits. Remand to Lincoln for a new decision is a common outcome.

For Lincoln policies **not** governed by ERISA, individual policies and policies issued to governmental and church employers, Michigan law applies: a jury, discovery, contract damages, and 12 percent penalty interest under MCL 500.2006 on benefits not timely paid when the claim was not reasonably in dispute.

## The Social Security connection

Lincoln requires claimants to apply for Social Security disability, offsets the LTD benefit by the award (and by an estimate before the award, absent a reimbursement agreement), and demands repayment of the overlap when back pay arrives. The SSDI award is also evidence of disability that the 2018 ERISA rules require Lincoln to address in its denial letter; an insurer that required the application, took the offset, and disregarded the result has created the inconsistency courts weigh in finding arbitrariness. ## Frequently asked questions about Lincoln Financial disability denials

**My policy was with Liberty Mutual. Why is Lincoln handling my claim?** Lincoln Financial acquired Liberty Mutual’s group benefits business, Liberty Life Assurance Company of Boston, in 2018. Policies written by Liberty are now administered by Lincoln, and the appeal goes to the address on the denial letter.

**How long do I have to appeal a Lincoln denial?** At least 180 days from receipt of the first denial under ERISA. If Lincoln offers a second level, the letter states its deadline and whether it is mandatory or voluntary. Separately, the policy sets a deadline for filing suit, which is enforceable under *Heimeshoff*; calculate it when the first denial arrives.

**Lincoln’s letter says I “may request a second review.” Do I have to?** It depends on whether the second level is mandatory or voluntary, which the letter and the plan must say. If mandatory, yes, before suit. If voluntary, no; you may sue after the first appeal is denied, and the limitation period is tolled if you choose to take the voluntary level. The decision depends on what evidence remains to be added.

**Lincoln says my occupation is “sedentary as performed in the national economy.” I lifted boxes all day.** Lincoln defines own occupation by how the occupation is generally performed, classified from the Dictionary of Occupational Titles, not by your actual job. The appeal addresses the occupation’s material duties as generally performed and whether Lincoln’s classification fits the work you did; a vocational assessment is usually needed.

**The denial was based on a doctor who never saw me.** Lincoln relies on peer reviews by non-examining physicians. Courts in Michigan’s federal circuit have treated reliance on a file review to reject treating physicians, when the insurer had the right to examine the claimant and did not, as evidence of an arbitrary decision. The appeal makes that point with the record to support it.

**My benefits were terminated at 24 months.** Either the own-occupation period ended and Lincoln found you able to perform any occupation on a transferable skills analysis, or the mental illness limitation was applied. The letter says which; the appeal answers the specific finding.

**Lincoln reduced my benefit for Social Security I have not received.** Lincoln policies allow an estimated offset unless you sign a reimbursement agreement. Ask which alternative applies, and coordinate the SSDI application so the award, the offset, and the repayment demand are handled together.

**Can I get damages for what the denial did to my family?** Not under ERISA, which allows recovery of the benefits, interest, and in the court’s discretion attorney fees. Under a non-ERISA Lincoln policy governed by Michigan law, contract damages and 12 percent penalty interest are available.

**Will a jury decide my case?** Not if ERISA governs. A federal judge decides on Lincoln’s file. A jury is available only for individual, governmental, and church-plan policies under Michigan law.
