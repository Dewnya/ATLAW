## Pittsfield Township Slip and Fall Lawyer

For twenty years, a fall on someone else’s property in Michigan was close to unwinnable, because a hazard that was “open and obvious” ended the case before it began. That rule is gone. Since the Michigan Supreme Court’s July 2023 decision in *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil*, a fall case turns on four questions:

- who had the duty to keep the place safe
- what they knew about the hazard, and when
- what they did about it
- how much of the fall was your own fault

In Pittsfield Charter Township, the answer to the first question depends on where you fell:

- **Stores, restaurants, and their parking lots,** in the shopping centers and big-box stores along Carpenter Road, Ellsworth Road, Washtenaw Avenue, State Street, and Ann Arbor-Saline Road.
- **Apartment complexes, condominium associations, and landlords,** across a township with many multifamily communities.
- **Public sidewalks and pathways,** where the township has a statutory duty and its own ordinance puts a duty on the abutting owner as well.
- **Township buildings, schools, and parks,** with a 120-day notice and limits set by governmental immunity.
- **Hospitals, senior communities, workplaces, and homes,** each with its own rules.

## Where Pittsfield Township falls happen, and who is responsible

**Stores, restaurants, and their lots.** The township’s retail is concentrated in shopping centers and big-box stores with large parking lots, along Carpenter, Ellsworth, Washtenaw, State Street, and Ann Arbor-Saline Road. The falls are:

- spills, produce, and freshly mopped floors without signs
- meltwater and slush tracked inside the entrance in winter
- merchandise, pallets, or boxes in the aisle
- cart corrals, wheel stops, and curbs at the edge of the walk
- potholes, broken pavement, and unlit areas in the lots
- ice where plowed snow piles melt and refreeze across the drive lanes

A shopper or diner is an **invitee**, owed the full duty of care: the owner must inspect, fix what it finds, and warn of what it cannot fix. The case is decided on **notice**:

- **Actual notice:** an employee saw the hazard or caused it.
- **Constructive notice:** it existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it.

In a shopping center, the owner and its management company usually control the common walks and lot, the tenant controls its own floor, and a snow-removal or maintenance contractor may share the duty. The leases and service contracts decide who, so we request them with the footage.

**Apartment complexes and rentals.** Many township residents rent, most in apartment complexes with exterior stairways, breezeways, balconies, and large lots. Under MCL 554.139, a landlord must keep the premises and common areas fit for their intended use and in reasonable repair. The usual claims are:

- broken or loose stair treads and nosings on exterior and interior stairways
- loose or missing handrails and balcony guardrails
- dark stairwells, breezeways, walks, and parking areas
- heaved or broken walks between buildings
- ice on stairs, walks, and in the lot

A lot with an inch or two of snow is still “fit” for parking under *Allison v AEW Capital* (2008), so tenant snow cases depend on whether the accumulation actually prevented use. The general negligence claim after *Kandil-Elsayed* is a separate route. A lease clause waiving the landlord’s liability for its own negligence is void.

The township’s property maintenance code requires a rental to be registered and to hold a certificate of compliance before it is occupied (Code ch. 6, art. IV). Residential rentals are inspected every two years, and at any time after a complaint. The code adopts the International Property Maintenance Code, which covers stairways, porches, balconies, handrails, and guards. A failed inspection, a temporary certificate listing open violations, or a complaint about the stair or railing that gave way is strong evidence of notice. A guest of a tenant who falls on a broken stair or balcony may have a claim against the landlord as well as the tenant. See our [tenant injury page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/tenant-injuries).

**Condominiums.** In a condominium community, the association usually controls the common walks, stairs, drives, and lots, and contracts for plowing and salting. A fall on a common element is a claim against the association, and possibly its management company and snow contractor. The master deed and bylaws say which parts are common elements and which are the co-owner’s. A co-owner who falls on a common element is not owed the landlord duty in MCL 554.139, so the claim is an ordinary premises claim.

**Homes.** Duty depends on why you were there:

- **A social guest** at a friend’s or relative’s house is a **licensee**. The owner must warn of hidden dangers the owner knows about, but need not inspect for unknown ones.
- **A guest there for the owner’s business,** such as a paying customer, is owed more.

A fall at a friend’s house is usually paid by the homeowner’s insurance, not by the friend personally.

**Public sidewalks and pathways.** The township owns no roads, but it is the municipal corporation in which most of its sidewalks run, so it has the statutory duty to keep a sidewalk beside a public road in reasonable repair (MCL 691.1401(e), 691.1402a(1)). The Washtenaw County Road Commission owns the road, not the sidewalk. The rules:

- The township must have known, or should have known, of the defect at least 30 days before the fall (MCL 691.1402a(2)).
- A defect under two inches is presumed to be in reasonable repair unless the evidence shows otherwise, and the judge decides whether the presumption is rebutted (MCL 691.1402a(3), (4)).
- Written notice must be served within 120 days, stating the exact location, the defect, the injury, and the witnesses (MCL 691.1404). A notice sent to the Road Commission or to the wrong municipality does not protect the claim.



The township’s own code adds a second possible defendant. Under Code § 28-6, the owner of property next to a public sidewalk must maintain, repair, and keep it safe, in good repair, and free of dangerous ice, snow, or other obstructions, and “shall be responsible and liable” for injuries caused by its disrepair. Under § 28-9, an owner whose property causes a sidewalk hazard, through trees, drainage, construction, or traffic, must repair it and is liable for the damage. Michigan courts have generally held that a local ordinance does not by itself create a claim by a pedestrian against an abutting owner. Whether Pittsfield’s express liability language changes that is a question for the attorney in each case. An owner whose own trees, drainage, or construction damaged the walk is the stronger case either way.

Measure and photograph the defect with a ruler before it is patched. A walk inside a shopping center, an apartment complex, or a condominium belongs to its owner or association, and the claim is an ordinary premises claim.

**Township buildings, schools, and libraries.** The township, the school districts that serve it, and a district library are governmental agencies. A fall caused by a dangerous or defective condition of one of their buildings proceeds under the public building exception. That exception requires the agency to have known, or to have had reason to know, of the defect and to have failed to fix it within a reasonable time, with written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1406). The Township Hall and the Department of Public Safety buildings are township buildings.

**Parks and paths.** A fall on a path or trail in a township park, such as Lillie Park, or on a pathway away from a public road, has no comparable exception, and governmental immunity usually bars it.

**City property inside the township.** The Ann Arbor Municipal Airport, on the township’s west side, is owned by the City of Ann Arbor. A fall in one of its buildings is a public building claim against the City, with the 120-day notice going to the City, not the township.

**Hospitals, nursing homes, and senior communities.** The township has senior apartments, assisted living communities, and nursing homes, and its residents use Trinity Health Ann Arbor, which sits in Superior Township, and University of Michigan Health. A fall at a hospital, clinic, or care community may be:

- an ordinary premises claim, if the cause was a wet floor or a broken step
- medical malpractice, if the cause was a failure to assess or supervise a patient or resident at risk of falling

Which kind of claim it is decides the deadline and the procedure. A fall at a University of Michigan Health building or clinic is a claim against the University in the Court of Claims. See our [nursing home abuse page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/nursing-home-abuse) and [medical malpractice page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/medical-malpractice).

**Workplaces.** A delivery driver, contractor, or staffing-agency worker who falls at a warehouse, an office park, a store’s loading dock, or a construction site in the township is not that business’s employee. Their own employer’s workers’ compensation pays their benefits. The property owner or the general contractor that controlled the dock, the site, or the icy lot can be sued as a third party, and the comp insurer is repaid from that recovery (MCL 418.827). A business’s own employee generally cannot sue the employer (MCL 418.131). See our [workers’ compensation page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

## A parking-lot fall can be a no-fault claim

If you fell while getting into or out of your own car, or while loading it, Michigan’s no-fault law may cover your medical bills and lost wages even though no crash occurred. An injury from a parked vehicle is covered when the person was “occupying, entering into, or alighting from” it, or loading or unloading it (MCL 500.3106(1)).

- That claim runs on the no-fault notice rules: written notice to the auto insurer within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)).
- It sits alongside any premises claim against the lot’s owner.
- A worker hurt on the job while loading, unloading, or getting into or out of a parked vehicle is generally limited to workers’ compensation for that injury, with narrow exceptions (MCL 500.3106(2)).

In a township where most errands end in a large lot, this claim is easy to miss. See our [no-fault insurance claim page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

## The law after *Kandil-Elsayed*

Under the old rule (*Lugo v Ameritech*, 2001), an owner owed no duty to protect visitors from a danger an average person would have noticed, and insurers denied fall claims on that basis. In *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil* (Michigan Supreme Court, July 28, 2023), decided with *Pinsky v Kroger*, the Court changed that:

- Whether a hazard was open and obvious no longer decides whether a duty existed.
- It is now a factor in whether the owner breached the duty of reasonable care, and in how much of the fault belongs to the person who fell.
- Both are questions for a jury.

That does not make every fall a case. A visible hazard still matters. If the jury assigns you some fault, your damages are reduced by that share. Above half, you recover no pain-and-suffering damages, only reduced economic losses (MCL 600.2959).

Duty also depends on why you were there:

- **Invitee** (a shopper, diner, tenant in a common area, or member of the public in a business open to it): owed inspection and repair.
- **Licensee** (a social guest at a home): owed a warning of hidden dangers the owner knows about.
- **Trespasser:** owed protection from willful and wanton misconduct.

Treating every visitor the same overstates or understates the claim.

## Snow and ice

An owner must take reasonable steps, within a reasonable time after snow or ice accumulates, to make walkways, entrances, stairs, and lots safe. What is reasonable depends on the property:

- a store opening in the morning after an overnight storm is expected to have treated its entrance and the walk from the lot
- an apartment complex or condominium association, its stairs, breezeways, and walks before residents leave for work
- a lot, its drive lanes and the walks to the door

A contractor with a salting contract that missed the morning run may share the fault. The law does not require an owner to keep every surface bare at every moment of a storm.

Some ice is a condition the owner created, and those cases are stronger than natural accumulation:

- black ice from refreezing meltwater
- a leaking gutter or downspout over a walk or stair
- snow piled where it melts across the walk or a drive lane
- a drain in a lot that pools and freezes

**The township’s sidewalk snow rule.** Under Code § 28-10, the owner or occupant must clear the public sidewalk beside the property, and the walks and ramps to a crosswalk, within 24 hours after each accumulation of more than one inch, whether it fell or drifted. The walk must be cleared for its full width and length. If it is not, the township can give notice and, if the walk is still not cleared within 24 hours, remove the snow and bill the owner, with unpaid charges becoming a lien on the property. One notice covers the whole season.

A municipality’s duty to repair sidewalks generally does not extend to natural accumulations of snow and ice, and Michigan courts have generally held that a snow ordinance does not by itself make the owner liable to a pedestrian. Pittsfield’s ordinance goes further than many, because § 28-6 states that owners who fail to keep the walk free of dangerous ice and snow are liable. Whether that holds up is an open question. An owner whose property caused the ice, by drainage or plowing, can be liable under ordinary premises law. Photograph the ice, the path, the alternatives you had, and the weather the same day.

## What to do after a fall in Pittsfield Township

1. **Get medical care the same day** and tell the provider where and how you fell. Trinity Health Ann Arbor and University of Michigan Health are the nearest hospitals.
2. **Photograph the hazard immediately,** from several angles, with something for scale, before it is mopped, salted, repaired, or patched. Photograph your shoes.
3. **Report the fall** to the store or building management, the landlord or association, or the township, and get the incident report number. Do not give a recorded statement to any insurer or risk manager.
4. **Get witness names,** including employees, and note the time. It matters for constructive notice.
5. **Measure a sidewalk or step defect** with a ruler in the photograph.
6. **Write down the exact location,** the nearest address or cross street, and whether the walk was public or inside the complex or shopping center. An Ann Arbor, Ypsilanti, or Saline address does not settle which government it is.
7. **Keep the shoes** unworn and unwashed.
8. **Call a lawyer within days.** Footage may be overwritten in days, and a stair or walk may be repaired within the week. If the township, another municipality, a school district, or the State may be involved, a 120-day or six-month notice is already running.

## Frequently asked questions

**I slipped on ice in a store parking lot on Carpenter Road. Who is responsible?** Usually the store or the shopping center’s owner and management company, whichever controlled the lot, and possibly their snow-removal contractor. Ice from plowed piles melting and refreezing across a drive lane is a condition the owner created, which is a stronger case. Since 2023, an obvious hazard no longer ends the case. If you fell getting out of your car, you may also have a no-fault claim.

**My address says Ann Arbor. How do I know whether I fell in the township?** The township’s parcel map and the police or incident report usually show it: the township’s Department of Public Safety responds in the township, the Ann Arbor Police Department in the city. We confirm it from the exact location before any notice goes out.

**I tripped on a broken sidewalk in Pittsfield Township. Who do I notify?** The township, not the Road Commission, within 120 days, stating the exact location, the defect, the injury, and the witnesses. The township must have known or should have known of the defect at least 30 days before, and a defect under two inches is presumed to be in reasonable repair. The township’s ordinance also makes the abutting owner responsible for the walk, which may give you a second claim.

**I fell on the stairs at my apartment complex. Do I have a case against the landlord?** Possibly. A landlord must keep the premises and common areas fit for their use and in reasonable repair, and broken treads, loose railings, and dark stairwells are the usual claims. Your maintenance requests and the township’s rental inspection file are the evidence.

**I fell on an icy walk in my condominium community. Who is responsible?** Usually the association, which controls the common walks and contracts for snow removal, and possibly its management company and contractor. The master deed says whether the walk was a common element.

**I fell in Lillie Park. Can I sue the township?** Usually not. Governmental immunity bars most claims for falls on park paths and grounds outside a building. A fall caused by a defect inside a township building is different, with a 120-day notice.

**How long do I have?** Three years to sue a private owner; 120 days for written notice to the township, another municipality, or a school district; 120 days for a fall in a State or University of Michigan building; and one year to notify a no-fault insurer. The footage disappears far sooner.
