## Washtenaw County Truck Accident Lawyer

Washtenaw County sits where two freight corridors cross. I-94 carries trucks between Detroit and Chicago through Ypsilanti, the south side of Ann Arbor, and Chelsea; US-23 carries them between Toledo and Flint along Ann Arbor’s east side; and M-14 links Ann Arbor to Plymouth and I-275. Off the freeways, trucks serve the industrial areas around Ypsilanti Township and Willow Run, the commercial corridors on Washtenaw Avenue and Carpenter Road, and the construction that never stops around two universities and their hospitals, and on the rural roads west of US-23 they share two-lane roads with farm equipment. In 2024, according to the Michigan State Police, 755 trucks and buses were involved in crashes in Washtenaw County, three of them in fatal crashes and 111 in injury crashes. ## Where Washtenaw County truck crashes happen

**I-94.** The county’s main freight route, with interchanges from Rawsonville Road and Ypsilanti through US-23, State Street, Ann Arbor-Saline Road, Jackson Road, Baker Road, and on toward Chelsea. The crashes are rear-end and underride crashes in the backups at the interchanges and the work zones, lane-change crashes into a car in the blind spot on the right side of the cab, ramp and merge crashes, and fatigue crashes on overnight runs. Ramps can be deadly for trucks too: in March 2022 a semi entering eastbound I-94 from Baker Road in Scio Township overturned across the freeway and was struck by a box truck, and both drivers died. A car that strikes the side or rear of a trailer can go under it; trailers must carry rear impact guards that meet federal standards (49 CFR 393.86), and a missing, damaged, or substandard guard is evidence against the carrier and the trailer’s owner.

**US-23 and M-14.** US-23 carries north-south freight from Ohio past Ann Arbor toward Brighton and Flint, and the US-23/M-14 interchange north of Ann Arbor and the US-23/I-94 interchange south of it are where trucks and commuters merge at speed. Work zones on US-23 in recent years have added lane shifts and narrowed shoulders. A work-zone crash can involve MDOT’s contractor as well as the truck.

**Washtenaw Avenue, Carpenter Road, and the commercial corridors.** Delivery trucks and tractor-trailers serving the shopping centers and businesses on Washtenaw Avenue, Carpenter Road, Ellsworth, and Michigan Avenue turn wide across lanes into lots, back into loading areas from the road, and stop in travel lanes. The crashes are right-turn crashes that catch a car, cyclist, or pedestrian on the inside of the turn, and crashes with trucks stopped where drivers do not expect them.

**Campus and downtown deliveries.** Around the University of Michigan and in downtown Ann Arbor and Ypsilanti, box trucks and delivery vans double-park, back into alleys, and turn across crowded crosswalks. A delivery van operated by a contracted delivery company is a claim against that company and its commercial auto policy. See our [pedestrian accident page](about:blank) and [bicycle accident page](about:blank).

**Rural roads, farm equipment, and haulers.** West and south of Ann Arbor, gravel trucks, grain trucks, and farm tractors and implements use two-lane roads with no shoulders. A slow-moving tractor at night without required lighting and reflective markings, or a wide implement crossing the center line, is a negligence claim against the operator. A farm tractor or implement is generally not a “motor vehicle” under the no-fault act, which affects both who pays your benefits and whether the no-fault threshold applies to your claim against the farm operator.

**Lost and spilled loads.** Cargo, gravel, or equipment that falls from a truck is a violation of the federal cargo securement rules (49 CFR 393.100 and following) and of Michigan law (MCL 257.720), and the carrier and the company that loaded the truck can both be responsible.

**Whose road it is.** I-94, US-23, M-14, Michigan Avenue (US-12 and Business US-12), and Washtenaw Avenue (M-17 east of US-23; Business Loop I-94 and Business US-23 west of it) are MDOT’s; a road-defect claim against the State goes to the Court of Claims, with written notice filed there within 120 days (MCL 691.1404) The county roads, including nearly every road in the townships, are the Washtenaw County Road Commission’s, and the cities own their own streets, each with written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1402, 691.1404).

## Who is responsible, and who pays

**The carrier, not just the driver.** The motor carrier is responsible for its drivers’ compliance with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations and is liable for a driver’s negligence in the course of employment. Michigan applies the federal rules to intrastate carriers (MCL 480.11a), so a local hauler that never leaves Washtenaw County is held to the same standards as an interstate carrier. The rules that decide truck cases most often:

| Rule | 49 CFR | What it means for the case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hours of service | Part 395 | 11 hours driving in a 14-hour window after 10 off; 60/70-hour weekly limits; recorded on an electronic logging device. Some short-haul drivers are exempt from logs but not from the limits. |
| Driver qualification | Part 391 | CDL, medical certificate, driving record, background check; negligent hiring and retention are claims against the carrier. |
| Post-crash testing | Part 382 | Alcohol within 8 hours and drugs within 32 after a fatal crash, or an injury or tow-away crash with a citation. |
| Inspection and maintenance | Part 396 | Pre-trip inspections, defect reports, maintenance records; brake, tire, lighting, and reflective-tape cases are maintenance cases. |
| Cargo securement | 393.100–393.136 | Loads must be contained or secured so they cannot shift or fall. |
| Equipment: rear impact guards, lighting, reflectors | Part 393 | Underride guards (393.86) and conspicuity marking; a trailer that cannot be seen at night or lacks a working guard is a defective trailer. |
| Handheld phone ban; hazardous conditions | 392.82, 392.14 | No handheld phone use; reduce speed or stop in snow, ice, and fog. |
| Minimum insurance | Part 387 | $750,000 general freight; $1 million oil; $5 million hazardous materials. |



**The owner of the truck or trailer.** In Michigan, a vehicle’s owner is liable for its negligent operation by someone driving with the owner’s consent (MCL 257.401). When the tractor, the trailer, and the driver belong to different companies, which is common in freight, each may be a defendant, although a company in the business of renting or leasing vehicles is generally shielded from owner liability by federal law (49 USC 30106).

**Michigan no-fault still applies to you.** Your own auto insurer pays your medical bills, 85 percent of lost wages up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year, replacement services, and attendant care regardless of fault (MCL 500.3107), with written notice due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). The Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays if no household policy applies (MCL 500.3172). Your claim against the driver and the carrier for pain and suffering and losses beyond no-fault requires a threshold injury (MCL 500.3135) and must be filed within three years (MCL 600.5805(2)). If you were more than 50 percent at fault, you cannot recover pain-and-suffering damages, and below that your share reduces them (MCL 500.3135(2)(b)). The difference from a car case is the money available: the carrier’s $750,000-plus policy, often with excess coverage above it. See our [no-fault insurance claim page](about:blank).

**Other defendants.** The shipper or broker that loaded the truck or hired an unsafe carrier; the facility whose dock or gate operations put trucks on the road; the maintenance contractor; the manufacturer of a failed component or a defective guard ([product liability](about:blank)); and, for a government vehicle, the agency that owns it: MDOT, the Road Commission, a city, TheRide, or a school district under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405), and the University of Michigan or Eastern Michigan University in the Court of Claims, with notice within six months (MCL 600.6431).

**If you were the truck driver.** Workers’ compensation pays your benefits, and your employer usually cannot be sued (MCL 418.131). If you were injured in your employer’s truck, no-fault benefits generally come from the insurer of that truck (MCL 500.3114(3)), coordinated with comp, which is paid first (MCL 500.3109(1)). You can also sue a third party, such as another driver who caused the crash. See our [workers’ compensation page](about:blank).

## The evidence, and how fast it disappears

Carriers must keep hours-of-service records for six months (49 CFR 395.8) and driver inspection reports for three months (49 CFR 396.11); after that the regulations no longer require them. MDOT freeway cameras, business cameras along the corridors, and dash cameras overwrite in days to weeks. The truck’s engine control module and event data recorder can be cleared when it is repaired and returned to service, and a damaged rear guard can be replaced before anyone measures it.

The first act in a Washtenaw County truck case is a **preservation letter**: to the carrier, its insurer, the tractor and trailer owners, the shipper or broker, and any facility whose operations were involved. It demands the ELD and engine data, dash camera footage, the driver’s qualification file and post-crash test results, dispatch records and bills of lading, maintenance files, and the truck and trailer themselves for inspection, including the brakes, load securement, and rear guard. Once a party knows a claim is coming, destroying that evidence exposes it to spoliation sanctions.

## What to do after a Washtenaw County truck crash

1. **Call 911 and get medical care the same day.** University of Michigan Health in Ann Arbor (Level I for adults and children) and Trinity Health Ann Arbor (Level I) are the county’s trauma centers on the State’s list.
2. **Photograph the truck**: the cab, the trailer, the USDOT number and carrier name on the door, both plates, the load and anything that fell from it, and the rear guard and reflective tape.
3. **Get the police report.** Freeway crashes in the county are generally worked by the Michigan State Police; others by the Washtenaw County Sheriff’s Office or a local department. A serious crash may produce a separate commercial vehicle inspection report. **[VERIFY: posts and agencies; commercial vehicle enforcement]**
4. **Do not speak to the carrier’s insurer or its investigator.**
5. **Report to your own insurer and file the Application for No-Fault Benefits** within the first week.
6. **Call a lawyer within days**, so the preservation letter goes out before the records expire and the truck is repaired.

## The courts and deadlines

Most Washtenaw County truck cases are filed in the Washtenaw County Trial Court (22nd Judicial Circuit Court) in Ann Arbor. Cases against out-of-state carriers, which are common on I-94 and US-23, may be filed in or removed to the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan, which sits in Ann Arbor as well as Detroit. Claims against the State and the universities go to the Court of Claims. ## Frequently asked questions

**How many truck accidents happen in Washtenaw County?** 755 trucks and buses were involved in Washtenaw County crashes in 2024, three of them in fatal crashes and 111 in injury crashes, according to the Michigan State Police. Statewide, 15,888 crashes involved a heavy truck or bus.

**The truck that hit me was from another state. Does that change anything?** The federal safety rules apply the same way, and the carrier’s insurer is often out of state, but Michigan no-fault still pays your benefits through your own insurer, and the suit can be filed in Washtenaw County. The carrier may remove it to federal court.

**My car went under the back of a trailer on I-94. Is that the truck’s fault?** It may be partly or wholly. Trailers must carry rear impact guards built to federal standards, and a guard that is missing, bent, or too high to stop a car is evidence against the carrier and the trailer’s owner. The trailer has to be inspected before it is repaired.

**I hit a farm tractor on a back road at night. Who is responsible?** Possibly the operator, if the tractor lacked the lights, reflectors, and slow-moving-vehicle emblem the law requires, or was over the center line. A farm tractor is generally not a motor vehicle under no-fault, so your own insurer pays your benefits and the claim against the farm operator is an ordinary negligence claim.

**I was rear-ended by a semi on US-23. Who pays?** Your own no-fault insurer pays your medical bills and lost wages first, regardless of fault. The carrier’s insurer pays for pain and suffering and losses beyond no-fault once a threshold injury is established.

**I drive a truck and was hurt in a crash in Washtenaw County. What are my claims?** Workers’ compensation through your employer, no-fault benefits coordinated with comp, and a lawsuit against any third party who caused the crash.

**Why does the lawyer need to act within days?** Because the records expire: six months for hours-of-service logs, three months for inspection reports, days or weeks for camera footage, and the truck’s electronic data and the trailer’s damage can disappear on repair.
