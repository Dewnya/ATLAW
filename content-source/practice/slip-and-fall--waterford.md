## Waterford, MI Slip and Fall Lawyer

For twenty years a fall on someone else’s property in Michigan was close to unwinnable, because a hazard that was “open and obvious” ended the case before it began. That rule is gone. Since the Michigan Supreme Court’s July 2023 decision in *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil*, a Waterford fall case turns on who had the duty to keep the place safe, what they knew about the hazard and when, what they did about it, and how much of the fall was your fault. In Waterford those questions have local answers: the big-box stores, grocery stores, and restaurants along Highland Road (M-59) and Dixie Highway, and their parking lots; the apartment complexes along M-59, Dixie, and Walton, where a management company controls the walks and stairs; lake homes, cottages, and rentals, where a fall on a friend’s steps, deck, or dock is a homeowner’s insurance claim; marinas, docks, and seawalls; the Waterford School District’s buildings and the township’s own buildings, each with a 120-day notice rule; and sidewalks, which the township must maintain even though it owns none of the roads beside them.

## Where Waterford falls happen, and who is responsible

**The M-59 and Dixie stores.** The big-box stores, grocery stores, pharmacies, and restaurants along Highland Road and Dixie Highway: spills, freshly mopped floors without signs, merchandise in the aisle, ice and meltwater at entrances, and potholes, broken curbs, and unlit areas in the lots. A shopper is an invitee: a property owner or possessor must exercise reasonable care under the circumstances, which may include inspecting for hazards, correcting dangerous conditions, or providing appropriate warnings. The case is decided on **notice**, actual (an employee saw or caused it) or constructive (it existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it). In a shopping plaza, the plaza owner and its management company usually control the common walks and lot, the tenant store controls its own floor, and a snow-removal or maintenance contractor may share the duty; the leases and service contracts decide who, so we request them with the footage.

**Apartments and condominiums.** Waterford’s apartment complexes, many along M-59, Dixie, and Walton, and its condominium communities. Landlords must keep the premises and common areas fit for their intended use and in reasonable repair (MCL 554.139): broken stairs, loose handrails, dark stairwells and parking lots, and failed lighting are the usual claims. A lot with an inch or two of snow is still “fit” for parking under *Allison v AEW Capital* (2008), so tenant snow cases depend on whether the accumulation actually prevented use; the general negligence claim after *Kandil-Elsayed* is a separate route. A lease clause waiving the landlord’s liability for its own negligence is void. In a condominium, the association, not the unit owner, usually controls the common walks, stairs, and lots, and its snow-removal contract and maintenance records are the evidence. See our [tenant injury page](/personal-injury/tenant-injuries).

**Lake homes, cottages, and rentals.** Many Waterford homes are on or near a lake, with steep lots, exterior stairs down to the water, decks, retaining walls, and docks. Duty depends on why you were there. A social guest at a friend’s or relative’s house is a **licensee**: the owner must warn of hidden dangers the owner knows about, but need not inspect for unknown ones. A guest who was there for the owner’s business, a renter of a lake cottage, or a contractor is owed more. A fall at a friend’s house is usually paid by the homeowner’s insurance, not by the friend personally, and a short-term rental’s owner usually carries its own policy. Rotted deck boards and stair treads, missing or loose railings on lake stairs, and unlit steps at night are the recurring claims.

**Docks, seawalls, and marinas.** A fall on a slippery or broken dock, a gap between dock sections, a seawall, or a boat ramp is a premises claim against whoever controls it: the homeowner or association that owns a private dock, the marina that rents slips, or the public agency that runs a launch. A marina’s customers are invitees. A fall at a free public launch or lake access site on state land faces governmental immunity and, for land opened to the public without charge, the recreational land use act, which allows a claim only for gross negligence or willful and wanton misconduct (MCL 324.73301).

**Sidewalks: the township, even though it does not own the roads.** Waterford Township has no jurisdiction over its roads, which belong to the Road Commission for Oakland County and MDOT. Sidewalks are different. Under the immunity act a township is a “municipal corporation,” and the municipal corporation in which a sidewalk runs beside a county or state road must keep that sidewalk in reasonable repair (MCL 691.1401(d), 691.1402a(1)). The duties of the State and the Road Commission under the highway exception stop at the roadway (MCL 691.1402(1)). So a fall on a broken public sidewalk along M-59, Dixie, Walton, or Sashabaw is generally a claim against Waterford Township, not the Road Commission or MDOT. The township must have known, or should have known, of the defect at least 30 days before the fall; a defect under two inches is presumed to be reasonable repair unless the evidence shows otherwise, and the judge decides whether the presumption is rebutted (MCL 691.1402a(2)–(4)). Measure and photograph the defect with a ruler before it is patched, and serve written notice on the township stating the exact location, the defect, the injury, and the witnesses within 120 days (MCL 691.1404). A walkway inside a shopping plaza, apartment complex, or condominium community belongs to its owner or association, and the claim is an ordinary premises claim.

**Schools, township buildings, and parks.** Most of the township is served by the Waterford School District, and a small part by the Pontiac School District. A school district is a governmental agency, and a fall caused by a dangerous or defective condition of one of its buildings proceeds under the public building exception, which requires the agency to have known of the defect and failed to fix it, with written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1406). The same rule applies to the township’s buildings on Civic Center Drive and to the public library. A fall on a township, county, or state park path, beach, or field, outside a building, has no comparable exception and is difficult to bring.

**Workplaces.** A delivery driver, contractor, or staffing-agency worker who falls at a store’s dock, a yard on Dixie, or a commercial building is not that business’s employee. Their own employer’s workers’ compensation pays their benefits, and the property owner or the general contractor that controlled the dock, the yard, or the icy lot can be sued as a third party. The comp insurer is repaid from that recovery (MCL 418.827). A business’s own employee generally cannot sue the employer (MCL 418.131). See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

**Nursing homes and senior communities.** A resident’s fall in one of the township’s three Medicare-certified nursing homes or an assisted living community may be premises, negligence, or medical malpractice, and which one decides the deadline. See our [Waterford nursing home abuse page](/personal-injury/nursing-home-abuse).

## A parking-lot fall can be a no-fault claim

If you fell while getting into or out of your own car, or while loading it, Michigan’s no-fault law may cover your medical bills and lost wages even though no crash occurred. An injury from a parked vehicle is covered when the person was “occupying, entering into, or alighting from” it, or was injured by property being lifted onto or lowered from it during loading or unloading (MCL 500.3106(1)). That claim runs on the no-fault notice rules: written notice to the auto insurer within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). It sits alongside any premises claim against the lot’s owner. A worker hurt on the job while loading, unloading, or getting into or out of a parked vehicle is generally limited to workers’ compensation for that injury, with narrow exceptions (MCL 500.3106(2)). Launching or loading a boat from a trailer attached to your car can raise the same question.

## The law after *Kandil-Elsayed*

Under the old rule (*Lugo v Ameritech*, 2001), an owner owed no duty to protect visitors from a danger an average person would have noticed, and insurers denied fall claims on that basis. In *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil* (Michigan Supreme Court, July 28, 2023), the Court held that whether a hazard was open and obvious no longer decides whether a duty existed. It is now a factor in whether the owner breached the duty of reasonable care, and in how much of the fault belongs to the person who fell; both are questions for a jury. That does not make every fall a case. A visible hazard still matters: if the jury assigns you some fault, your damages are reduced by that share, and above half you recover no pain-and-suffering damages, only reduced economic losses (MCL 600.2959).

Duty also depends on why you were there. A shopper, tenant, marina customer, or business visitor is an invitee, owed inspection and repair; a social guest at a home is a licensee, owed a warning of hidden dangers the owner knows about; a trespasser is owed protection from willful and wanton misconduct. Treating every visitor the same overstates or understates the claim.

## Snow and ice

An owner must take reasonable steps, within a reasonable time after snow or ice accumulates, to make walkways, entrances, stairs, and lots safe. What is reasonable depends on the property: a grocery store opening at 7 a.m. after an overnight storm is expected to have treated its entrance, and an apartment complex its stairs and paths before residents leave for work. A contractor with a salting contract that missed the morning run may share the fault. The law does not require an owner to keep every surface bare at every moment of a storm. Black ice from refreezing meltwater, a leaking gutter or downspout, or snow piled where it melts across the walk can be a condition the owner created, and those cases are stronger than natural accumulation. On a lake lot, exterior stairs and steep walks that are never cleared in winter are a recurring hazard for guests.

Some Michigan communities require adjacent owners to clear snow and ice from public sidewalks by ordinance. Photograph the ice, the path, the alternatives you had, and the weather the same day.

## What to do after a fall in Waterford

1. **Get medical care the same day** and tell the provider where and how you fell. McLaren Oakland and Trinity Health Oakland in Pontiac, both Level II trauma centers, are close by.
2. **Photograph the hazard immediately**, from several angles, with something for scale, before it is mopped, salted, repaired, or patched. Photograph your shoes.
3. **Report the fall** to the store or plaza management, the landlord or association, the marina, or the township, and get the incident report number. Do not give a recorded statement to any insurer.
4. **Get witness names**, including employees, and note the time; it matters for constructive notice.
5. **Measure a sidewalk or dock defect** with a ruler in the photograph.
6. **Keep the shoes** unworn and unwashed.
7. **Call a lawyer within days.** Footage may be overwritten in days, a deck or dock may be repaired within the week, and if the township or the school district may be involved, a short notice period is already running.

## Frequently asked questions

**I slipped on ice outside a store on M-59. Who is responsible?** Usually the store or the plaza owner and its management company, whichever controlled the walk, and possibly their snow-removal contractor. Since 2023, an obvious hazard no longer ends the case; the questions are whether the walk should have been treated by the time you arrived, whether you had a realistic alternative route, and how much of the fault is yours.

**I fell on a broken sidewalk along Dixie Highway. Is that MDOT’s responsibility?** Generally not. MDOT owns Dixie, but its duty stops at the roadway; Waterford Township must keep the public sidewalk beside it in reasonable repair. The township must have known or should have known of the defect at least 30 days before, defects under two inches are presumed reasonable repair, and written notice must reach the township within 120 days.

**I fell on the stairs down to the lake at a friend’s house. Do I have to sue my friend?** The claim is against the owner, but it is usually paid by the homeowner’s insurance, if the owner has a policy that covers it. As a social guest, the question is whether the owner knew of a hidden danger, such as a rotted step or a loose railing, and did not warn you.

**I slipped on a dock at a marina. Is the marina liable?** It can be. A marina’s customers are invitees, owed inspection and repair of the docks and walkways it controls. Photograph the dock surface, any gap or broken board, and the lighting, and get the incident report.

**My mother fell in her apartment complex’s stairwell. Do we have a case?** Possibly. A landlord must keep stairs and common areas in reasonable repair and fit for their use, and a general negligence claim may apply too. Broken stairs, loose handrails, and failed lighting are the usual claims, and the complex’s maintenance requests and prior complaints are the evidence.

**I fell getting out of my car in a parking lot. Is that a car accident claim?** It may be. No-fault can cover injuries from entering or leaving a parked vehicle, separate from any claim against the lot’s owner.

**How long do I have?** Three years to sue a private owner; 120 days for written notice to the township or the school district; and one year to notify a no-fault insurer. The footage disappears far sooner.
