## Where Pontiac truck crashes happen

**The warehouse and distribution traffic.** Distribution centers run on schedules: tractor-trailers arrive and leave in waves, line up on the approach roads when the yard is full, and turn across traffic into and out of the gates, while fleets of delivery vans leave for their routes. The crashes are trucks turning wide across lanes at the gates, trucks queued or parked on the shoulder or in a travel lane waiting to get in, vans pulling out into traffic, and rear-end crashes in the stop-and-go near the entrances. When a facility’s gate layout or appointment schedule puts trucks on the public road, the facility owner or its logistics operator may share responsibility with the carrier. Delivery vans are often operated by contracted delivery companies rather than the retailer itself, so the van’s operator, its insurer, and the contract between them have to be identified.

**M-59 and I-75.** On the freeways, the crashes are rear-end and underride crashes in congestion and at the backups near the interchanges, lane changes into a car in the blind spot on the right side of the cab, merge crashes on the ramps, and crashes caused by driver fatigue on long runs. A car that strikes the side or rear of a trailer can go under it; that is an **underride** crash, among the most lethal in trucking. Trailers must carry rear impact guards that meet federal standards (49 CFR 393.86), and a missing, damaged, or substandard guard is evidence against the carrier and the trailer’s owner. Huron Street, the surface stretch of M-59, adds signal and left-turn crashes at Telegraph, Perry, Opdyke, and Baldwin, and its closure between the two sides of the Loop is scheduled for spring to fall 2027, which will send detoured trucks onto other streets. **[VERIFY]**

**Telegraph, the Loop, and the city streets.** Telegraph carries trucks to and from Dixie Highway and the north county, past the county complex and retail centers. Downtown and in the neighborhoods, delivery trucks, box trucks, and garbage trucks make tight turns, back into alleys and loading zones, and double-park in travel lanes; the crashes are right-turn crashes that catch a car, cyclist, or pedestrian on the inside of the turn, backing crashes, and crashes with trucks stopped where drivers do not expect them. Heavy trucks on designated state routes in Michigan can weigh up to 164,000 pounds, about twice the federal Interstate limit, and need far more distance to stop. See our [pedestrian accident page](/personal-injury/pedestrian-accidents) and [bicycle accident page](/personal-injury/bicycle-accidents).

**Lost and spilled loads.** Cargo, equipment, or debris that falls from a truck is a violation of the federal cargo securement rules (49 CFR 393.100 and following) and of Michigan law (MCL 257.720), and the carrier and the company that loaded the truck can both be responsible.

**Whose road it is.** Pontiac is a city, so the answer has three parts. M-59, I-75, Telegraph, Woodward, and the Loop are MDOT’s; a road-defect claim against the State goes to the Court of Claims, with written notice filed there within 120 days (MCL 691.1404; *Champine v Department of Transportation*, Mich 2022). Most city streets are the City of Pontiac’s, and some roads are the Road Commission for Oakland County’s by segment, each with written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1402, 691.1404).

## Who is responsible, and who pays

**The carrier, not just the driver.** The motor carrier is responsible for its drivers’ compliance with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations and is liable for a driver’s negligence in the course of employment. Michigan applies the federal rules to intrastate carriers (MCL 480.11a), so a local hauler or delivery carrier that never leaves Oakland County is held to the same standards as an interstate carrier. The rules that decide truck cases most often:

| Rule | 49 CFR | What it means for the case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hours of service | Part 395 | 11 hours driving in a 14-hour window after 10 off; 60/70-hour weekly limits; recorded on an electronic logging device. Some short-haul drivers are exempt from logs but not from the limits. |
| Driver qualification | Part 391 | CDL, medical certificate, driving record, background check; negligent hiring and retention are claims against the carrier. |
| Post-crash testing | Part 382 | Alcohol within 8 hours and drugs within 32 after a fatal crash, or an injury or tow-away crash with a citation. |
| Inspection and maintenance | Part 396 | Pre-trip inspections, defect reports, maintenance records; brake, tire, lift-axle, lighting, and reflective-tape cases are maintenance cases. |
| Cargo securement | 393.100–393.136 | Loads must be contained or secured so they cannot shift or fall; a spilled load is evidence of a violation. |
| Equipment: rear impact guards, lighting, reflectors | Part 393 | Underride guards (393.86) and conspicuity marking; a trailer that cannot be seen at night or lacks a working guard is a defective trailer. |
| Handheld phone ban; hazardous conditions | 392.82, 392.14 | No handheld phone use; reduce speed or stop in snow, ice, and fog. |
| Minimum insurance | Part 387 | $750,000 general freight; $1 million oil; $5 million hazardous materials. |



**The owner of the truck or trailer.** In Michigan, a vehicle’s owner is liable for its negligent operation by someone driving with the owner’s consent (MCL 257.401). When the tractor, the trailer, and the driver belong to different companies, which is common in freight and in warehouse drayage, each may be a defendant, although a company in the business of renting or leasing vehicles is generally shielded from owner liability by federal law (49 USC 30106).

**Michigan no-fault still applies to you.** Your own auto insurer pays your medical bills, 85 percent of lost wages up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year, replacement services, and attendant care regardless of fault (MCL 500.3107), with written notice due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). The Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays if no household policy applies (MCL 500.3172). Your claim against the driver and the carrier for pain and suffering and losses beyond no-fault requires a threshold injury (MCL 500.3135) and must be filed within three years (MCL 600.5805(2)). If you were more than 50 percent at fault, you cannot recover pain-and-suffering damages, and below that your share reduces them (MCL 500.3135(2)(b)). The difference from a car case is the money available: the carrier’s $750,000-plus policy, often with excess coverage above it. See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

**Other defendants.** The shipper or broker that loaded the truck or hired an unsafe carrier; the warehouse or distribution facility whose gate or dock operations contributed; the maintenance contractor; the manufacturer of a failed component or a defective guard ([product liability](/personal-injury/product-liability)); and, for a government truck (MDOT, the City of Pontiac, the Road Commission, Oakland County, SMART, or a school bus), the agency under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405).

**If you were the truck driver.** Workers’ compensation pays your benefits, and your employer usually cannot be sued (MCL 418.131). If you were injured in your employer’s truck, no-fault benefits generally come from the insurer of that truck (MCL 500.3114(3)), coordinated with comp, which is paid first (MCL 500.3109(1)). You can also sue a third party, such as another driver or a facility whose dock or yard operation caused the crash. See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

## The evidence, and how fast it disappears

Carriers must keep hours-of-service records for six months (49 CFR 395.8) and driver inspection reports for three months (49 CFR 396.11); after that the regulations no longer require them. Warehouse gate and yard cameras, business cameras along M-59 and Telegraph, MDOT freeway cameras, and dash cameras overwrite in days to weeks. The truck’s engine control module and event data recorder can be cleared when it is repaired and returned to service; a spilled load is swept up within hours; and a damaged rear guard can be replaced before anyone measures it.

The first act in a Pontiac truck case is a **preservation letter**: to the carrier, its insurer, the tractor and trailer owners, the shipper or broker, and, where the crash happened at or near a warehouse or yard, the facility and its logistics operator. It demands the ELD and engine data, dash camera footage, the driver’s qualification file and post-crash test results, dispatch records, bills of lading, gate and dock appointment logs, maintenance files, and the truck and trailer themselves for inspection, including the brakes, lift axles, load securement, and rear guard. Once a party knows a claim is coming, destroying that evidence exposes it to spoliation sanctions. 

## What to do after a Pontiac truck crash

1. **Call 911 and get medical care the same day.** McLaren Oakland and Trinity Health Oakland, both in Pontiac, are Level II trauma centers on the State’s list; the most serious injuries may go to Corewell Health William Beaumont University Hospital in Royal Oak, the county’s Level I adult trauma center.
2. **Photograph the truck**: the cab, the trailer, the USDOT number and carrier name on the door, both plates, the load and anything that fell from it, the rear guard and reflective tape, and the gate, yard, or loading zone if the truck was entering or leaving one.
3. **Get the police report.** Crashes on city streets are worked by the Oakland County Sheriff’s Office, which polices Pontiac under contract; freeway crashes may be worked by the Michigan State Police. A serious crash may produce a separate commercial vehicle inspection report from the State Police.
4. **Do not speak to the carrier’s insurer or its investigator.**
5. **Report to your own insurer and file the Application for No-Fault Benefits** within the first week.
6. **Call a lawyer within days**, so the preservation letter goes out before the records expire and the truck is repaired.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many truck accidents happen in Pontiac?** The State does not publish truck crash figures by city. Countywide, 2,081 heavy trucks and buses were involved in Oakland County crashes in 2024, and statewide 15,888 crashes involved a heavy truck or bus.

**A truck waiting to get into a warehouse was stopped in the lane and I hit it. Is that my fault?** Not necessarily. Following too closely is usually the claim against the rear driver, but a truck stopped in a travel lane without warning or lights, or as a routine result of a facility’s gate schedule, can carry fault too, and so can the facility.

**A delivery van hit me. Who is responsible?** The van’s driver and the company that employs the driver, which is often a contracted delivery company rather than the retailer whose name is on the package. That company’s commercial auto policy is usually the coverage. Get the van’s company name and plate.

**My car went under the back of a trailer on I-75. Is that the truck’s fault?** It may be partly or wholly. Trailers must carry rear impact guards built to federal standards, and a guard that is missing, bent, or too high to stop a car is evidence against the carrier and the trailer’s owner. The trailer has to be inspected before it is repaired.

**I was rear-ended by a semi on M-59. Who pays?** Your own no-fault insurer pays your medical bills and lost wages first, regardless of fault. The carrier’s insurer pays for pain and suffering and losses beyond no-fault once a threshold injury is established.

**I drive a truck and was hurt in a crash in Pontiac. What are my claims?** Workers’ compensation through your employer, no-fault benefits coordinated with comp, and a lawsuit against any third party who caused the crash.

**Why does the lawyer need to act within days?** Because the records expire: six months for hours-of-service logs, three months for inspection reports, days or weeks for camera footage, and the truck’s electronic data and the trailer’s damage can disappear on repair.
