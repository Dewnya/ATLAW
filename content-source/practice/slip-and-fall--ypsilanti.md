## Ypsilanti Slip and Fall Lawyer

For twenty years, a fall on someone else’s property in Michigan was close to unwinnable, because a hazard that was “open and obvious” ended the case before it began. That rule is gone. Since the Michigan Supreme Court’s July 2023 decision in *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil*, a fall case turns on four questions:

- who had the duty to keep the place safe
- what they knew about the hazard, and when
- what they did about it
- how much of the fall was your own fault

In Ypsilanti, the answer to the first question depends on where you fell:

- **Eastern Michigan University,** whose buildings, stadium, residence halls, and walks are State property, with a claim in the Court of Claims and its own notice deadlines.
- **Landlords and management companies,** from older houses near campus converted to apartments to the apartment complexes in the township.
- **Stores, restaurants, and their parking lots,** downtown, in Depot Town, and along Washtenaw Avenue, Ecorse Road, and Michigan Avenue.
- **The City of Ypsilanti or Ypsilanti Charter Township,** for a public sidewalk or a public building, with a 120-day notice to the right one.
- **Homeowners,** for a fall at a friend’s or relative’s house.

## Where Ypsilanti falls happen, and who is responsible

**Stores, restaurants, and their lots.** Along Washtenaw Avenue, Ecorse Road, and Michigan Avenue in the township, and in the downtown and Depot Town storefronts and restaurants, the falls are:

- spills and freshly mopped floors without signs
- meltwater and slush tracked inside the entrance in winter
- merchandise or boxes in the aisle
- worn or uneven stairs in older downtown and Depot Town buildings
- potholes, broken curbs, wheel stops, and unlit areas in the lots

A shopper or diner is an **invitee**, owed the full duty of care: the owner must inspect, fix what it finds, and warn of what it cannot fix. The case is decided on **notice**:

- **Actual notice:** an employee saw the hazard or caused it.
- **Constructive notice:** it existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it.

In a shopping center, the owner and its management company usually control the common walks and lot, the tenant controls its own floor, and a snow-removal or maintenance contractor may share the duty. The leases and service contracts decide who, so we request them with the footage.

**Rentals near campus and apartment complexes.** Many EMU students and other renters live in older houses near campus that have been converted to apartments, with porches, exterior wooden stairs, balconies, and basement units. Others live in the apartment complexes across the township. Under MCL 554.139, a landlord must keep the premises and common areas fit for their intended use and in reasonable repair. The usual claims are:

- rotted porch boards, stair treads, and balcony railings
- loose or missing handrails
- dark stairwells, walks, and parking areas
- basement stairs that do not meet the code
- ice in a lot or on a walk the landlord controls

A lot with an inch or two of snow is still “fit” for parking under *Allison v AEW Capital* (2008), so tenant snow cases depend on whether the accumulation actually prevented use. The general negligence claim after *Kandil-Elsayed* is a separate route. A lease clause waiving the landlord’s liability for its own negligence is void.

Both governments inspect rentals, and the inspection file can show what the landlord knew:

- **In the city,** a rental needs a Certificate of Compliance and is inspected every two years under the property maintenance code.
- **In the township,** a rental needs a certificate of compliance. The township’s checklist covers stairs free from defects, a graspable handrail or guardrail in place, porch and step concrete free from defects, and sidewalk trip hazards of two inches or more. An item the inspector treats as a life-safety concern must be corrected within seven days.

A failed inspection or an open violation on the stair or railing that gave way is strong evidence of notice. A guest at a tenant’s party who falls on a broken porch or balcony may have a claim against the landlord as well as the tenant. In a condominium, the association usually controls the common walks, stairs, and lots. See our [tenant injury page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/tenant-injuries).

**Parking lots and public lots.** Most Ypsilanti parking is in private lots, and a fall there is an ordinary premises claim against the owner and any snow or maintenance contractor. The public lots downtown and in Depot Town, and EMU’s lots, raise governmental immunity, where a claim outside a building is difficult to bring. A fall getting out of your own car may also be a no-fault claim (see below).

**Homes.** Duty depends on why you were there:

- **A social guest** at a friend’s or relative’s house is a **licensee**. The owner must warn of hidden dangers the owner knows about, but need not inspect for unknown ones.
- **A guest there for the owner’s business,** such as a paying customer, is owed more.

A fall at a friend’s house is usually paid by the homeowner’s insurance, not by the friend personally.

**Public sidewalks.** The municipal corporation in which a sidewalk runs beside a road must keep it in reasonable repair (MCL 691.1401(e), 691.1402a(1)). In the city, that is the City of Ypsilanti. In the township, it is the township, not the Washtenaw County Road Commission, which owns the road but not the sidewalk. The rules:

- The City or township must have known, or should have known, of the defect at least 30 days before the fall (MCL 691.1402a(2)).
- A defect under two inches is presumed to be in reasonable repair unless the evidence shows otherwise, and the judge decides whether the presumption is rebutted (MCL 691.1402a(3), (4)).
- Written notice must be served within 120 days, stating the exact location, the defect, the injury, and the witnesses (MCL 691.1404). A notice sent to the wrong government does not protect the claim.

Measure and photograph the defect with a ruler before it is patched. Tree roots lifting slabs in the older neighborhoods near campus and downtown are a recurring cause. A walkway inside a shopping center or apartment complex belongs to its owner, and the claim is an ordinary premises claim.

**Schools, libraries, and City and township buildings.** Ypsilanti Community Schools, Lincoln Consolidated Schools, the Ypsilanti District Library, and the City and township are governmental agencies. A fall caused by a dangerous or defective condition of one of their buildings proceeds under the public building exception. That exception requires the agency to have known, or to have had reason to know, of the defect and to have failed to fix it within a reasonable time, with written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1406). A fall on a park path outside a building, in Riverside Park, Frog Island Park, Ford Lake Park, or on the Border-to-Border Trail, has no comparable exception and is difficult to bring.

**Hospitals, nursing homes, and senior communities.** A fall at Trinity Health Ann Arbor, which has an Ypsilanti mailing address but sits in Superior Township, or at a clinic, may be an ordinary premises claim, if the cause was a wet floor or a broken step, or medical malpractice, if the cause was a failure to assess or supervise a patient at risk of falling. A fall at University of Michigan Health is a claim against the University in the Court of Claims. Which kind of claim it is decides the deadline and the procedure. A resident’s fall in a nursing home or assisted living community raises the same question. See our [nursing home abuse page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/nursing-home-abuse) and [medical malpractice page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/medical-malpractice).

**Workplaces.** A delivery driver, contractor, or staffing-agency worker who falls at a warehouse near Willow Run Airport, a store’s loading dock, or a construction site is not that business’s employee. Their own employer’s workers’ compensation pays their benefits. The property owner or the general contractor that controlled the dock, the site, or the icy lot can be sued as a third party, and the comp insurer is repaid from that recovery (MCL 418.827). A business’s own employee generally cannot sue the employer (MCL 418.131). See our [workers’ compensation page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

## Falls on Eastern Michigan University property

Eastern Michigan University is a constitutional state university, so a fall on its property is a claim against the State, heard in the Michigan Court of Claims by a judge, not a jury. A verified notice, or the claim itself, must be filed with that court within six months (MCL 600.6431), and a building claim needs its own notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1406). The Michigan Supreme Court has held that the notice requirement applies to any claim against the State, wherever it is filed (*Christie v Wayne State University*, 2023).

Where you fell on campus changes whether there is a claim at all:

- Inside a building open for use by members of the public, such as classrooms, the student center, the library, Rynearson Stadium, and the arena, the public building exception can apply: a dangerous or defective condition of the building that the University knew about, or should have known about, and failed to fix (MCL 691.1406). A broken stair, a loose railing, or a defective floor are the kinds of claims it covers. The exception has its own 120-day notice, which should be served along with the Court of Claims notice.
- **Outside a building,** on a campus walk, a plaza, or a University lot, the claim is much harder. The sidewalk duty in MCL 691.1402a applies to municipal corporations, such as the City and the township, not to the State or the University, and the highway exception does not reach a walk that is not part of a public road. A fall on a campus walk outside a building may have no immunity exception that fits.
- **A fall caused by a contractor,** such as a cleaning or snow-removal company working for the University, may be a claim against the contractor in the ordinary courts.

A private apartment complex next to campus is not University property, and a fall there is an ordinary claim against its owner.

## A parking-lot fall can be a no-fault claim

If you fell while getting into or out of your own car, or while loading it, Michigan’s no-fault law may cover your medical bills and lost wages even though no crash occurred. An injury from a parked vehicle is covered when the person was “occupying, entering into, or alighting from” it, or loading or unloading it (MCL 500.3106(1)).

- That claim runs on the no-fault notice rules: written notice to the auto insurer within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)).
- It sits alongside any premises claim against the lot’s owner.
- A worker hurt on the job while loading, unloading, or getting into or out of a parked vehicle is generally limited to workers’ compensation for that injury, with narrow exceptions (MCL 500.3106(2)).

For an EMU student, the no-fault insurer may be a parent’s policy.

## The law after *Kandil-Elsayed*

Under the old rule (*Lugo v Ameritech*, 2001), an owner owed no duty to protect visitors from a danger an average person would have noticed, and insurers denied fall claims on that basis. In *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil* (Michigan Supreme Court, July 28, 2023), decided with *Pinsky v Kroger*, the Court changed that:

- Whether a hazard was open and obvious no longer decides whether a duty existed.
- It is now a factor in whether the owner breached the duty of reasonable care, and in how much of the fault belongs to the person who fell.
- Both are questions for a jury.

That does not make every fall a case. A visible hazard still matters. If the jury assigns you some fault, your damages are reduced by that share. Above half, you recover no pain-and-suffering damages, only reduced economic losses (MCL 600.2959).

Duty also depends on why you were there:

- **Invitee** (a shopper, diner, tenant in a common area, or ticket holder): owed inspection and repair.
- **Licensee** (a social guest at a home): owed a warning of hidden dangers the owner knows about.
- **Trespasser:** owed protection from willful and wanton misconduct.

Treating every visitor the same overstates or understates the claim.

## Snow and ice

An owner must take reasonable steps, within a reasonable time after snow or ice accumulates, to make walkways, entrances, stairs, and lots safe. What is reasonable depends on the property:

- a store or restaurant opening in the morning after an overnight storm is expected to have treated its entrance
- an apartment complex or rental house, its stairs and walks before residents leave for class or work
- a lot, its drive lanes and the walks to the door

A contractor with a salting contract that missed the morning run may share the fault. The law does not require an owner to keep every surface bare at every moment of a storm.

Some ice is a condition the owner created, and those cases are stronger than natural accumulation:

- black ice from refreezing meltwater
- a leaking gutter or downspout
- snow piled where it melts across the walk
- a drain in a lot that pools and freezes

The City of Ypsilanti requires property owners to clear the public sidewalks beside their property of all snow within 24 hours of the first accumulation, and of ice immediately or, where that is impracticable, to treat it, from October 1 to May 31 (City Code §§ 94-133 to 94-136). If they do not, a City contractor clears the walk and the owner is billed. Michigan courts have generally held that an ordinance like this does not by itself make the owner liable to a pedestrian, and a municipality’s duty to repair sidewalks does not extend to natural accumulations of snow and ice. An owner whose property caused the ice, by drainage or plowing, can be liable. Photograph the ice, the path, the alternatives you had, and the weather the same day.

## What to do after a fall in Ypsilanti

1. **Get medical care the same day** and tell the provider where and how you fell. Trinity Health Ann Arbor and University of Michigan Health are the nearest hospitals.
2. **Photograph the hazard immediately,** from several angles, with something for scale, before it is mopped, salted, repaired, or patched. Photograph your shoes.
3. **Report the fall** to the store or building management, the landlord or association, the University, or the City or township, and get the incident report number. On campus, the University’s police department can take a report. Do not give a recorded statement to any insurer or risk manager.
4. **Get witness names,** including employees, and note the time. It matters for constructive notice.
5. **Measure a sidewalk or step defect** with a ruler in the photograph.
6. **Write down whether you were in the city or the township,** and the nearest address or cross street.
7. **Keep the shoes** unworn and unwashed.
8. **Call a lawyer within days.** Footage may be overwritten in days, and a porch or step may be repaired within the week. If the City, the township, a school district, or EMU may be involved, a 120-day or six-month notice is already running.

## Frequently asked questions

**I slipped on ice outside a store on Washtenaw Avenue. Who is responsible?** Usually the store or the shopping center’s owner and management company, whichever controlled the entrance, and possibly their snow-removal contractor. If you fell on the public sidewalk rather than the store’s own walk, the question is different: an owner’s duty under a snow ordinance generally does not make it liable to you, unless its property caused the ice. Since 2023, an obvious hazard no longer ends the case.

**How do I know whether I fell in the city or the township?** The police or incident report usually shows it: the Ypsilanti Police Department responds in the city, the Sheriff’s Office in the township. A mailing address that says “Ypsilanti” does not settle it. We confirm it from the location before any notice goes out.

**I fell inside an EMU building. Can I sue the University?** Possibly. The claim goes to the Court of Claims, with written notice filed within 120 days. It must fit the public building exception: a dangerous or defective condition of the building that the University knew about, or should have known about, and did not fix.

**I fell on a walk on campus, outside a building. Is that the same?** No, and it is often harder. The sidewalk rule that makes the City or township responsible for public sidewalks does not apply to the University’s walks and plazas, and the building exception does not reach outside the building. If a contractor caused the hazard, that may be a separate claim.

**I fell on the porch of my rental near campus. Do I have a case against the landlord?** Possibly. A landlord must keep the premises and common areas fit for their use and in reasonable repair, and rotted boards, broken stairs, and loose railings are the usual claims. Your maintenance requests and the City’s or township’s rental inspection records are the evidence.

**I tripped on a broken sidewalk in Ypsilanti Township. Who do I notify?** The township, not the Road Commission, within 120 days, stating the exact location, the defect, the injury, and the witnesses. The township must have known or should have known of the defect at least 30 days before, and a defect under two inches is presumed to be in reasonable repair. In the city, the notice goes to the City.

**How long do I have?** Three years to sue a private owner; 120 days for written notice to the City, the township, or a school district; 120 days for a fall in an EMU building; and one year to notify a no-fault insurer. The footage disappears far sooner.
