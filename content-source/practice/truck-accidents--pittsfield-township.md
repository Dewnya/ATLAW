## Pittsfield Township Truck Accident Lawyer

Heavy trucks in Pittsfield Charter Township travel four kinds of roads:

- **I-94 and US-23,** which meet at the township’s northeast corner. I-94 carries freight between Detroit and Chicago, with interchanges at State Street and Ann Arbor-Saline Road. US-23 carries it north and south, with interchanges at Michigan Avenue and Washtenaw Avenue. The township recorded 148 crashes on interstate routes in 2024.
- **Michigan Avenue (US-12),** southwest toward Saline, past subdivisions, schools, and commercial strips. The township recorded 312 crashes on U.S. routes in 2024.
- **Carpenter Road, Ellsworth Road, and Washtenaw Avenue,** where tractor-trailers and box trucks deliver to the big-box stores and shopping centers, among shoppers, bus riders, and apartment traffic.
- **State Street and the business parks,** where trucks serve the industrial, research, and office parks between I-94 and Ellsworth Road.

In 2024, according to the Michigan State Police, 755 heavy trucks and buses were involved in crashes in Washtenaw County. Statewide, 110 people were killed and 3,613 injured in heavy truck and bus crashes.

A truck crash is not a car crash with a larger vehicle:

- **The rules are federal.** The driver and the carrier are governed by federal safety regulations, and a violation is evidence of negligence.
- **The insurance is larger.** An interstate carrier must carry $750,000 or more in insurance.
- **The records expire.** The driver’s electronic logs and the truck’s engine data, the records that prove the case, can be lawfully discarded within months.
- **The other side moves fast.** The carrier’s insurer will have an investigator on the case within days.

## Where Pittsfield Township truck crashes happen

**I-94 and US-23.** The crashes are:

- rear-end and underride crashes in the backups at the ramps and at the I-94 and US-23 junction, and in work zones
- lane changes into a car in the blind spot on the right side of the cab
- trucks merging from short entrance ramps
- fatigue crashes on overnight runs between Detroit and Chicago, or between Toledo and Flint

A car that strikes the side or rear of a trailer can go under it. That is an **underride** crash, which is often fatal or catastrophic. Trailers must carry rear impact guards that meet federal standards (49 CFR 393.86). A missing, damaged, or substandard guard is evidence against the carrier and the trailer’s owner.

Federal rules require a truck driver to use extreme caution in hazardous conditions, to reduce speed, and to stop if conditions become too dangerous (49 CFR 392.14). A jackknife or loss-of-control crash on an icy ramp or overpass is often a speed-for-conditions case.

**Michigan Avenue toward Saline.** Michigan Avenue is a higher-speed U.S. route with long gaps between signals, and it meets Carpenter Road at one of the county’s ten highest-crash intersections. The truck crashes are:

- a truck turning left across traffic into a driveway, a business, or a side road
- a truck pulling out of a driveway into a lane, slowly, and being struck or striking a car (MCL 257.652)
- rear-end crashes behind a truck slowing to turn, or a truck failing to stop behind traffic
- crossover and head-on crashes on undivided stretches
- deer: the township recorded 96 deer-involved crashes in 2024, and a truck swerving to miss a deer can cross into the next lane



**Store deliveries on Carpenter, Ellsworth, and Washtenaw.** Tractor-trailers and box trucks serve the big-box stores and shopping centers on these roads throughout the day. Three of Washtenaw County’s ten highest-crash intersections in 2024 were on Carpenter Road, and a fourth was at Hogback and Washtenaw. The truck crashes are:

- trucks turning into and out of the shopping centers across several lanes
- wide right turns that catch a car, a cyclist, or a pedestrian on the inside of the turn
- trucks backing across a drive aisle or a sidewalk into a loading dock
- trucks parked or stopped in a lane or a fire lane to unload
- pedestrians crossing between signals to reach a bus stop or a store

A crash at a loading dock or in a shopping center can involve the carrier, the store or its owner, the shopping center’s management company, and a staffing agency that supplied the driver or the dock worker. See our [pedestrian accident page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/pedestrian-accidents) and [bicycle accident page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/bicycle-accidents).

**State Street and the business parks.** Trucks serving the industrial and business parks enter and leave State Street and Ellsworth Road from yards and docks. The crashes are trucks turning across traffic, trucks backing into a road, and trucks parked on the shoulder at night without warning devices (49 CFR 392.22).

**County roads and seasonal weight limits.** Dump trucks, gravel haulers, and construction trucks use the township’s county roads to reach subdivisions and building sites. The Washtenaw County Road Commission sets seasonal weight restrictions in spring, when the roads are softest. A truck running overweight in violation of those limits, or a load of gravel or debris spilled on a two-lane road, is evidence against the hauler (MCL 257.720, 257.722).

**Lost and spilled loads.** Cargo, gravel, debris, or equipment that falls from a truck is a violation of the federal cargo securement rules (49 CFR 393.100 and following) and of Michigan law (MCL 257.720). The carrier and the company that loaded the truck can both be responsible.

**Buses.** TheRide runs routes on Washtenaw Avenue, Carpenter Road, State Street, and Packard Road. A crash with a TheRide bus is a claim under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405). A bus passenger’s no-fault benefits follow the bus-passenger priority rules (MCL 500.3114(2)). A school bus crash is a claim against the school district under the same exception. See our [bus accident page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/bus-accidents).

## Whose road it is, and whose vehicle

**Road owners.** The township owns no roads. A road-defect claim depends on who owns the road:

- **MDOT** owns I-94, US-23, US-12 (Michigan Avenue), and M-17 (Washtenaw Avenue east of US-23). A road-defect claim goes to the Court of Claims, with written notice filed there within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **The Washtenaw County Road Commission** owns Carpenter, Packard, Ellsworth, State Street, Platt, Hogback, and the subdivision streets. Written notice is due within 120 days (MCL 691.1402, 691.1404).

A road agency is liable for failing to keep the roadway in reasonable repair, not for its design.

**Public vehicles.**

- **A Road Commission, township, or school district vehicle,** such as a plow, a road maintenance truck, or a school bus, is a claim under the motor vehicle exception against the agency that owns it (MCL 691.1405). An individual driver is personally liable only for gross negligence (MCL 691.1407(2)).
- **A State or MDOT truck,** or a University of Michigan vehicle, is a claim against the State in the Court of Claims, with the six-month notice.
- **A private contractor’s truck** working for a public body, such as a plow or a construction truck under contract, is a claim against the contractor in the ordinary courts.



## Checking a trucking company’s safety record

Every interstate carrier, and many Michigan intrastate carriers, has a USDOT number painted on the cab door. That number pulls up the carrier’s federal record:

- **The FMCSA Company Snapshot** (on the SAFER website) shows the carrier’s registered name and address, its number of trucks and drivers, and its inspections and crashes over the past two years, including out-of-service rates.
- **The FMCSA Safety Measurement System** groups the carrier’s inspection and crash results by category, including unsafe driving, hours-of-service compliance, and vehicle maintenance. Some results are not shown to the public.
- **The carrier’s safety rating,** if FMCSA has rated it, is “satisfactory,” “conditional,” or “unsatisfactory.” Many carriers have never been rated.

A poor record does not by itself prove fault in a crash. It can support a claim that the carrier failed to supervise or maintain its fleet, and that a shipper or broker was negligent in hiring a carrier with that record.

## Who is responsible, and who pays

**The carrier and the driver.** The motor carrier is responsible for its drivers’ compliance with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations. An employer is liable for its driver’s negligence within the scope of the employment, and the driver can be sued as well. Michigan applies the federal rules to intrastate carriers (MCL 480.11a), so a local hauler that never leaves Washtenaw County is held to the same standards.

These are the rules that decide truck cases most often:

| Rule | 49 CFR | What it means for the case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hours of service | Part 395 | 11 hours driving in a 14-hour window after 10 hours off; 60/70-hour weekly limits; recorded on an electronic logging device. Some short-haul drivers are exempt from logs but not from the limits. |
| Driver qualification | Part 391 | CDL, medical certificate, driving record, background check. Negligent hiring and retention are claims against the carrier. |
| Post-crash testing | Part 382 | Alcohol within 8 hours and drugs within 32 hours after a fatal crash, or after an injury or tow-away crash with a citation. |
| Inspection and maintenance | Part 396 | Pre-trip inspections, defect reports, maintenance records. Brake, tire, lighting, and reflective-tape cases are maintenance cases. |
| Cargo securement | 393.100–393.136 | Loads must be contained or secured so they cannot shift or fall. |
| Equipment: rear impact guards, lighting, reflectors | Part 393 | Underride guards (393.86) and conspicuity marking. A trailer that cannot be seen at night or lacks a working guard is a defective trailer. |
| Hazardous conditions; stopped vehicles; phones | 392.14, 392.22, 392.82 | Reduce speed or stop in snow, ice, and fog; warning devices for a stopped truck; no handheld phone use. |
| Minimum insurance | Part 387 | $750,000 general freight; $1 million oil; $5 million hazardous materials. |



**The owner of the truck or trailer.** In Michigan, a vehicle’s owner is liable for its negligent operation by someone driving with the owner’s consent (MCL 257.401). When the tractor, the trailer, and the driver belong to different companies, each may be a defendant. Lessors’ liability is limited by the same statute.

**Michigan no-fault still applies to you.** Your own auto insurer, or a household policy, pays these benefits regardless of fault (MCL 500.3107):

- your medical bills, up to the PIP medical level on the policy
- 85 percent of lost wages, up to $7,455 a month for crashes on or after October 1, 2026 (the cap DIFS sets each year)
- replacement services
- attendant care

Written notice is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). If you have no household policy, as is common for a pedestrian or cyclist without a car, a vehicle occupant claims from the insurer of the vehicle they were in, and then the Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3114(4), 500.3172). A pedestrian or cyclist with no household policy claims through the Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3115(1)).

Your claim against the driver and the carrier is for pain and suffering and for losses beyond no-fault, including medical bills above your PIP level. It requires a threshold injury (MCL 500.3135) and must be filed within three years (MCL 600.5805(2)). Michigan’s comparative fault rule concerns **your** share of fault, not which defendants can be sued:

- **More than 50 percent at fault:** you cannot recover pain-and-suffering damages.
- **50 percent or less:** your share reduces them (MCL 500.3135(2)(b); MCL 600.2959). At exactly 50 percent, you recover half.

The noneconomic damages caps apply to medical malpractice and to product liability claims, such as a claim against the maker of a failed part (MCL 600.1483, 600.2946a), not to the claim against the driver and carrier.

The difference from a car case is the money available: the carrier’s commercial policy, often with excess coverage above it. If that coverage still falls short, your own underinsured motorist coverage may add to it. Your insurer’s written consent is needed before you accept the carrier’s limit. See our [no-fault insurance claim page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

**Other defendants.**

- the shipper or broker that loaded the truck or hired an unsafe carrier
- the store, shopping center, or business park whose dock, yard, or delivery practice caused the crash
- the maintenance contractor
- the manufacturer of a failed component or a defective guard ([product liability](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/product-liability))
- for a government vehicle, the agency that owns it, and for a road defect, the road owner, each with its own notice



**If you were the truck driver or a dock worker.** Workers’ compensation pays your benefits, and your employer usually cannot be sued (MCL 418.131). If you were injured in your employer’s truck, no-fault benefits generally come from the insurer of that truck (MCL 500.3114(3)), coordinated with comp, which is paid first (MCL 500.3109(1)). You can also sue a third party, such as another driver, a shipper, or a store or business park operator that is not your employer. See our [workers’ compensation page](https://atlawgroup.com/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

## The evidence, and how fast it disappears

Carriers must keep two kinds of records for a limited time:

- hours-of-service records, for six months (49 CFR 395.8)
- driver inspection reports, for three months (49 CFR 396.11)

After that, the regulations no longer require them. Other evidence goes faster:

- the cameras at stores, gas stations, and loading docks along Carpenter, Ellsworth, Washtenaw, and Michigan Avenue overwrite in days to weeks
- the truck’s engine control module and event data recorder can be cleared when the truck is repaired and returned to service
- a spilled load is swept up within hours
- a damaged rear guard can be replaced before anyone measures it

The first act in a Pittsfield Township truck case is a **preservation letter.** It goes to the carrier, its insurer, the tractor and trailer owners, and the shipper or broker. Where the crash involved a store or a dock, it also goes to the store and the property’s owner. It demands:

- the ELD and engine data
- dash camera footage
- the driver’s qualification file and post-crash test results
- dispatch records, bills of lading, and delivery records
- maintenance files
- the truck and trailer themselves, for inspection of the brakes, lights, load securement, and rear guard

Once a party knows a claim is coming, destroying that evidence exposes it to spoliation sanctions.

## What to do after a Pittsfield Township truck crash

1. **Call 911 and get medical care the same day.** Trinity Health Ann Arbor, in neighboring Superior Township, and University of Michigan Health in Ann Arbor are Level I trauma centers.
2. **Photograph the truck.** Get these shots: the cab and trailer; the company name and USDOT number on the door; both plates, and the trailer number if it differs; the load and anything that fell from it; the rear guard and reflective tape; and the ramp, driveway, dock, intersection, or crosswalk.
3. **Get the police report.** Freeway crashes are generally worked by the State Police, and crashes on township roads by the Pittsfield Township Department of Public Safety. A serious crash may produce a separate commercial vehicle inspection report.
4. **Note where you were.** A mailing address that says Ann Arbor, Ypsilanti, or Saline does not settle whether the crash was in the township, and that decides the road owner and the district court.
5. **Do not speak to the carrier’s insurer or its investigator.**
6. **Report to your own insurer** and file the Application for No-Fault Benefits promptly.
7. **Call a lawyer within days,** so the preservation letter goes out before the records expire and the truck is repaired.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many truck accidents happen in Pittsfield Township?** The State’s county profile does not break out truck crashes by community. Countywide, 755 heavy trucks and buses were involved in Washtenaw County crashes in 2024. The township recorded 148 crashes on interstate routes and 312 on U.S. routes.

**A semi changed lanes into me on I-94 near the US-23 junction. Who is at fault?** Usually the truck driver, who must make sure a lane is clear before moving into it. A truck has large blind spots on its right side and directly behind the trailer. The truck’s dash camera and engine data are often the deciding evidence, and they are overwritten quickly.

**A delivery truck backed out of a store’s loading dock into me. Who is responsible?** Usually the truck driver and the carrier, and possibly the store or the shopping center’s owner if the dock’s layout or the delivery practice put the truck across a drive aisle or sidewalk.

**A truck hit me while I was walking on Carpenter Road. Who pays my medical bills?** A no-fault policy in your household pays first. If there is none, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays. Your claim for pain and suffering is against the driver and the company.

**Do I have to prove the trucking company was more than 50 percent at fault?** No. Michigan’s 50 percent rule concerns your own share of fault. If you were more than 50 percent at fault, you cannot recover pain-and-suffering damages. Every company that contributed to the crash can be named, whatever its share.

**Is there a cap on what I can recover from a trucking company?** Not on pain and suffering against the driver or carrier. Michigan’s damages caps apply to medical malpractice and product liability claims. In practice, the limit is the insurance available: the carrier’s policy, any excess policy, and your own underinsured coverage.

**How fast can a truck legally go on I-94 or US-23?** Michigan limits trucks over 10,000 pounds to 65 mph on freeways posted at 70 or 75. A truck driving faster than conditions allow can be negligent even below the limit (49 CFR 392.14).

**Why does the lawyer need to act within days?** Because the records expire. Hours-of-service logs must be kept for six months and inspection reports for three. Camera footage lasts days or weeks. The truck’s electronic data and the trailer’s damage can disappear when the truck is repaired.
