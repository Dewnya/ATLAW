## Michigan Uninsured & Underinsured Motorist Claim Lawyer

The driver who hit you had no insurance, or had the $50,000 minimum and your injuries are worth far more. In Michigan that is not unusual. The state’s insurance department estimated that one driver in five was uninsured before the 2019 reform, and in Detroit the estimate was three in five. Michigan also lets drivers carry as little as $50,000 in liability coverage. The protection against both problems is uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage, UM and UIM, which you buy on your own policy and claim from your own insurer.

That claim is not automatic. UM/UIM is optional in Michigan, so the first question is whether you have it. It is a contract, so the policy’s notice deadlines, consent-to-settle clause, and shortened time limits control, and Michigan courts enforce them as written. And your own insurer, once you make the claim, defends it the way the at-fault driver’s insurer would have. This page explains how UM and UIM work in Michigan, how a UIM payment is calculated, the conditions that void claims, what happens in a hit-and-run, and what your options are against the uninsured driver personally.

## What UM and UIM coverage are, and why Michigan makes them optional

Michigan’s no-fault law requires every policy to carry personal injury protection, property protection, and residual liability coverage (MCL 500.3101, 500.3009). It does not require uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage. Insurers offer both as optional endorsements, and many drivers decline them to save a few dollars a month without understanding what they decline.

- **Uninsured motorist (UM) coverage** pays what the at-fault driver’s liability insurance would have paid if the driver had none: your pain and suffering and the economic losses no-fault does not cover. It also applies, under most policies, to hit-and-run crashes where the driver is never identified.
- **Underinsured motorist (UIM) coverage** pays when the at-fault driver has insurance but the limits are lower than your UIM limits and lower than your damages. It fills the gap, up to your UIM limit.

Both are on your own policy, or on a policy of a relative you live with, or on the policy covering the car you were in. If you have none of those, you have no UM/UIM claim, and your options are the no-fault benefits described next and a claim against the uninsured driver personally.

## How no-fault fits: PIP first, UM/UIM for the rest

Whatever the at-fault driver’s insurance, your medical bills, 85% of wage loss (up to $7,455 a month for crashes on or after October 1, 2026), replacement services, and attendant care are paid as no-fault PIP benefits by your own insurer, a household insurer, or, if there is none, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3107, 500.3114, 500.3172). The at-fault driver’s lack of insurance does not affect your PIP benefits.

What it affects is everything PIP does not pay: pain and suffering, wage loss above the monthly cap or beyond three years, and medical expenses above your PIP limit. Those come from the at-fault driver’s liability policy when there is one, and from your UM/UIM coverage when there is not enough. The Assigned Claims Plan pays PIP benefits only; it does not pay pain and suffering. Our [no-fault insurance claim](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance) page covers the PIP side in detail.

## When an uninsured motorist claim applies

- **The at-fault driver had no insurance.** Michigan requires proof of insurance to register a car, but policies lapse, and the 2024 crash reports show it. The police report’s insurance field, a letter from the driver’s listed insurer, and a Secretary of State record establish “uninsured.”
- **Hit-and-run.** Most policies cover a crash caused by an unidentified vehicle, with conditions: a prompt police report, and often “physical contact” between the vehicles. Michigan courts have read physical contact to include indirect contact, such as an object propelled by the fleeing vehicle, but a swerve-to-avoid crash with no contact fails under many policies. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current case law on physical-contact clauses]** Report the crash to police immediately, and note anything that touched your car.
- **The at-fault driver’s insurer denies coverage or is insolvent.** A policy rescinded for misrepresentation, or an insurer in liquidation, can turn an insured driver into an uninsured one. Policy definitions differ on this.

## When an underinsured motorist claim applies, and how it is calculated

UIM applies when the at-fault driver’s liability limit is less than your UIM limit and less than your damages. The calculation is a subtraction, and the policy language decides what is subtracted.

Example: you carry $250,000 in UIM coverage. The at-fault driver carries Michigan’s $50,000 minimum. Your damages are $400,000.

1. You recover the at-fault driver’s full $50,000 limit (policies require you to exhaust it).
2. Your UIM insurer owes your damages up to your UIM limit, minus the at-fault driver’s limit: $250,000 − $50,000 = $200,000 available.
3. Total recovered: $250,000. The remaining $150,000 is uncompensated unless another policy (the car owner’s, your employer’s, a second household policy) applies.

Two consequences people learn too late. If your UIM limit equals the at-fault driver’s limit, say $50,000 and $50,000, your UIM coverage pays nothing. And if the policy subtracts the at-fault limit from the UIM limit (a “limits-to-limits” policy) rather than from your damages, buying $100,000 in UIM against a minimum-limits driver yields $50,000 at most. The declarations page and the UIM endorsement answer these questions, and we read both in the first week.

## The serious impairment threshold

A UM/UIM policy pays what you are “legally entitled to recover” from the at-fault driver, and most Michigan policies write the no-fault threshold into the coverage: pain and suffering is recoverable only for death, permanent serious disfigurement, or a serious impairment of body function (MCL 500.3135). Comparative fault applies as well, so a recovery is reduced by your share of fault and barred above 50% (MCL 600.2959). ## The policy conditions that void UM/UIM claims

Michigan treats an insurance policy as a contract and enforces its terms as written. The Michigan Supreme Court has held that a one-year limitation period written into a UM policy is enforceable (Rory v Continental Insurance Co, 2005) and that Michigan does not apply a “reasonable expectations” doctrine to rescue insureds from clear policy language (Wilkie v Auto-Owners, 2003). The conditions that end claims:

| Condition | What it requires | How claims are lost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Notice | Written notice of the UM/UIM claim within the period the policy sets, sometimes 30 days, sometimes “as soon as practicable” | Treating the crash as the other driver’s problem and notifying your own insurer months later |
| Consent to settle | The UIM insurer’s written consent before you accept the at-fault driver’s limits and sign a release | Signing the at-fault insurer’s release to get the $50,000 quickly; the UIM insurer’s subrogation right is destroyed and the UIM claim with it |
| Exhaustion | Recovery of the at-fault driver’s full policy limit before UIM pays | Settling for less than the limit |
| Contractual limitation period | Suit or arbitration demand within the policy’s period, often one to three years from the crash | Assuming the three-year statute applies to the UM claim |
| Physical contact and police report (hit-and-run) | Contact with the unidentified vehicle; report to police within the period set, often 24 hours | No report, or a no-contact swerve crash |
| Cooperation | Recorded statement, examination under oath, medical authorizations, independent medical examination | Refusing what the policy allows the insurer to require |
| Arbitration clause | Disputes decided by arbitration rather than a jury, under the procedure the policy sets | Filing in the wrong forum, or past the demand deadline |
| Setoffs | PIP benefits paid, workers’ compensation, and amounts from other policies subtracted as the policy provides | Expecting the full limit when the policy subtracts no-fault benefits |

Each of these is in the policy, and each is read in the first week of a UM/UIM case. A lawyer who handles the claim against the at-fault driver without reading the client’s own policy can settle the first claim in a way that ends the second.

## Your own insurer is the opposing party

Once you make a UM/UIM claim, your insurer “stands in the shoes” of the at-fault driver. It will argue that the other driver was not at fault, that you were, that your injury does not meet the threshold, and that your treatment was unrelated. Expect the same adjusting tactics an opposing insurer uses: a recorded statement early, an independent medical examination, and an offer calibrated to the cost of arbitration.

Michigan gives you one specific lever. Under the Uniform Trade Practices Act, a claim not paid within 60 days after the insurer receives satisfactory proof of loss bears 12% penalty interest, and the Michigan Supreme Court has held that for a UM/UIM insured, unlike a third-party claimant, the insurer owes that interest even when it reasonably disputed the claim (MCL 500.2006(4); Nickola v MIC General Insurance, 2017). On a $200,000 UIM claim paid two years late, that is $48,000. Michigan does not recognize a separate bad-faith lawsuit against a first-party insurer; the penalty interest and the contract are the remedies.

## Suing the uninsured driver personally

Michigan’s no-fault law abolishes most lawsuits against insured drivers, but the abolition applies only to drivers who carried the required insurance (MCL 500.3135(3)). An uninsured at-fault driver has no such protection: they can be sued personally for all of your damages, including pain and suffering without the serious impairment threshold and vehicle damage beyond the $3,000 mini-tort limit. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

The obstacle is collection. Most uninsured drivers are uninsured because they could not afford insurance, and a judgment against them is paid slowly or not at all. Two tools help: Michigan suspends the driver’s license of a person who fails to pay a judgment arising from a motor vehicle crash (MCL 257.512, 257.513), which motivates payment plans, and a judgment can be renewed and enforced against wages and assets for years. Whether to pursue the driver personally is a judgment call made with the client, after the UM/UIM and no-fault claims are secured. **[CONFIRM: the firm pursues judgments against uninsured drivers where collectible]**

## Passengers, pedestrians, and family members

You do not have to be driving your own car to have a UM/UIM claim:

- **Passengers** can usually claim under the policy on the car they were in, which covers its occupants, and under their own or a household policy.
- **Pedestrians and cyclists** hit by an uninsured or hit-and-run driver can claim UM under their own or a household policy.
- **Resident relatives** of the named insured are covered under most policies while in any vehicle or on foot.

When two policies apply, the “other insurance” clauses decide which pays first and whether their limits add together. Michigan courts enforce anti-stacking clauses as written, so the answer is in the policies, and sometimes a second policy that looked like a backup pays nothing. ## What to do after a crash with an uninsured or underinsured driver

1. **Get the police report.** It records the other driver’s insurance status, or its absence, and it is required for a hit-and-run claim.
2. **Find your declarations page.** It lists whether you carry UM and UIM and the limits. If you do not know, call your agent the same day. Check household members’ policies too.
3. **Notify your own insurer in writing** that you may have a UM/UIM claim, even before you know the other driver’s limits. Late notice is the first defense.
4. **Do not sign anything from the at-fault driver’s insurer**, and do not accept its policy limits, until your UIM insurer has consented in writing.
5. **Keep treating and keep records.** The threshold and the value of the claim are proved by medical evidence.
6. **Call a lawyer who reads the policy first.** The order of the claims, the consent letter, and the limitation period are set in the first weeks.

## What a Michigan UM/UIM claim is worth

The recovery is the same category of damages you would have recovered from an insured at-fault driver: pain and suffering, loss of enjoyment of life, disfigurement, and economic losses PIP does not cover, up to your UM/UIM limit, less the setoffs the policy provides. The ceiling is the limit you bought, which is why the declarations page is the first document we ask for. Beyond the limit, recovery depends on other policies and on the uninsured driver’s assets.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan uninsured and underinsured motorist claims

**Is uninsured motorist coverage required in Michigan?** No. Michigan requires PIP, property protection, and liability coverage (MCL 500.3101, 500.3009). UM and UIM are optional. Check your declarations page; if it does not list them, you do not have them.

**I was hit by an uninsured driver. Who pays my medical bills?** Your own no-fault insurer, or a household member’s, or the Assigned Claims Plan if there is none. PIP benefits do not depend on the other driver’s insurance. UM coverage is for pain and suffering and the losses PIP does not cover.

**What is the difference between uninsured and underinsured coverage?** UM applies when the at-fault driver has no insurance or cannot be identified. UIM applies when the driver has insurance but less than your UIM limit and less than your damages.

**How much will my UIM policy pay?** Your UIM limit minus the at-fault driver’s liability limit, up to your damages, under most Michigan policies. $250,000 in UIM against a $50,000 driver leaves $200,000 available. If your UIM limit equals the other driver’s limit, UIM pays nothing.

**Can I settle with the other driver’s insurer first?** Not without your UIM insurer’s written consent. Most policies require it, and Michigan courts enforce the requirement. Settling without consent can void the UIM claim.

**The driver fled. Do I have a claim?** Under most policies, yes, if you reported the crash to police promptly and the policy’s physical-contact condition is met. Your no-fault benefits come through your own policy or the Assigned Claims Plan regardless.

**Do I have three years to make a UM claim?** Not necessarily. The three-year statute applies to the lawsuit against the driver. The UM/UIM claim is governed by the policy, and Michigan courts have upheld one-year contractual periods (Rory v Continental, 2005). Read the policy.

**Will my rates go up for using my own UM coverage?** Michigan law prohibits insurers from raising rates or refusing renewal based on a claim for which you were not at fault (MCL 500.2118(2)(a)). **[ATTORNEY CHECK: citation]**

**Can I sue the uninsured driver?** Yes. An uninsured driver has no no-fault tort immunity (MCL 500.3135(3)) and can be sued for all damages. Collection is the practical problem; a license suspension for an unpaid judgment (MCL 257.512) is one tool.

**My insurer is delaying my UIM claim. What can I do?** After 60 days from satisfactory proof of loss, the claim bears 12% penalty interest (MCL 500.2006(4)), and under Nickola v MIC (2017) the insurer owes it even if it disputed the claim in good faith. The claim is then arbitrated or litigated under the policy.
