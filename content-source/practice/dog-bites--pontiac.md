## Pontiac, MI Dog Bite Lawyer

Michigan’s dog bite law is short and strict. If a dog bites you without provocation while you are on public property or lawfully on private property, the owner is liable for your damages, regardless of whether the dog had ever bitten before or whether the owner knew it was dangerous (MCL 287.351). You do not have to prove negligence. In Pontiac the bites happen on the sidewalks and in the yards of the city’s neighborhoods, where many residents walk, in rental homes and apartment complexes, in parks, and on mail, package, and food delivery routes, often by a dog that got loose from an unfenced yard or a broken chain. The victim is often a child, a neighbor out for a walk, a tenant in the same building, or a worker at the door. And the person who pays is usually not the owner: when the owner is insured, the claim is paid by the owner’s homeowner’s or renter’s insurance.

## The statute, and the two defenses

**Strict liability.** Under MCL 287.351 you prove three things. First, a dog bit you. Second, you were on public property or lawfully on private property. That includes the owner’s own property as a guest, a customer, a tenant, a delivery driver, a utility worker, a mail carrier, or a child playing with permission; the statute specifically covers people performing a duty imposed by law or by the postal service. Third, you did not provoke the dog. The dog’s history and the owner’s knowledge do not matter. There is no “one free bite” in Michigan.

**Provocation.** The owner’s main defense. Michigan courts judge it by whether the person’s conduct would provoke a dog, not by what the person intended, so it can be unintentional: stepping on a dog’s tail by accident can be provocation. Petting a dog, walking past a fence, entering a yard with permission, or knocking on a door is not; hitting, teasing, or cornering a dog may be. The witnesses to the seconds before the bite decide it.

**Trespass.** A person unlawfully on private property is not covered by the statute. A child who wanders into a neighbor’s yard is the contested case; a delivery driver or a guest is lawfully present.

**Comparative fault.** Michigan courts have held that the general comparative-fault rule does not reduce a claim under the dog bite statute; the owner’s defenses are limited to trespass and provocation (*Hill v Sacka*, Mich App 2003).

**Injuries without a bite.** A knockdown by a jumping dog, a fall while fleeing a dog, or a bicycle crash caused by a chasing dog is not covered by the statute. It is a negligence claim, and comparative fault applies. The owner’s knowledge of the dog’s tendencies, a dog running at large in violation of the leash rules, or a broken fence or chain are the evidence. ## What Pontiac’s animal ordinance adds

Pontiac’s animal rules are in Chapter 6 of the City Code, with nuisance provisions in Chapter 18. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: verify the current text and section numbers]** As described in published summaries of the code:

- **A broad definition of “owner.”** The ordinance treats as an owner anyone who has a property right in the dog, keeps it on property they own or occupy, has custody or care of it, or knowingly lets the dog and its owner live on their property. That can reach a householder or landlord who is not the dog’s legal owner, for purposes of the ordinance. Under the bite statute, Michigan courts read “owner” more narrowly: the Dog Law’s broader definition, which includes a keeper or harborer, does not apply to the bite statute (*Trager v Thor*, Mich App 1993).
- **Responsibility for known behavior.** An owner can be held responsible for the dog’s prohibited acts where the owner knew or reasonably should have known the dog was likely to engage in them.
- **Prohibited acts.** Running at large, chasing vehicles, injuring or annoying a person, and bringing a dog onto school grounds.

For a bite victim, the ordinance matters in the same way any local rule does:

- **It does not change the statute.** The owner of any dog is strictly liable under MCL 287.351 for a bite, whatever the ordinance says.
- **It is evidence in a non-bite case.** A dog running at large, a dog with a history of complaints, or an owner who should have known, is evidence of negligence in a knockdown or chase injury.
- **It can identify another responsible person.** A householder who kept the dog, or a landlord who knowingly allowed a dangerous dog to stay, may be a defendant under ordinary negligence even when the statute reaches only the owner.
- **It can produce a dangerous-animal finding.** A finding under Michigan’s Dangerous Animals Act (MCL 287.321 to 287.323) is strong evidence in the civil case, but the civil case does not depend on it.

## Who pays in Pontiac

**Homeowner’s and renter’s insurance.** Dog bite liability is covered by the standard homeowner’s policy and many renter’s policies, often with $100,000 to $500,000 in liability coverage. The owner reports the bite, the insurer pays, and the insurer cannot require the owner to give up the dog. That is why a claim against a neighbor or a relative is less of a barrier than it sounds, and why many bite claims settle without a lawsuit. Policies differ on breed and bite-history exclusions, and coverage must be read. An umbrella policy above the homeowner’s limit may also apply, and is checked.

**Rental homes, apartments, and landlords.** About half of Pontiac households rent (ACS 2020–2024), and bites happen in shared hallways, courtyards, yards, and parking lots. A tenant who owns the dog is liable under the statute, but many tenants carry no renter’s insurance, which makes the landlord’s policy the question. A landlord is generally not liable for a tenant’s dog, except where the landlord knew the dog was dangerous and had the ability to have it removed, through the lease or otherwise, and did not (*Szkodzinski v Griffin*, Mich App 1988). A landlord that received complaints about a dog running loose or attacking, and could have enforced the lease, is the case to look for. Where the landlord is the Pontiac Housing Commission, it is a governmental agency, and governmental immunity likely bars a claim against it for a tenant’s dog; the claim is against the dog’s owner. Prior complaints to the management office, pet registration records, and the complex’s pet policy are the evidence, and we ask for them early. In a condominium, the association’s bylaws usually regulate pets, and an association that knew of a dangerous dog and had the power to require its removal may face a similar claim. See our [tenant injury page](/personal-injury/tenant-injuries).

**Delivery and utility workers.** Package and food delivery drivers, meter readers, and home health aides bitten on a Pontiac route have the statutory claim against the owner and a workers’ compensation claim against their employer at the same time. The comp insurer has a lien on the recovery (MCL 418.827(5)). **Postal carriers** are federal employees: their work-injury benefits come under the Federal Employees’ Compensation Act, not Michigan’s Act, and the federal government is repaid from any recovery against the owner (5 USC 8101 et seq, 8132). Gig drivers who are classified as independent contractors may have no comp coverage, which makes the owner’s insurance the main source. See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

**Uninsured owners.** Where the owner rents and carries no insurance, the claim is against the owner personally and is worth what the owner can pay. That is when the search for another responsible party matters: the landlord, a householder who kept the dog, a co-owner or keeper, or a business that kept it on its property (see our [premises liability page](/personal-injury/premises-liability)).

Dog bites cause puncture and crush wounds, torn tendons and nerves, hand and forearm fractures, and facial injuries. Wounds are often left open and treated with antibiotics, and infection is a routine complication. **Children** are often bitten on the face and head. A facial bite on a child can mean scar revision surgeries over years as the child grows, and psychological injury that Michigan law compensates and a counselor should document. A minor’s settlement must be approved by the court, with the funds protected until adulthood (MCR 2.420); for a Pontiac child that is usually the Oakland County Probate Court, on Telegraph Road in the city, or the circuit court where the case is pending. A child has until the later of the ordinary three years or one year after the 18th birthday to sue (MCL 600.5851(1)). The evidence does not keep that long; settle or sue when the plastic surgeon’s projection of future revisions is clear.

**Damages** include medical treatment and future scar revision, lost wages (including a parent’s time off work), pain and suffering, permanent disfigurement, and psychological injury, with no cap. ## What to do after a dog bite in Pontiac

1. **If the dog is still attacking or loose, call 911.** The Sheriff’s Office responds to dangerous-animal emergencies in Pontiac.
2. **Get medical care the same day**, even for a bite that looks minor; the record dated that day is the case.
3. **Identify the dog and the owner**: name, address or unit number, and vaccination status. If the owner refuses, note the address and the dog’s description; the license records can identify the owner.
4. **Report the bite** to Oakland County Animal Control at (248) 858-1090, or to the Oakland County Health Division at (248) 858-1280 if you did not see a doctor, and get the Sheriff’s report number if deputies responded. In a rental, also report it to the landlord or management office in writing and keep a copy.
5. **Photograph the wounds** that day and as they heal, and the place where the bite happened, including any broken fence, gate, or chain.
6. **Get witness names.** Provocation is decided by what witnesses say happened in the seconds before the bite.
7. **Do not give a recorded statement** to the owner’s insurer or accept an early offer; facial scarring is not valued until it has matured.
8. **Ask about the owner’s insurance**, including umbrella coverage, and in a rental, the landlord’s.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does Pontiac have its own animal control?** Animal control for Pontiac is handled by Oakland County Animal Control, whose office is on the county campus in the city: (248) 858-1090. Call 911 if a dog is attacking or still loose; the Sheriff’s Office will respond. If you did not see a doctor, report the bite to the Oakland County Health Division at (248) 858-1280. **[VERIFY]**

**The dog had never bitten anyone before. Does the owner still have to pay?** Yes. Michigan’s statute makes the owner liable regardless of the dog’s history or the owner’s knowledge, as long as you were lawfully present and did not provoke the dog.

**The dog belongs to someone who doesn’t live at the house where it is kept. Who is responsible?** Under the statute, only the dog’s owner; Michigan courts have held that a person who merely keeps or cares for a dog is not an “owner” under the bite statute. That person may still be liable in negligence. The homeowner’s or renter’s policy at the house where the dog lived is often the coverage.

**The owner is my neighbor and I do not want to sue them.** In an insured case the claim is paid by the owner’s homeowner’s or renter’s insurance, not by your neighbor, and the insurer cannot make them give up the dog. Many cases settle without a lawsuit.

**I was bitten by another tenant’s dog. Is the landlord responsible?** Possibly, for a private landlord. The dog’s owner is liable under the statute. A landlord may be liable if it knew the dog was dangerous, for example from earlier complaints, and could have required its removal but did not. A claim against the Pontiac Housing Commission for a tenant’s dog is likely barred by governmental immunity.

**I am a mail carrier and was bitten on my Pontiac route. What are my claims?** Two: federal workers’ compensation under the Federal Employees’ Compensation Act, through the Postal Service, and a claim against the dog’s owner. The federal government is repaid part of its benefits from the owner recovery, so the two must be coordinated.

**My child was bitten on the face. How long do we have?** Until the later of three years or one year after the child’s 18th birthday. The case should still be brought when the medical picture is clear, and a child’s settlement is approved by the court with the funds protected until adulthood.

**What counts as provocation?** Conduct that would provoke a dog, judged by what happened rather than by intent: hitting, teasing, cornering, stepping on it. Petting a dog, walking past it, or knocking on a door is not provocation.
