## Where St. Clair Shores motorcycle crashes happen

**Jefferson Avenue and the Nautical Mile.** Riders use Jefferson along the lake for the water views, and in summer it is crowded with boat traffic, diners, and visitors. The crashes are:

- **A driver turning left** across Jefferson into a restaurant, marina, or launch lot, who misjudges a motorcycle’s speed or never sees it. Under Michigan law, a driver must yield to oncoming traffic before turning left (MCL 257.650).
- **A driver pulling out** of a lot onto Jefferson into the rider’s path. A driver leaving a lot must yield to traffic on the road (MCL 257.652).
- **A vehicle towing a boat trailer,** turning slowly or swinging wide into a marina, with the trailer blocking a lane or cutting across the rider’s path.
- **Drunk drivers** leaving the bars and restaurants, often late at night.
- **Pedestrians crossing** between the parking lots and the restaurants, and a rider who brakes or swerves to avoid them.
- **Gravel, sand, and wet pavement** at marina and launch driveways.

**I-94.** The freeway runs along the city’s west side, with interchanges and service drives at the mile roads. The crashes are:

- drivers changing lanes into a motorcycle in the next lane (MCL 257.642(1)(a))
- drivers merging from a short entrance ramp into a rider
- rear-end crashes in the stop-and-go, and where traffic backs up from an exit ramp
- trucks, with blind spots, wind blast, and debris from loads
- crashes at the ramp terminals and service-drive signals, where drivers turn across the rider’s lane onto the mile roads

In 2025 State Police intersection data, 12 Mile at I-94 and 10 Mile at I-94 were among Macomb County’s ten highest-crash intersections. See our [St. Clair Shores truck accident page](/personal-injury/truck-accidents/st-clair-shores).

**Harper Avenue.** The city’s commercial strip produces the left-turn crash at the mile-road signals and at the store and restaurant driveways in between, where a driver turning in crosses the rider’s lane and a driver pulling out must yield. Rear-end crashes behind turning cars add to it.

**Greater Mack, Little Mack, and the mile roads.** Intersection crashes at the signals and four-way stops, drivers pulling from side streets or backing out of driveways on residential streets, and school-zone traffic.

**The road itself.** A single-vehicle crash caused by a pothole, a broken pavement seam, a raised utility cover, or a loose surface left by road work is a claim against the agency with jurisdiction, under the highway exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1402). In St. Clair Shores there are three possible defendants:

- **MDOT** for I-94 and its ramps: written notice filed with the Court of Claims within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **Macomb County** (the Department of Roads) or the City of St. Clair Shores for Jefferson, Harper, Greater Mack, Little Mack, and the mile roads, depending on the segment, and MDOT or the local agency for the I-94 service drives: written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **The City** for the local streets: written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).

A defect that was readily apparent to an ordinary observer for 30 days or more is conclusively presumed known to the agency (MCL 691.1403). Notice to the wrong agency is not notice.

**Drinking and riding.** 31 percent of Michigan’s 2024 rider deaths involved alcohol. The State Police recorded 55 alcohol-involved crashes of all kinds in St. Clair Shores in 2024, about one crash in twenty-five. ## Who pays a St. Clair Shores rider’s medical bills

**If a car or truck was involved, you have no-fault benefits, but not from your motorcycle insurer, and not first from your own car insurer.** Under MCL 500.3114(5), your Personal Injury Protection benefits come, in order, from:

1. the insurer of the owner or registrant of the motor vehicle involved
2. the insurer of that vehicle’s operator
3. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s operator
4. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s owner or registrant

Priority follows the vehicles’ insurers, not who was at fault. “Involved” does not require contact; a car that cut you off is involved even if it never touched you. If no insurer in that chain exists, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays (MCL 500.3172), with its $250,000 medical cap, unless you owned the motorcycle and it was uninsured (MCL 500.3113(b)). In a crash with an uninsured driver, and with no car policy of your own in the household, the Assigned Claims Plan is often where the claim goes.

The benefits are the ones a driver would get under MCL 500.3107:

- medical expenses, up to the PIP limit on the policy that pays
- 85 percent of lost wages for up to three years, up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year
- $20 a day in replacement services
- attendant care
- mileage

Written notice is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

The first medical limit is set by a policy you did not choose. Since the 2019 reform, the PIP medical level on the policy first in priority pays first; if the driver who hit you chose $250,000, or a lower level, that policy pays up to its limit, and a lower-priority policy with more coverage, such as your own car policy, may then pay the rest. This is why optional first-party medical coverage on a motorcycle policy matters.

**If no car or truck was involved, no-fault does not apply.** Several common crashes produce no PIP claim:

- a slide on gravel or sand at a marina driveway
- a pothole, seam, or raised utility cover on a county road or City street
- a deer or other animal strike with no other vehicle involved
- a low-speed drop in a parking lot

Your bills go to your health insurance and to any medical coverage you bought on the motorcycle policy. Any injury claim is against whoever caused the hazard, if anyone did. A car that forced you to swerve, even without contact, is a vehicle “involved” in the crash, and that changes the answer.

**Your motorcycle.** Your own collision or comprehensive coverage pays for the bike. The mini tort claim does not cover a motorcycle, because a motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle” under the no-fault act (*Nerat v Swacker*, 150 Mich App 61, 1986).

## Your claim against the driver who hit you

For pain and suffering and economic losses beyond no-fault, you can sue the at-fault driver if your injury meets the threshold: serious impairment of body function, permanent serious disfigurement, or death (MCL 500.3135). A serious impairment is an objectively manifested impairment of an important body function that affects your general ability to lead your normal life. It does not have to be permanent (*McCormick v Carrier*, 2010).

Motorcycle injuries often clear that threshold. Common examples include:

- fractures
- road rash requiring grafts
- torn ligaments
- [brain injuries](/personal-injury/brain-injury)
- [spinal cord injuries](/personal-injury/spinal-cord-injury)

You also recover excess economic damages above the PIP limit that applied to you, including lost wages above the monthly cap (MCL 500.3135(3)(c)). You have three years to sue (MCL 600.5805(2)). A fatal crash is brought by the estate through the Macomb County Probate Court. The wrongful death claim also has three years, with an extension for the estate’s personal representative (MCL 600.5852). See our [wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death).

**What the claim is worth depends on the policies.** Some drivers carry only the $50,000 opt-down minimum (MCL 500.3009(5)), and some carry nothing. The recovery above the other driver’s limit turns on your own **uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage**. That coverage must be on the motorcycle policy, because many car policies exclude the insured while riding an owned motorcycle. Your UIM insurer’s written consent is needed before you accept the at-fault driver’s limit. After a drunk-driving crash, the claim against the bar is a separate source, and it has to be coordinated with the UIM claim. See our [uninsured motorist page](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).

**Comparative fault is the fight.** Michigan’s rule is modified comparative fault (MCL 500.3135(2)(b), 600.2959):

- **More than 50 percent at fault:** you recover no noneconomic damages.
- **50 percent or less:** your award is reduced by your share.
- **Exactly 50 percent:** you recover half.

Economic damages beyond no-fault are reduced by your share as well. Insurers argue speed, lane position, lane splitting (illegal in Michigan), and “came out of nowhere.” The evidence that answers them:

- the St. Clair Shores Police report, or the State Police report for a crash on I-94, and witnesses
- the other driver’s phone records
- restaurant, marina, store, and gas-station cameras along Jefferson and Harper
- photographs of the intersection, the driveway, the trailer, the ramp, or the road surface
- the vehicles’ event data recorders
- the rider’s own camera

## The helmet question

Since 2012, Michigan has let riders 21 and older ride without a helmet if they meet two conditions (MCL 257.658):

- They carry at least $20,000 in first-party medical coverage.
- They have either held a motorcycle endorsement for two years or passed an approved safety course.

Riders under 21 must wear a helmet, and a passenger has rules of their own. If you met those conditions, riding without a helmet was legal, so it was not a traffic violation. In a head-injury case, the insurer will argue a helmet would have reduced the injury. That is a damages and causation argument for the jury, not a bar to the claim. In a leg or shoulder case, it is irrelevant.

## When the vehicle that hit you was a police car or a city truck

A crash with a St. Clair Shores Police car, a fire truck, a city or county truck, or a SMART bus is a claim against the agency that owns it, under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405). The agency is the owner of the “motor vehicle involved,” so its insurer, or the agency itself if it is self-insured, is generally first in line for your no-fault benefits under the rider priority rule. Your claim for pain and suffering is against the agency, and an individual officer or employee is personally liable only for gross negligence (MCL 691.1407(2)). Dash camera, body camera, and dispatch records are overwritten on short schedules, so we request them by FOIA promptly.

## What to do after a St. Clair Shores motorcycle crash

1. **Get medical care the same day.** Henry Ford St. John Hospital, just south on Moross in Detroit, is a Level I trauma center, and Henry Ford Macomb Hospital in Clinton Township is a Level II. Corewell Health Grosse Pointe is also nearby.
2. **Get the police report.** Crashes on city streets are worked by the St. Clair Shores Police Department. If a car caused the crash without making contact, make sure the report says so.
3. **Identify every vehicle involved,** including the one that never touched you and any vehicle towing a trailer.
4. **Photograph everything:** the bike, the scene, the intersection, driveway, or ramp, the road surface and any gravel or debris, your gear, and your injuries. Do not repair or sell the bike.
5. **If you think the other driver had been drinking,** tell the officer, and write down anything the driver said about where they had been.
6. **Read your policies.** Check your motorcycle policy for first-party medical, UM/UIM, collision, and comprehensive coverage, and your car policy’s PIP level.
7. **Apply for no-fault benefits with the right insurer promptly.** Do not give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurer.
8. **If a road defect or a bar was involved, call within days.** The 120-day road-defect notice is already running, and the dram shop notice runs from the day you hire a lawyer.

## Frequently asked questions


**A driver turned left across Jefferson into a restaurant lot in front of me. Who is at fault?** Usually the driver turning left, who must yield to oncoming traffic, including a motorcycle. Insurers will argue your speed, so restaurant and marina cameras and witnesses matter, and footage is overwritten within days.

**A car merged into me on I-94. Do I have a claim?** Usually. A driver must make sure a lane is clear before moving into it. The car is “involved” under no-fault whether or not it touched you, so its owner’s insurer is first in line for your medical bills. Your claim for pain and suffering is against the driver. Get the plate and witnesses, because the car may not stop.

**The driver who hit me had been drinking at a bar on the Nautical Mile. Can I sue the bar?** Possibly. A bar that served a visibly intoxicated person can be liable for the injuries that person causes. Written notice must reach the bar within 120 days after you hire a lawyer, and the suit must be filed within two years.

**A police car hit me on my motorcycle. Who pays?** The City is the owner of the police car, so its insurer, or the City itself if it is self-insured, is generally first in line for your no-fault benefits. Your claim for pain and suffering is against the City under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity.

**Does the at-fault driver’s insurance pay my medical bills?** Not because the driver was at fault. Under the rider priority rule, the insurer of the car involved pays first, whoever caused the crash. Fault matters for your separate claim for pain and suffering.

**I have my own car insurance. Doesn’t that cover me on the motorcycle?** Not first. If a car was involved, that car’s insurer is first in line. Your own car insurer is third, after the involved vehicle’s owner’s and operator’s insurers. A motorcycle policy itself does not carry Michigan PIP. If no car was involved, there is usually no PIP claim at all.

**The driver who hit me had no insurance. Who pays my medical bills?** If the car was uninsured, the next insurer in the priority chain pays: the driver’s own insurer, if the driver insures another vehicle; then your own car insurer; then the car insurer of the motorcycle’s owner. If there is none, the Assigned Claims Plan pays, up to $250,000 in medical benefits. Your pain-and-suffering claim depends on uninsured motorist coverage on your motorcycle policy.

**I wasn’t wearing a helmet. Can I still recover?** Yes, if three conditions were met: - you were 21 or older - you carried the required $20,000 in medical coverage - you had either two years of endorsement or a safety course. The insurer can argue a helmet would have reduced a head injury. That goes to damages, not to whether you have a claim.

**How long do I have?** - One year to notify the responsible no-fault insurer. - Three years to sue the at-fault driver, and three years for a wrongful death claim. - 120 days for a notice to Macomb County or the City. - 120 days for a road-defect notice against MDOT. - 120 days for a dram shop notice, and two years to sue a bar.
