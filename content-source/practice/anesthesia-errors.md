## Anesthesia Error Lawyer Michigan

Modern anesthesia is safe when its standards are followed, which is why an injury under anesthesia is so often the result of a standard not followed: a patient not monitored, an airway not secured, a dose not checked, a drug allergy or a sleep apnea history not read, a patient left awake and paralyzed, a surgery center without the equipment to rescue a patient from a complication it should have anticipated. The injuries are among the worst in medicine, hypoxic brain damage, cardiac arrest, nerve injury, and death, and they happen to people who walked in healthy for a routine procedure.

Michigan law generally treats injuries caused by negligent anesthesia care as medical malpractice claims,, which carries rules that no other injury case has: a two-year deadline, a 182-day pre-suit notice, an affidavit from a qualified expert before filing, a same-specialty rule for that expert, and a cap on non-economic damages, with a higher tier for certain qualifying permanent brain and spinal cord injuries that anesthesia errors can cause. The claim also raises a question other cases do not: who is liable when the anesthesia was given by a nurse anesthetist, by a staffing company’s physician, or in a dental office, and whether the hospital answers for them. ## How anesthesia injuries happen

Anesthesia care has three phases, and the errors cluster in each.

| Phase | Error | Typical injury |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Before** (pre-anesthetic evaluation) | Failure to review the history: sleep apnea, obesity, difficult airway, cardiac disease, drug allergies, medications (including GLP-1 drugs that delay stomach emptying), prior anesthesia reactions, family history of malignant hyperthermia; wrong fasting instructions | Aspiration, failed intubation, cardiac event, allergic reaction, malignant hyperthermia |
| **During** (induction and maintenance) | Dose error (wrong drug, wrong amount, wrong patient weight); failed or esophageal intubation not recognized; inadequate monitoring of oxygen, carbon dioxide, blood pressure, and temperature; failure to respond to alarms; inadequate anesthesia with paralysis (awareness); nerve injury from positioning; epidural or spinal needle injury; local anesthetic toxicity | Hypoxic brain injury, cardiac arrest, awareness with recall, paralysis, nerve palsy, dental and airway trauma, death |
| **After** (emergence and recovery) | Premature extubation; inadequate recovery-room monitoring; opioid-induced respiratory depression on the floor; discharge of a sedated patient without a competent adult | Respiratory arrest, brain injury, falls, death |

The American Society of Anesthesiologists’ Standards for Basic Anesthetic Monitoring require qualified anesthesia personnel present throughout every general, regional, and monitored anesthesia case, with continuous evaluation of oxygenation (pulse oximetry), ventilation (capnography for every patient with an airway device), circulation (ECG, blood pressure at least every five minutes), and temperature when changes are expected. An anesthesia record that shows gaps in those measurements, or alarms silenced, is the evidence in most hypoxic injury cases. Awareness under general anesthesia, in which a paralyzed patient is conscious, is estimated to occur in roughly one to two of every thousand general anesthetics and is far more frequent when paralytics are used without adequate anesthetic depth monitoring.

## Who can be liable in Michigan

Anesthesia is delivered by several professionals in several settings, and Michigan law assigns responsibility differently to each:

- **The anesthesiologist**, a physician, for the plan, the supervision, and the care personally delivered.
- **The certified registered nurse anesthetist (CRNA).** Since 2021 Public Act 53 (signed July 2021), Michigan permits qualifying CRNAs to provide anesthesia without physician supervision under specified conditions, including experience or doctoral education and collaborative participation in a patient-centered care team. The statute also permits services under facility policies that ensure a qualified health care professional is immediately available to address urgent or emergent concerns (MCL 333.17210). A hospital or surgery center may be liable for negligent policies or supervision depending on the circumstances. A CRNA is held to the standard of care of a CRNA, and the entity that credentialed, employed, or scheduled the CRNA may also be liable depending on its own negligence or its legal relationship with the provider.
- **The surgeon**, for positioning decisions, for the choice of setting, and for failing to respond to the anesthesia team’s warnings.
- **The hospital or ambulatory surgery center**, for its own negligence (credentialing, staffing, equipment, policies, recovery-room monitoring) and, through vicarious liability, for the anesthesia provider’s negligence. Many metro Detroit hospitals contract anesthesia to outside staffing companies, and the hospital will argue the anesthesiologist was an independent contractor. Michigan’s ostensible agency doctrine answers that argument: a patient who looks to the hospital for care and is assigned a provider by the hospital may hold the hospital liable unless the patient was told and understood that the provider was not the hospital’s agent (Grewe v Mt Clemens General Hospital, 1978). The Michigan Supreme Court reaffirmed and strengthened the rule in Markel v William Beaumont Hospital (2022, and again by order of July 9, 2025), holding that a patient who enters a hospital through the emergency room and is assigned a treating physician need not establish an additional act of reliance to raise a factual question of ostensible agency, unless the patient was advised and understood that the physician was not the hospital's agent.
- **The anesthesia staffing company** that employed the provider.
- **A dentist or oral surgeon** administering sedation or general anesthesia in an office, under the Michigan Board of Dentistry's anesthesia and sedation rules, with the office’s monitoring and rescue equipment a frequent issue. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current dental anesthesia permit rules]**
- **A drug or device manufacturer**, where a pump, vaporizer, monitor, or drug was defective; that claim follows product liability rules.

Identifying every provider in the room, their employer, and the hospital’s contracts is the first task in an anesthesia case, because Michigan’s expert rule, below, is applied provider by provider.

## Michigan’s medical malpractice rules, applied to anesthesia

| Rule | What it requires | Statute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Two-year deadline** | Suit within 2 years of the act or omission; a 6-month discovery period from when the patient discovered or should have discovered the claim; an absolute 6-year repose with narrow exceptions | MCL 600.5805(8), 600.5838a |
| **Notice of intent** | A written notice to every defendant at least 182 days before filing, stating the factual basis, the standard of care, the breach, what should have been done, and the injury; the deadline is tolled during the notice period | MCL 600.2912b, 600.5856(c) |
| **Affidavit of merit** | An affidavit from a qualified expert filed with the complaint, stating the standard of care, the breach, and causation | MCL 600.2912d |
| **Same-specialty expert** | The expert must practice or teach in the same specialty as the defendant, and if the defendant is board-certified, so must the expert be; a claim against a board-certified anesthesiologist generally needs an anesthesiologist board-certified in the same specialty; a claim against a CRNA generally needs an appropriately qualified nursing expert in the same profession | MCL 600.2169 |
| **Lost opportunity** | Where the claim is that the error reduced the chance of a better outcome, the lost opportunity must exceed 50% | MCL 600.2912a(2) |
| **Non-economic damages cap** | $596,400 in 2026; $1,065,000 for qualifying hemiplegia, paraplegia, or quadriplegia causing total permanent functional loss of one or more limbs from brain or spinal cord injury; permanent cognitive impairment preventing independent responsible life decisions and normal daily activities; or permanent reproductive-organ damage causing inability to procreate. Adjusted annually by the State Treasurer. | MCL 600.1483 **[ATTORNEY CHECK: 2026 Treasury figures]** |
| **Comparative fault** | Damages reduced by the patient’s percentage of fault (misstated history, failure to follow fasting instructions) and non-economic damages barred above 50% | MCL 600.2959 |
| **Wrongful death** | Brought by the estate’s personal representative; the same malpractice procedure applies, with a saving provision for the estate | MCL 600.2922, 600.5852 |

Two consequences for anesthesia cases. The cap’s higher tier was written for qualifying catastrophic injuries: a patient left with permanent cognitive impairment or paralysis from a hypoxic event may fall within the $1,065,000 tier if the statutory requirements are met, and economic damages, including lifetime care, are not capped at all. And the same-specialty rule means an anesthesia case is built with anesthesiologists and CRNAs as experts from the start, not with a general surgeon or an internist.

## The evidence: the anesthesia record and the monitors

Unlike most malpractice claims, an anesthesia case is documented minute by minute. The evidence that decides it:

| Evidence | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| The anesthesia record (now usually electronic, with monitor data captured automatically) | Vital signs, oxygen saturation, end-tidal CO₂, drug doses and times, alarms, and the gaps |
| Monitor and anesthesia machine data logs | What the equipment recorded, independent of what was charted; retained for a limited time |
| The pre-anesthetic evaluation and consent | What history was taken, what risk class was assigned, what the patient was told |
| Operating room and recovery-room nursing records | The timeline from a second set of hands |
| The code record, if resuscitation occurred | Minutes of hypoxia, drugs given, response times |
| Staffing and credentialing files, anesthesia contracts, facility policies on supervision | Who was responsible for whom, and whether the hospital answers for the provider |
| Equipment maintenance and drug dispensing records | Device failures and dose errors |
| Incident reports and peer review | Protected from discovery in Michigan in most cases (MCL 333.20175, 333.21515), which is why the primary records matter |

## What to do if you suspect an anesthesia error

1. **Request the complete medical record**, including the anesthesia record and the pre-anesthetic evaluation, in writing, under Michigan’s Medical Records Access Act (MCL 333.26261 and following). You are entitled to it.
2. **Write down what you were told** by the surgeon, the anesthesiologist, and the nurses, and when. Explanations change.
3. **Do not sign a release or accept an apology as a resolution**; Michigan’s apology statute makes expressions of sympathy inadmissible, but statements of fault are not protected (MCL 600.2155).
4. **Get the injured person’s ongoing care documented** by specialists: neurology, physiatry, neuropsychology for cognitive injury.
5. **Call a malpractice lawyer within months, not years.** The two-year period, the 182-day notice, and the expert review that must precede the affidavit of merit together consume most of the time available.

## What a Michigan anesthesia malpractice case is worth

Economic damages, uncapped: past and future medical care, including lifetime attendant care and rehabilitation for a brain injury, lost wages and earning capacity, and the cost of home and vehicle modifications, projected by a life care planner and an economist. Non-economic damages: pain and suffering, loss of enjoyment of life, and a spouse’s loss of consortium, capped at $596,400 or $1,065,000 in 2026 depending on the injury. In a death, the damages recoverable under the wrongful death act, including the family’s loss of society and companionship, subject to the cap. The value of the case is set by the injury and by the number of defendants whose policies respond; an anesthesia case with a hospital, a staffing company, and a provider as defendants may have more potential sources of recovery than a claim against a single provider, depending on liability and available insurance coverage.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan anesthesia errors

**Is an anesthesia injury always malpractice?** No. Anesthesia carries risks that occur without negligence. Malpractice requires proof that the provider departed from the standard of care, usually a monitoring, airway, dosing, or evaluation failure, and that the departure caused the injury, established through a same-specialty expert.

**How long do I have to sue in Michigan?** Two years from the error, or six months from discovery if later, with a six-year outer limit (MCL 600.5805, 600.5838a). A 182-day notice of intent must be served first (600.2912b), so the practical deadline for calling a lawyer is much earlier.

**Can I sue the hospital if the anesthesiologist worked for a staffing company?** Often, yes. Under Michigan's ostensible agency rule, a patient who looked to the hospital for care and was assigned a provider by the hospital may hold the hospital liable when the evidence establishes an ostensible agency relationship (Grewe, 1978; Markel v William Beaumont Hospital, 2022 and 2025).

**Who is responsible if a nurse anesthetist made the error?** The CRNA, the CRNA’s employer, and the facility whose policies governed supervision. Michigan permits CRNAs to provide anesthesia without physician supervision under specified conditions established in 2021, which makes the facility's own policy decisions potentially relevant to the case.

**Is there a cap on damages?** Non-economic damages are capped: $596,400 in 2026, or $1,065,000 for qualifying permanent cognitive impairment, paralysis involving total permanent functional loss of a limb, or reproductive-organ damage causing inability to procreate, when the requirements of MCL 600.1483 are met. Medical expenses and lost earnings are not capped.

**I woke up during surgery. Is that a claim?** Awareness with recall under general anesthesia can be malpractice where anesthetic depth was inadequate, particularly with paralytic drugs, and it causes recognized psychological injury. The anesthesia record and drug doses are the evidence.

**My family member died during a routine procedure. What now?** Request the complete record immediately, including the anesthesia record and any code record, and call a lawyer; a wrongful death malpractice claim follows the same two-year and notice rules, with a saving provision for the estate (MCL 600.5852).

**What is an affidavit of merit?** A sworn statement from a qualified expert, filed with the lawsuit, that the standard of care was breached and caused the injury (MCL 600.2912d). For a board-certified anesthesiologist defendant, the expert generally must be board-certified in the same specialty (600.2169).

**Can the hospital’s internal investigation be used?** Usually not. Peer review and incident reports are privileged in Michigan. The primary records, the monitor data, and depositions are the evidence.

**Does the dentist’s office follow the same rules?** A dentist or oral surgeon who administers sedation or general anesthesia must comply with applicable Michigan Board of Dentistry rules, including monitoring and equipment requirements; an injury caused by negligent office sedation may support a malpractice claim under the same two-year and notice rules.
