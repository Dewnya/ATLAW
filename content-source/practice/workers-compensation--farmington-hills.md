## Where Farmington Hills workers are hurt, and who handles the claim

**Office and engineering campuses.** Office work produces fewer traumatic injuries but real ones: falls on icy campus walks, parking-structure ramps, and stairs; repetitive-strain injuries to wrists, elbows, and necks; back injuries from moving equipment; and, at the technical centers, injuries in test labs, garages, and prototype shops. Two rules matter more here than elsewhere. First, an injury on the way to or from work is generally not covered, but an injury in the employer’s parking lot or structure, or while traveling for work, generally is (MCL 418.301(3)). Second, an injury while working from home can be covered if it arose out of and in the course of the work, and the facts of exactly what you were doing decide it. Many corporate employers are self-insured or use a third-party administrator, and many offer employer-paid short- and long-term disability that is coordinated with comp (MCL 418.354).

**The cap for higher earners.** Comp pays 80 percent of after-tax wages only up to the state maximum, about $1,201 a week for 2026 injuries. For an engineer or manager whose after-tax pay is well above that, the benefit replaces only part of the income. Comp is still the only remedy against the employer, but if someone other than the employer caused the injury, a third-party suit recovers the full wage loss, along with pain and suffering, which comp never pays.

**The hospital, nursing homes, assisted living, and home care.** Patient-handling injuries to backs and shoulders from lifting and transfers; needlesticks and exposures; slips on wet floors; and assaults by patients or residents. Health care workers’ injuries are often cumulative, and the employer’s insurer may argue the condition is degenerative rather than work-related (see “wear and tear” below). Home-care aides injured in a client’s home have a comp claim against their agency and, where a hazard in the home caused the injury, possibly a premises claim against the homeowner.

**Retail, restaurants, and delivery.** Stock, warehouse, and kitchen work along Orchard Lake Road and Grand River: lifting injuries, burns, cuts, and falls, in high-turnover jobs where injuries often go unreported past the 90 days. Delivery drivers and route workers are hurt in crashes and on customers’ property. Some small employers carry no workers’ compensation insurance. An uninsured employer loses the exclusive-remedy protection: the worker may claim comp benefits and may also sue the employer in circuit court, and officers can be personally liable (MCL 418.641, 418.647).

**Light industry, construction, and staffing agencies.** The light-industrial parks off Haggerty, Halsted, and Eleven and Twelve Mile, and the building and road projects around the city: crush and amputation injuries from machinery, forklift strikes, falls from ladders and scaffolds, and struck-by injuries in work zones. Many of these workers are placed by a staffing agency or employed by a subcontractor. A placed worker is the agency’s employee for comp, and the host company may also be protected by the exclusive-remedy bar under Michigan’s economic-reality test, which affects whether it can be sued as a third party; on a construction site, a general contractor or another subcontractor usually can be.

**City, schools, and the college.** Farmington Hills Police, Fire, and Public Services; Farmington Public Schools; and Oakland Community College: public-safety injuries, custodial and maintenance injuries, and assaults. Public employers have pension and duty-disability provisions that run alongside comp, and police officers and firefighters have a statutory presumption for certain heart and respiratory conditions (MCL 418.405).

**Drivers.** Workers hurt in crashes at the I-696/I-96/I-275/M-5 interchange, on Orchard Lake, Twelve Mile, or Grand River while working, which brings Michigan no-fault into the claim (see below). Independent-contractor drivers may have no comp claim; see our [Farmington Hills truck accident page](/personal-injury/truck-accidents/farmington-hills).

## What a Farmington Hills worker is owed

**Medical care** in full, for as long as needed, with no deductible or co-pay (MCL 418.315), including attendant care at home. For the first 28 days the employer chooses the doctor; after that you may treat with your own physician on notice to the employer (MCL 418.315(1)).

**Wage loss** at 80 percent of your after-tax average weekly wage, computed from the highest 39 of the 52 weeks before the injury (MCL 418.351, 418.371), capped at the state maximum. Overtime and shift premiums count toward the average weekly wage, so the pay records matter. Partial wage loss is paid if you return at lower pay (MCL 418.361(1)), and the seven-day waiting period is paid from day one if the disability lasts two weeks (MCL 418.311).

**Specific loss** for a body part or its use, regardless of wage loss, which matters for amputation injuries (MCL 418.361(2)); total and permanent disability for at least 800 weeks (MCL 418.361(3)). **Vocational rehabilitation** up to 52 weeks, extendable to 104 (MCL 418.319). **Death benefits** to dependents for 500 weeks plus burial (MCL 418.321, 418.345), alongside the third-party wrongful death claim on our [Farmington Hills wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death/farmington-hills).

## Where Farmington Hills claims are denied, and how they are won

- **“Disability”** means a limitation in wage-earning capacity in work suitable to your qualifications and training (MCL 418.301(4)); a written job-search log within your restrictions is the evidence (MCL 418.301(5)). For a professional, “suitable” work is measured against your qualifications and training, which is often the dispute.
- **Pre-existing conditions and wear and tear.** Years at a keyboard, on a nursing unit, or on a shop floor produce degenerative necks, shoulders, backs, and knees. The question is whether work contributed to or aggravated the condition in a significant manner (MCL 418.301(2)); the pre- and post-injury records decide it.
- **“It didn’t happen at work.”** Parking-lot, travel, and work-from-home injuries are denied on this ground; the facts of where you were and what you were doing for the employer decide it.
- **Reasonable employment.** Refusing a job within your restrictions without good reason can end benefits (MCL 418.301(9)). Whether an offered job is real and within your limits is a common dispute.
- **The IME** (MCL 418.385): the insurer’s doctor says you have recovered; your treating physician’s records are the answer, which is why the 28-day rule matters.
- **Late notice.** 90 days from the injury or from when you knew it was work-related (MCL 418.381(1)). A verbal report to a supervisor can satisfy the law but is hard to prove; put it in writing and keep a copy.
- **Retaliation** for filing is prohibited (MCL 418.301(13)) and is a separate lawsuit.

## The hearing, and the lawsuit comp does not bar

When an employer or insurer denies a claim or stops paying, the worker files an Application for Mediation or Hearing (Form WC-104A) with the Workers’ Disability Compensation Agency. Disputed claims go to mediation and then to a magistrate, with appeal to the Michigan Compensation Appellate Commission. Oakland County claims are heard at the agency’s Detroit office in Cadillac Place, where the Pontiac hearing site moved on April 1, 2025. A contested claim often takes a year or more, which is why many disputed claims resolve by **redemption**, a lump-sum settlement approved by the magistrate that closes the claim, including future medical care (MCL 418.835). Do not sign one without advice.

Workers’ compensation is your exclusive remedy against an insured employer (MCL 418.131), with one narrow exception: an employer that specifically intended an injury, meaning it had actual knowledge that an injury was certain to occur and willfully disregarded that knowledge (MCL 418.131(1)). You can sue anyone else whose negligence caused the injury (MCL 418.827):

- the other driver in a crash on the job, with workers’ compensation primary and no-fault paying the difference (MCL 500.3109(1)); see our [Farmington Hills car accident page](/personal-injury/auto-accidents/farmington-hills);
- a building owner, property manager, or snow-removal contractor that is not your employer, for a fall at a leased office building or campus ([Farmington Hills slip and fall page](/personal-injury/slip-and-fall/farmington-hills));
- a general contractor or another subcontractor on a construction job;
- the manufacturer of a machine, forklift, lift, or tool with a missing guard or a defective safety device ([product liability](/personal-injury/product-liability));
- a homeowner whose hazard injured a home-care aide or delivery worker;
- a dog owner, for a delivery driver ([dog bite page](/personal-injury/dog-bites)).

The comp insurer has a lien on that recovery (MCL 418.827(5)), reduced by its share of fees and costs; negotiating it is part of the work. The third-party suit is filed in the Sixth Judicial Circuit Court in Pontiac, or the 47th District Court for claims up to $25,000, within three years (MCL 600.5805(2)). A permanent injury may also qualify for Social Security Disability, coordinated with comp under the federal offset (42 USC 424a), and an employer-provided long-term disability plan, usually governed by ERISA, may pay too; see our [Social Security Disability](/disability/social-security-disability) and [long-term disability](/disability/short-long-term-disability) pages.

## What to do after a work injury in Farmington Hills

1. **Report it in writing the same day**, to your supervisor and to HR, and keep a copy, including for a fall in the parking structure.
2. **Get treatment**, with the employer’s designated provider for the first 28 days if required, and tell every provider exactly how the injury happened at work.
3. **Count 28 days.** On day 29, if you are not satisfied with the employer’s doctor, see your own and notify the employer in writing.
4. **If you were placed by a staffing agency, report to the agency too**, and keep the names of both companies.
5. **Keep your pay stubs and W-2s**, including overtime, shift premiums, and bonuses, and, once released with restrictions, a written log of every job you apply for.
6. **Do not give a recorded statement** to the adjuster without advice, and do not sign a redemption agreement without understanding that it ends your medical coverage.
7. **If a machine, a contractor, a driver, a building owner, or a property hazard was involved, call within days.** The third-party evidence, including the machine and the footage, disappears on the same schedule as in any injury case.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long do I have to report a work injury in Farmington Hills?** Ninety days from the injury, or from when you knew it was work-related, to notify your employer, and two years to file a claim. Report it in writing the day it happens.

**I slipped in my employer’s parking structure on the way in. Is that covered?** Generally yes. The going-and-coming rule excludes the commute itself, but an injury in the employer’s parking lot or structure is usually treated as in the course of employment. If the building owner or a snow-removal contractor that is not your employer let the ice build up, you may also have a third-party claim.

**I was hurt working from home. Is that workers’ comp?** It can be, if the injury arose out of and in the course of the work you were doing. The facts decide it, so write down exactly what you were doing and report it the same day.

**My salary is well above the comp maximum. Is that all I get?** From comp, the benefit is 80 percent of after-tax wages up to the weekly maximum. An employer disability plan may add to it, coordinated with comp. If someone other than your employer caused the injury, a third-party suit can recover the full wage loss and pain and suffering.

**I hurt my back lifting patients. Will the insurer call it wear and tear?** Often. The question is whether your work contributed to or aggravated the condition in a significant manner. Your treating physician’s records and a description of the lifting you did are the evidence.

**Can I sue my employer?** Not if it is insured, except in the narrow case where it knew an injury was certain to occur and willfully disregarded that. You can sue any other company or person whose negligence caused the injury, and an uninsured employer directly.

**What does a Farmington Hills workers’ comp lawyer cost?** Nothing up front. Fees are limited by state rule to a percentage of the benefits recovered and must be approved by the magistrate. You owe nothing if no benefits are recovered.
