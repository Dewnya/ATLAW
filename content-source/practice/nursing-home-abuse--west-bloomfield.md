## Understaffing, and the Attorney General’s 2026 lawsuit

Much neglect traces back to staffing. Some residents need two aides to transfer safely, help every two hours to turn, or someone at the table to eat. A short shift cannot give them that care.

Michigan law sets two staffing minimums for nursing homes (MCL 333.21720a):

- at least 2.25 hours of nursing care per resident per day
- minimum staff-to-resident ratios on each shift

Federal rules add two more (42 CFR 483.35):

- a registered nurse on duty at least eight consecutive hours a day, seven days a week
- enough staff to meet each resident’s assessed needs

These numbers are a floor. A facility can meet the minimum and still be short for the residents it actually has.

On July 30, 2026, the Michigan Attorney General announced a civil lawsuit under the Michigan Medicaid False Claim Act. The defendants are a nursing home management company, its owner, and nine nursing homes it manages in southeast and central Michigan: three in Wayne County, and two each in Macomb, Oakland, and Gratiot counties. The State’s announcement and the press coverage identify the homes’ counties, not their cities.

The State alleges that from 2020 to 2025 the homes billed Medicaid more than $111 million while doing the following:

- falling below the 2.25-hour minimum on 1,454 occasions
- missing per-shift ratios at least 496 times
- going without a registered nurse for eight consecutive hours on at least 4,658 occasions

These are the State’s allegations, and the defendants have not had their day in court.

Two points matter for a family:

- **The State’s suit does not compensate any resident.** It seeks repayment and penalties for Medicaid. A resident who was harmed, or the estate of one who died, has a separate claim and a separate deadline.
- **The State’s evidence is the same kind that proves an individual case.** The State relied on staffing records, including payroll-based staffing reports submitted to CMS. Those records also show what the staffing was on the day of the fall or the week the pressure sore developed.

## The rules a West Bloomfield facility must follow

**Federal regulations** (42 CFR Part 483) set the standard of care for every Medicare- and Medicaid-certified facility. A resident has the right to be free from abuse, neglect, exploitation, and restraints used for discipline or convenience (483.12). The facility must also:

- prevent pressure sores unless clinically unavoidable, and treat existing ones (483.25(b))
- keep the environment free of accident hazards and provide the supervision and devices needed to prevent falls (483.25(d))
- maintain nutrition and hydration (483.25(g))
- manage medications without significant error (483.45)
- staff to meet residents’ needs (483.35)

The facility must write and follow a care plan for each resident (483.21). That care plan is the measure of whether it did its job.

**Michigan’s Public Health Code** gives every resident a bill of rights (MCL 333.20201). It includes adequate and appropriate care, freedom from mental and physical abuse, freedom from restraints except on a physician’s order, and notice to a family member or representative of changes in condition. Michigan’s Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs (LARA) licenses and inspects facilities. Michigan law also requires reporting and punishes abuse:

- A facility must report abuse and neglect to the State and investigate (MCL 333.21771).
- Health professionals and others must report suspected abuse of a vulnerable adult to Adult Protective Services (MCL 400.11a).
- Abuse of a vulnerable adult is a crime (MCL 750.145m to 750.145r).

**Assisted living, memory care, homes for the aged, and adult foster care.** Many West Bloomfield seniors live in one of these settings rather than a certified nursing home. LARA licenses them under different rules (MCL 400.701 et seq.; 333.21301 et seq.), and some are not licensed as care facilities at all. They are not subject to 42 CFR Part 483 or the nursing home staffing minimum unless they include a certified nursing unit. A claim against one is measured against the licensing rules, the resident’s service agreement, and ordinary negligence law, so the first question is which kind of place it is.

Many adult foster care homes are licensed for a small number of residents in an ordinary house. A small home can have one caregiver on a shift, and the licensing rules on staffing, supervision, and resident assessment are the measure of its care. LARA posts inspection reports for licensed homes for the aged and adult foster care homes. A family choosing a community should read them as closely as a Care Compare report.

## Short-term rehabilitation after a hospital stay

Many nursing home residents are not long-term residents. They arrive from the hospital after a hip fracture, a stroke, a joint replacement, or a serious infection, for a few weeks of skilled nursing and therapy before going home. Medicare Part A covers a limited skilled stay after a qualifying hospital admission.

A short stay carries its own risks:

- **The handoff.** The hospital’s discharge orders, medication list, and precautions have to reach the facility and be followed from the first shift. A missed anticoagulant, a dropped antibiotic, or weight-bearing restrictions that never made it into the care plan can cause harm in days.
- **Falls in the first days,** when a resident is in a new room, on new medications, and weaker than the family realizes.
- **Infections and wound breakdown** at a surgical site, or a pressure sore that starts during the stay.
- **A rushed or unsafe discharge home,** without the equipment, services, or instructions the resident needs.
- **Missed decline,** when a resident’s condition worsens and the facility does not send them back to the hospital in time.

The claim is the same kind of claim as for a long-term resident, with the same two deadlines. The evidence includes the hospital’s discharge summary, which shows what the facility was told, and the facility’s admission assessment, which shows what it did with it. When a resident goes back to Henry Ford West Bloomfield Hospital on Maple Road, or to another hospital, the readmission note often describes the resident’s condition more plainly than the facility’s chart.

## Multi-level senior living campuses

Some senior communities offer independent living, assisted living, memory care, and nursing care on one campus, sometimes under one operator and one contract. The rules that apply depend on where the resident actually lived and received care:

- **The nursing unit**, if it is Medicare- or Medicaid-certified, is subject to 42 CFR Part 483 and Michigan’s nursing home staffing minimum.
- **Assisted living and memory care** are licensed under different rules, or may not be licensed as care facilities at all.
- **Independent living** is usually a landlord-tenant arrangement, with ordinary premises and landlord duties (MCL 554.139).

A resident who moved between levels, or who should have been moved to a higher level of care and was not, may have a claim that turns on that decision. The contract, the resident’s assessments, and the campus’s own transfer criteria are the evidence.

## What neglect looks like, and where the proof is

- **Pressure sores**, especially on the tailbone, hips, and heels. A stage 3 or 4 sore that developed in the facility is treated under federal rules as the facility’s failure unless the clinical record shows it was unavoidable (42 CFR 483.25(b)(1)).
- **Falls and fractures**, especially in a resident whose care plan called for a two-person assist, a bed alarm, or a low bed. A visitor’s fall is a premises case; see our [slip and fall page](/personal-injury/slip-and-fall).
- **Dehydration, malnutrition, and weight loss.**
- **Medication errors** and sedation for staff convenience.
- **Elopement** by a resident with dementia, including from memory care units and onto busy roads such as Maple, Orchard Lake Road, or the lake roads, or toward a lake.
- **Choking** by a resident whose care plan required supervision or a modified diet at meals.
- **Infections and sepsis** from untreated wounds, catheters, or aspiration, and [brain injuries](/personal-injury/brain-injury) from unwitnessed falls.
- **Physical, sexual, and financial abuse** by staff, other residents, or visitors.

The proof is in the facility’s records:

- the care plan and nursing notes
- incident reports
- wound-care logs and weight charts
- medication administration records
- staffing schedules and time records
- the facility’s report to the State

The resident or an authorized representative has the right to the full chart. We request it in writing early, along with the incident reports and staffing records.

A transfer to Henry Ford West Bloomfield Hospital on Maple Road, or to another hospital, produces the hospital’s admission note. That note often describes the resident’s condition on arrival more plainly than the facility’s chart does. ## When a staff member is charged with a crime

Abuse of a vulnerable adult is a crime in Michigan (MCL 750.145m to 750.145r), and the Attorney General prosecutes cases in nursing homes. A criminal case punishes the individual. It does not compensate the resident, and the family does not have to wait for it.

The civil claim is usually against the facility as well as the employee. A facility has several duties toward its direct-care staff:

- run a criminal background check before hiring them (MCL 333.20173a)
- screen, train, and supervise them
- investigate and report abuse allegations (42 CFR 483.12; MCL 333.21771)

A facility can be liable for negligent hiring, retention, or supervision even when the assault itself was outside the employee’s job. That includes a facility that hired someone it should not have, ignored earlier complaints, or left a resident unsupervised with a known risk.

## Who can bring the claim when the resident cannot

A resident who is competent brings the claim in their own name. Many residents with dementia cannot, and someone else needs legal authority to act for them:

- **A durable power of attorney** may authorize the agent to hire a lawyer and pursue a claim, depending on its terms.
- **A patient advocate designation** covers medical decisions, not lawsuits.
- **If no document gives that authority,** a family member petitions the **Oakland County Probate Court**, on the county campus at 1200 N. Telegraph Road in Pontiac, to be appointed guardian or conservator (MCL 700.5303, 700.5401). The conservator then brings the claim.

The court approves any settlement for a protected person. If the resident has died, the estate’s personal representative brings the claim (see below).

Where a resident’s dementia was severe enough at the time of the injury that they could not understand their rights, Michigan law may extend the deadline (MCL 600.5851(1), (2)). That extension is fact-specific and contested, and a family should not rely on it.

## Negligence or malpractice: the question that sets the deadline

Michigan courts decide whether a nursing home claim is ordinary negligence or medical malpractice by asking two questions. Did the conduct involve professional medical judgment? Would a jury need expert testimony to evaluate it? (*Bryant v Oakpointe Villa Nursing Centre*, Mich 2004.)

- **Malpractice:** a nurse’s wound-treatment decision or a physician’s medication order. See our [medical malpractice page](/personal-injury/medical-malpractice).
- **Ordinary negligence any juror can judge:** leaving a resident who could not swallow alone with a tray, ignoring a care plan that said “two-person assist,” or failing to supervise a resident known to wander.

| | Ordinary negligence | Medical malpractice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deadline | 3 years (MCL 600.5805(2)) | 2 years (600.5805(8)), with the 6-month discovery rule (600.5838a) |
| Pre-suit notice | None | Notice of Intent 182 days before filing (600.2912b) |
| Expert affidavit | Not required to file | Affidavit of Merit with the complaint (600.2912d) |
| Noneconomic damages | No cap | Capped: $596,400 / $1,065,000 in 2026 (600.1483) |

Many nursing home cases include both kinds of claim. We plead both, meet the malpractice requirements for the parts that need them, and preserve the three-year claim for the parts that do not. A family that waits past two years may have lost the malpractice claims. Past three years, it has ordinarily lost everything.

**Wrongful death.** If the resident died, the estate’s personal representative brings the claim under MCL 600.2922. For a West Bloomfield resident, the estate is opened through the Oakland County Probate Court, and the claim follows the deadline of the underlying claim. See our [wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death).

**Medicaid and Medicare.** If Medicaid or Medicare paid for treatment of the injury, it has a right to be repaid from the recovery, and that repayment is resolved before the money is distributed. Michigan’s Medicaid estate recovery program is a separate claim against the resident’s probate estate after death.

## The arbitration clause

Many admission packets include an agreement to arbitrate. A certified facility must follow three rules (42 CFR 483.70(m)):

- It may not require the agreement as a condition of admission.
- It must explain the agreement in a way the resident or representative understands.
- It must allow the agreement to be rescinded within 30 days.

An agreement signed by a family member without legal authority to bind the resident, such as a power of attorney or guardianship, is generally not enforceable against the resident or the estate. Assisted living agreements are not covered by the federal rule and must be read on their own terms. An enforceable agreement changes where the claim is heard, not the claim itself.

## Frequently asked questions

**Who do I call today about neglect in a West Bloomfield nursing home?** Adult Protective Services at 855-444-3911, LARA at 800-882-6006, and the Long-Term Care Ombudsman at 866-485-9393. Call 911 if the resident is in danger.

**The home always seems short-staffed. Is that neglect?** Understaffing is not a claim by itself, but it is usually the reason a resident falls, develops a pressure sore, or goes without food or water. Michigan requires at least 2.25 hours of nursing care per resident per day. The facility’s staffing records show whether it met that on the day your parent was hurt.

**I read online that a West Bloomfield nursing home has many violations, or saw it on a lawyer’s list. Does that prove my case?** No. A violation count is a starting point, not proof. Inspection reports show what the State found and whether it was corrected. Your parent’s claim depends on what happened to your parent: the chart, the care plan, and the staffing that day.

**The Attorney General sued a nursing home company in July 2026. Does that cover my mother’s injury?** No. That suit seeks repayment and penalties for Medicaid. An injured resident, or the estate of one who died, has a separate claim with its own deadline.

**My mother has dementia and cannot make decisions. Can we still bring a claim?** Yes, through someone with legal authority. That is either an agent under a power of attorney that covers claims, or a conservator appointed by the Oakland County Probate Court. We help the family get that authority if it is missing.

**How long do we have?** Two years for claims involving medical judgment, and three years for ordinary negligence. Many cases include both, so call before two years have passed.

**We signed an arbitration agreement at admission. Can we still sue?** Possibly. At a certified facility, the agreement cannot have been a condition of admission, and the signer must have had authority to bind the resident. It can also be rescinded within 30 days. Even if it is enforceable, it moves the claim rather than ending it.

**My father is in an assisted living or adult foster care home in West Bloomfield, not a nursing home. Do the same rules apply?** No. Assisted living, memory care, homes for the aged, and adult foster care homes are licensed under different Michigan rules, and some are not licensed as care facilities at all. The nursing home staffing minimum does not apply to them. A claim against one is measured against those rules, the service agreement, and ordinary negligence law.

**My mother was only there for rehab after the hospital. Does that change anything?** No. A short-term rehabilitation resident has the same rights and the same claim as a long-term resident, with the same two deadlines. The hospital’s discharge orders and the facility’s admission assessment show whether the facility followed what it was told.

**My father lives on a campus with independent living, assisted living, and a nursing unit. Which rules apply?** The rules for the part of the campus where he lived and received care when he was hurt. The nursing unit follows the federal and Michigan nursing home rules; assisted living and independent living do not. If he needed a higher level of care and was not moved, that decision can be part of the claim.

**I found a “West Brookfield” or “Bloomfield” nursing home lawyer page from another state. Do those rules apply here?** No. Some pages that come up for West Bloomfield are written for West Brookfield, Massachusetts, or Bloomfield, New Mexico, and describe another state’s law and agencies. In Michigan, a claim involving medical judgment has two years and a 182-day notice, and an ordinary negligence claim has three years. Reports go to Michigan Adult Protective Services, LARA, and the Long-Term Care Ombudsman.

**How do I check a facility’s record?** CMS Care Compare shows each certified nursing home’s star ratings, staffing, inspection results, and penalties. LARA’s inspection reports cover licensed homes for the aged and adult foster care homes.
