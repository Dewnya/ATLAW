## Macomb Township, MI Motorcycle Accident Lawyer

A motorcycle crash in Macomb Township is governed by a rule many riders learn only after they are hurt: under Michigan no-fault, a motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle.” That definition decides:

- who pays your medical bills, and how much they pay
- whether you have a no-fault claim at all
- how the insurer will argue the helmet question

Macomb Township adds its own risks:

- **Hall Road (M-59),** where left turns are made by Michigan left, and a driver making the U-turn at a crossover pulls into the lanes a rider is in.

- **Deer,** at dawn and dusk, on every one of those roads.
- **Two road owners,** MDOT and Macomb County, each with its own notice clock when the road itself caused the crash. The township owns none.

## Where Macomb Township motorcycle crashes happen

**Hall Road and the Michigan lefts.** On Hall Road, a driver who wants to turn left passes the intersection and makes a U-turn at a median crossover. The recurring rider crash is a driver making that U-turn who pulls into the far lanes in front of a motorcycle, misjudging its speed or never seeing it. A driver entering traffic from a crossover must yield to traffic in the lanes being entered. Hall Road also produces:

- red-light and late-yellow crashes at the signals at Hayes, Garfield, Romeo Plank, and Card
- drivers pulling out of strip-center driveways into the rider’s lane (MCL 257.652)
- lane changes into a motorcycle in the next lane (MCL 257.642(1)(a))
- rear-end crashes where traffic stacks up for a crossover
- trucks, with blind spots and wind blast

In the State Police’s 2025 intersection data, Hall Road at Hayes had 67 crashes, seventh in Macomb County. Hall Road belongs to MDOT.

**The arterials, roundabouts, and subdivision entrances.** On Romeo Plank, Garfield, Hayes, Card, and North Avenue, the crashes are:

- **A driver turning left** into a subdivision entrance or a school lot, across the rider’s path. Under Michigan law, a driver must yield to oncoming traffic before turning left (MCL 257.650).
- **A driver entering a roundabout** without yielding to a motorcycle already in it, or changing lanes inside a two-lane roundabout into a rider.
- **Rear-end crashes** in the school-hour stop-and-go.

**The mile roads.** Some segments of 21 through 26 Mile are five lanes, and some are two lanes with ditches and no shoulders. For a rider, the hazards are the road surface and the road edge:

- gravel, sand, and mud on the pavement at construction-site entrances, and gravel spilled from trucks
- the pavement edge dropping into a ditch, with no shoulder to recover on
- potholes and breakup where heavy construction trucks run on roads not built for them
- a driver pulling out from a stop sign at an unsignalized crossing
- a construction truck crossing the center line on a narrow road

**Deer.** The State Police recorded 63 deer-involved crashes in Macomb Township in 2024, more than in Sterling Heights (50), Shelby Township (61), or Clinton Township (30). For a rider, a deer strike is often a fall, and often a serious one. It is also, in most cases, a crash with no no-fault claim (see below).

**The road itself.** A single-vehicle crash caused by a pothole, a broken pavement seam, a drop-off in the traveled lanes, or a loose surface left by road work is a claim against the agency with jurisdiction, under the highway exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1402). Townships do not own public roads, so in Macomb Township there are two possible road defendants:

- **MDOT** for Hall Road: written notice filed with the Court of Claims within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **Macomb County** (the Department of Roads) for every other public road: written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).

A defect that was readily apparent to an ordinary observer for 30 days or more is conclusively presumed known to the agency (MCL 691.1403). A notice sent to Macomb Township is sent to the wrong defendant. Gravel or mud left by a construction site or a truck is a claim against the hauler or the site contractor, not the road agency. See our [Macomb Township truck accident page](/personal-injury/truck-accidents/macomb-township).

**Drinking and riding.** 31 percent of Michigan’s 2024 rider deaths involved alcohol. The State Police recorded 33 alcohol-involved crashes of all kinds in Macomb Township in 2024. A bar or restaurant that over-serves a driver who then hits a rider can be liable under the dram shop act. The notice is due within 120 days after you hire a lawyer, and suit within two years (MCL 436.1801(3)). See our [drunk driving accident page](/personal-injury/drunk-driving-accidents).

## Who pays a Macomb Township rider’s medical bills

**If a car or truck was involved, you have no-fault benefits, but not from your motorcycle insurer, and not first from your own car insurer.** Under MCL 500.3114(5), your Personal Injury Protection benefits come, in order, from:

1. the insurer of the owner or registrant of the motor vehicle involved
2. the insurer of that vehicle’s operator
3. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s operator
4. the motor vehicle insurer of the motorcycle’s owner or registrant

Priority follows the vehicles’ insurers, not who was at fault. “Involved” does not require contact; a car that cut you off is involved even if it never touched you. If no insurer in that chain exists, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays (MCL 500.3172), with its $250,000 medical cap, unless you owned the motorcycle and it was uninsured (MCL 500.3113(b)). In a crash with an uninsured driver, and with no car policy of your own in the household, the Assigned Claims Plan is often where the claim goes.

The benefits are the ones a driver would get under MCL 500.3107:

- medical expenses, up to the PIP limit on the policy that pays
- 85 percent of lost wages for up to three years, up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year
- $20 a day in replacement services
- attendant care
- mileage

Written notice is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

The first medical limit is set by a policy you did not choose. Since the 2019 reform, the PIP medical level on the policy first in priority pays first; if the driver who hit you chose $250,000, or a lower level, that policy pays up to its limit, and a lower-priority policy with more coverage, such as your own car policy, may then pay the rest. This is why optional first-party medical coverage on a motorcycle policy matters.

**If no car or truck was involved, no-fault does not apply.** This is where a rider’s claim differs most from a driver’s. A driver who hits a deer has PIP benefits from their own policy. A rider who hits a deer, with no motor vehicle involved, generally has none. The same is true of:

- a deer strike or a fall avoiding a deer, with no other vehicle involved
- a slide on gravel, sand, or mud on a mile road or at a construction entrance
- a pothole, a pavement drop-off, or a broken seam
- a low-speed drop in a parking lot

Your bills go to your health insurance and to any medical coverage you bought on the motorcycle policy. Any injury claim is against whoever caused the hazard, if anyone did: the hauler or contractor that left gravel or mud on the road, or the road agency for a defect. A car or truck that forced you to swerve, even without contact, is a vehicle “involved” in the crash, and that changes the answer.

**Your motorcycle.** Your own collision or comprehensive coverage pays for the bike. The mini tort claim does not cover a motorcycle, because a motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle” under the no-fault act (*Nerat v Swacker*, 150 Mich App 61, 1986).

## Your claim against the driver who hit you

For pain and suffering and economic losses beyond no-fault, you can sue the at-fault driver if your injury meets the threshold: serious impairment of body function, permanent serious disfigurement, or death (MCL 500.3135). A serious impairment is an objectively manifested impairment of an important body function that affects your general ability to lead your normal life. It does not have to be permanent (*McCormick v Carrier*, 2010).

Motorcycle injuries often clear that threshold. Common examples include:

- fractures
- road rash requiring grafts
- torn ligaments
- [brain injuries](/personal-injury/brain-injury)
- [spinal cord injuries](/personal-injury/spinal-cord-injury)

You also recover excess economic damages above the PIP limit that applied to you, including lost wages above the monthly cap (MCL 500.3135(3)(c)). **You have three years to sue** (MCL 600.5805(2)). A fatal crash is brought by the estate through the Macomb County Probate Court. The wrongful death claim also has three years, with an extension for the estate’s personal representative (MCL 600.5852). See our [Macomb Township wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death/macomb-township).

**What the claim is worth depends on the policies.** Some drivers carry only the $50,000 opt-down minimum (MCL 500.3009(5)), and some carry nothing. The recovery above the other driver’s limit turns on your own **uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage**. That coverage must be on the motorcycle policy, because many car policies exclude the insured while riding an owned motorcycle. Your UIM insurer’s written consent is needed before you accept the at-fault driver’s limit. See our [uninsured motorist page](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).

**Comparative fault is the fight.** Michigan’s rule is modified comparative fault (MCL 500.3135(2)(b), 600.2959):

- **More than 50 percent at fault:** you recover no noneconomic damages.
- **50 percent or less:** your award is reduced by your share.
- **Exactly 50 percent:** you recover half.

Economic damages beyond no-fault are reduced by your share as well. Insurers argue speed, lane position, lane splitting (illegal in Michigan), and “came out of nowhere.” The evidence that answers them:

- the Macomb County Sheriff’s Office crash report and witnesses
- the other driver’s phone records
- store and gas-station cameras along Hall Road, and doorbell cameras near subdivision entrances
- photographs of the crossover, the roundabout, the intersection, the gravel or mud, or the road edge
- the vehicles’ event data recorders
- the rider’s own camera

## The helmet question

Since 2012, Michigan has let riders 21 and older ride without a helmet if they meet two conditions (MCL 257.658):

- They carry at least $20,000 in first-party medical coverage.
- They have either held a motorcycle endorsement for two years or passed an approved safety course.

Riders under 21 must wear a helmet, and a passenger has rules of their own. If you met those conditions, riding without a helmet was legal, so it was not a traffic violation. In a head-injury case, the insurer will argue a helmet would have reduced the injury. That is a damages and causation argument for the jury, not a bar to the claim. In a leg or shoulder case, it is irrelevant.

## When the vehicle that hit you was a Sheriff’s car, a county truck, or a school bus

Macomb Township’s police services are provided by the Macomb County Sheriff’s Office. A crash with a Sheriff’s patrol car or a Department of Roads truck is a claim against Macomb County. A crash with a township fire vehicle is a claim against the township, and a school bus crash is a claim against the district. Each falls under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405).

The agency is the owner of the “motor vehicle involved,” so its insurer, or the agency itself if it is self-insured, is generally first in line for your no-fault benefits under the rider priority rule. An individual officer or employee is personally liable only for gross negligence (MCL 691.1407(2)). Dash camera, body camera, and dispatch records are overwritten on short schedules, so we request them by FOIA promptly.

## What to do after a Macomb Township motorcycle crash

1. **Get medical care the same day.** Henry Ford Macomb Hospital in Clinton Township, just south of Hall Road, and McLaren Macomb in Mount Clemens are both Level II trauma centers.
2. **Get the police report.** Crashes in the township are worked by the Macomb County Sheriff’s Office. If a car caused the crash without making contact, make sure the report says so.
3. **Identify every vehicle involved,** including the one that never touched you and any truck that left gravel or mud on the road.
4. **Photograph everything:** the bike, the scene, the crossover, roundabout, or intersection, the road surface and any gravel, mud, or debris, the nearest construction entrance, your gear, and your injuries. Do not repair or sell the bike.
5. **Read your policies.** Check your motorcycle policy for first-party medical, UM/UIM, collision, and comprehensive coverage, and your car policy’s PIP level.
6. **Apply for no-fault benefits with the right insurer promptly, if a motor vehicle was involved.** Do not give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurer.
7. **If a road defect, a construction site, or a bar was involved, call within days.** The 120-day road-defect notice is already running, the dram shop notice runs from the day you hire a lawyer, and gravel and mud are cleaned up quickly.

## Frequently asked questions


**A driver making a Michigan left on Hall Road pulled out in front of me. Who is at fault?** Usually the driver making the U-turn, who must yield to traffic in the lanes being entered, including a motorcycle. Insurers will argue your speed, so store cameras and witnesses matter, and footage is overwritten within days.

**I hit a deer on my motorcycle on 25 Mile. Does no-fault pay?** Usually not, if no car or truck was involved. A motorcycle is not a “motor vehicle” under no-fault, so a deer strike with no other vehicle generally produces no PIP claim. Your health insurance and any medical coverage on your motorcycle policy pay instead, and comprehensive coverage pays for the bike. If a car forced you off the road, or hit you after you went down, the answer changes.

**I slid on gravel or mud near a construction site. Is anyone responsible?** Possibly the trucking company that spilled the load or the site contractor that let mud and gravel be tracked onto the road. A site’s soil erosion permit generally requires controls to keep material off the road. Photograph the road, the material, and the site entrance before they are cleaned up.

**I was hit in a roundabout. Who is at fault?** Usually the driver entering the roundabout, who must yield to traffic already in it, including a motorcycle.

**How long do I have to sue after a motorcycle crash?** Three years against the at-fault driver (MCL 600.5805(2)), and three years for a wrongful death claim, with an extension for the estate. Shorter deadlines apply to other parts of the claim: one year to notify the no-fault insurer, 120 days for a notice to Macomb County, 120 days for a road-defect notice against MDOT, and 120 days for a dram shop notice, with two years to sue a bar.

**Does the at-fault driver’s insurance pay my medical bills?** Not because the driver was at fault. Under the rider priority rule, the insurer of the car involved pays first, whoever caused the crash. Fault matters for your separate claim for pain and suffering.

**I have my own car insurance. Doesn’t that cover me on the motorcycle?** Not first. If a car was involved, that car’s insurer is first in line. Your own car insurer is third, after the involved vehicle’s owner’s and operator’s insurers. A motorcycle policy itself does not carry Michigan PIP. If no car was involved, there is usually no PIP claim at all.

**A Sheriff’s patrol car hit me. Who pays?** Macomb County owns the patrol car, so its insurer, or the county itself if it is self-insured, is generally first in line for your no-fault benefits. Your claim for pain and suffering is against the county under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity.

**I wasn’t wearing a helmet. Can I still recover?** Yes, if three conditions were met: - you were 21 or older - you carried the required $20,000 in medical coverage - you had either two years of endorsement or a safety course. The insurer can argue a helmet would have reduced a head injury. That goes to damages, not to whether you have a claim.

**My address says “Macomb, MI.” Is this page for me?** Yes. “Macomb, MI” is the mailing address for most of Macomb Township, and the township’s roads, police, and courts described here apply. If your crash happened elsewhere in Macomb County, the no-fault and liability rules are the same, but the road agency, police department, and district court may differ.
