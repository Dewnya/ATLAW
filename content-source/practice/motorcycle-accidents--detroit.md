## Where Detroit motorcycle crashes happen

**The left-turn crash on the arterials.** Woodward, Gratiot, Grand River, Michigan Avenue, Jefferson, Fort, Van Dyke, Livernois, Seven Mile, and Eight Mile: wide, multi-lane, with long signal spacing and a car turning left across the rider’s lane at nearly every block. A driver who says they did not see an approaching motorcycle may still have failed to yield. Michigan law requires drivers making left turns to yield to qualifying oncoming traffic under MCL 257.650. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

**The freeways and interchanges.** I-75, I-94, I-96, the Lodge, the Southfield Freeway, and the Davison, and the interchanges where they meet, shared with the freight traffic described on our [Detroit truck accident page](/personal-injury/truck-accidents/detroit). Lane-change crashes by drivers who did not check the mirror, rear-end crashes in stopped traffic, and single-vehicle crashes on the curves, the grooved pavement, and the expansion joints of the older freeways.

**The road itself.** Detroit’s streets produce the single-vehicle crashes that no-fault does not cover at all: potholes, pavement drop-offs at resurfacing edges, steel plates, loose gravel, and manhole covers below grade. A rider injured by a qualifying defect in the traveled roadway may have a claim against the responsible road agency under the highway exception to governmental immunity, MCL 691.1402. For a state highway claim, the notice must be filed in the Court of Claims. The exception covers the traveled portion of the road and requires that the agency knew or should have known of the defect in time to repair it. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: road jurisdiction within Detroit; highway exception standard]**

**The riverfront and Belle Isle.** Jefferson Avenue and the Belle Isle bridge carry weekend and evening riding traffic. Belle Isle has been a state park since 2014, so a crash caused by a road or premises condition on the island is a claim against the Michigan Department of Natural Resources in the Court of Claims, with a six-month notice, not a claim against the City.

**Downtown and the bar districts.** Greektown, Corktown, the stadium district, and the casinos produce the late-night cases: an intoxicated driver striking a rider, or a rider who had been drinking. Thirty-one percent of Michigan’s 2024 rider deaths involved alcohol. A bar that over-served a visibly intoxicated driver who then hit a rider can be liable under the dram shop act (MCL 436.1801, 120-day notice). ## Who pays a Detroit rider’s medical bills

If a car or truck was involved, you may qualify for no-fault benefits under Michigan’s special motorcycle priority rules. These benefits generally come from an applicable automobile policy, not ordinary motorcycle liability insurance. Under MCL 500.3114(5), your Personal Injury Protection benefits come, in this order, from the insurer of the owner of the motor vehicle involved, then the insurer of that vehicle’s driver, then your own auto insurer if you have a car policy, then the insurer of your motorcycle. “Involved” does not require contact; a car that cut you off and caused you to go down is involved even if it never touched you, which is why identifying that car matters. If no applicable PIP coverage is available under the statutory priority rules, an eligible motorcyclist may claim benefits through the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan, generally subject to a $250,000 PIP medical limit. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: MACP eligibility for motorcyclists]**

The benefits are the same ones a driver would get under MCL 500.3107: medical expenses up to the PIP limit on the policy that pays, 85 percent of lost wages for up to three years up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each October, $20 a day in replacement services, attendant care, and mileage. Written notice to the responsible insurer is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

The medical limit depends on the available policies. Under Michigan’s motorcycle no-fault priority rules, the insurer first in priority generally pays covered medical benefits, subject to its PIP medical limit. But that limit does not necessarily determine the rider’s total available benefits. In a March 2026 decision, the Michigan Court of Appeals held that a motorcyclist who exhausts a higher-priority insurer’s $250,000 PIP medical coverage may seek additional benefits from a lower-priority insurer providing greater coverage, including unlimited coverage. PIP opt-outs, exclusions, available insurance policies, and aggregate limits must also be considered under MCL 500.3107c and 500.3114. Optional first-party medical coverage on a motorcycle policy may provide additional protection, but it is separate from statutory automobile PIP benefits and should be evaluated alongside all potentially available coverage.

If no motor vehicle was involved, a motorcycle-only accident generally does not qualify for statutory automobile PIP benefits. A single-vehicle crash, a pothole, a deer on the Davison, gravel in a curve: there is no PIP claim. Your bills go to your health insurance and to any medical coverage you bought on the motorcycle policy, and a negligence or product-liability claim may be available against a responsible road agency, property owner, contractor, or manufacturer, depending on the circumstances (see our [product liability page](/personal-injury/product-liability)).

**Your motorcycle.** Damage to your motorcycle may be covered by collision insurance on your motorcycle policy. Because a motorcycle is not a "motor vehicle" under Michigan’s no-fault law, the ordinary $3,000 mini-tort remedy generally does not apply to motorcycle damage. Other property-damage claims may depend on the circumstances, available insurance, and applicable liability rules. Keep the motorcycle, helmet, and protective gear available for inspection before repairs or disposal.

## Your claim against the driver who hit you

A pain-and-suffering claim against a legally responsible driver generally requires death, serious impairment of body function, or permanent serious disfigurement under MCL 500.3135. Certain economic losses beyond available no-fault benefits may be recoverable separately under MCL 500.3135(3)(c). Motorcycle crashes can cause serious injuries, including fractures, extensive road rash, ligament and tendon injuries, traumatic brain injuries, and spinal cord injuries. Whether an injury meets Michigan’s legal threshold depends on its documented effects on the injured person’s body functions and normal life. You also recover **excess economic damages**: medical bills above the PIP limit that applied to you, wage loss above the cap or after three years, and future care (MCL 500.3135(3)(c)). The deadline is three years (MCL 600.5805(2)).

**What the claim is worth in Detroit depends on the other driver’s policy.** An at-fault driver may have limited liability coverage or no insurance, making available policies important to investigate. Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage may provide additional recovery, but eligibility depends on the specific insurance policies and endorsements. Motorcycle policies may offer UM/UIM coverage, while automobile policies may exclude injuries sustained while riding an owned motorcycle. Both policies should be reviewed before deciding what coverage is available. If the driver fled, which is common in Detroit, the UM claim also requires a prompt police report and notice to your insurer per the policy. See our [uninsured motorist page](/personal-injury/uninsured-motorist-claims).

**Comparative fault is the fight.** If you were more than 50 percent at fault you recover no noneconomic damages; below that your award is reduced by your share (MCL 500.3135(2)(b), 600.2959). Detroit insurers argue speed, lane position, lane splitting (illegal in Michigan), and “came out of nowhere.” The answer is evidence: the UD-10, witnesses, the other driver’s phone records, Project Green Light and business camera footage, and the rider’s own helmet or bike camera. A person struck after dismounting a motorcycle may fall under different no-fault priority rules than someone operating or riding the motorcycle. Whether the person is treated as a motorcycle occupant or a pedestrian depends on the circumstances. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: dismounted rider under 3114(5) vs 3115]**

## The helmet question in a Detroit case

Since 2012, Michigan has let riders 21 and older ride without a helmet if the applicable first-party medical coverage requirements are satisfied, generally at least $20,000 for the operator and additional coverage for a passenger when required and have either held a motorcycle endorsement for two years or passed an approved safety course (MCL 257.658). If you lawfully rode without a helmet under Michigan’s exemption, the lack of a helmet is not itself a violation of the helmet statute. Insurers may nevertheless dispute whether helmet use affected the nature or severity of an injury. Such arguments require case-specific legal and medical evidence and do not automatically defeat the claim. If you rode without a helmet when the law required one, the violation may become relevant to the case. Whether it affects damages depends on the applicable legal rules and evidence connecting the violation to the injury; it does not automatically bar recovery. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

## What to do after a Detroit motorcycle crash

1. **Get medical care the same day.** Detroit Receiving Hospital and Henry Ford Hospital are Level I adult trauma centers. DMC Sinai-Grace is a Level II trauma center, while Harper University Hospital and Henry Ford St. John Hospital are additional regional treatment options. **[CONFIRM: hospital list]** Tell the provider it was a motorcycle crash.
2. **Get the police report.** Detroit Police response to crashes without serious injury can be slow; if no officer responds, file at the precinct. The UD-10 is purchased for $15 at michigan.gov/tcps. If a car caused the crash without contact, make sure the report says so.
3. **Identify every vehicle involved**, including the one that never touched you. The involved motor vehicle’s insurance may be first in priority for PIP benefits, subject to the statutory rules and applicable coverage.
4. **Photograph the bike, the scene, the road surface, your gear, and your injuries.** Do not repair or sell the bike.
5. **Read your motorcycle policy for first-party medical, UM/UIM, and collision coverage**, and your car policy’s PIP level. These decide what the claim is worth.
6. **Apply for no-fault benefits with the right insurer within the first week**, not the eleventh month, and do not give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurer.
7. **If a road defect was involved, call within days**; the City’s 120-day notice is short, and the defect will be patched.

## The courts

Motorcycle injury lawsuits seeking more than $25,000 may be filed in the Third Judicial Circuit Court, depending on jurisdiction and venue. Smaller qualifying civil claims may proceed in the 36th District Court. The ordinary automobile mini-tort remedy generally does not apply to motorcycle damage. Qualifying highway-defect claims against MDOT, and other actionable claims against state agencies, generally proceed in the Michigan Court of Claims, subject to governmental immunity and applicable notice requirements. Wrongful death cases are brought by the estate’s personal representative (MCL 600.2922) with the Wayne County Probate Court’s approval of any distribution; see our [Detroit wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death/detroit).

## Frequently asked questions

**How many motorcycle accidents happen in Detroit?**  **[CONFIRM: data: Detroit and Wayne County 2024 motorcycle figures]** Statewide, 3,187 motorcycle crashes in 2024 injured 2,460 riders and killed 168, according to the Michigan State Police. County figures for all crash types are on our [Wayne County page](/personal-injury/personal-injury/wayne-county).

**A car cut me off in Detroit and I went down, but it never hit me. Do I have a claim?** Potentially, yes. Physical contact is not always necessary for a motor vehicle to be considered involved in a motorcycle accident. The evidence must establish the vehicle’s legally sufficient involvement and the circumstances of the crash. If it does, that vehicle’s insurer may be first in line for your medical bills and wage loss, and if the driver was at fault you have an injury claim against them. The problem is identification: get the plate, the witnesses, and any camera footage before the car is gone.

**The driver who hit me had no insurance. Who pays?** Michigan’s motorcycle PIP priority starts with applicable insurance covering the involved motor vehicle’s owner and then its driver. Other potentially applicable automobile policies, including those covering the motorcycle operator or owner, follow in the statutory order. If no applicable PIP insurer is available, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan may provide benefits, subject to eligibility and limits. Your pain-and-suffering claim depends on whether you carry uninsured motorist coverage on the motorcycle policy; without applicable uninsured motorist coverage, practical recovery may depend on the uninsured driver’s assets and any other legally responsible parties.

**I hit a pothole on a Detroit street and crashed. Can I sue the City?** Possibly. The highway exception to governmental immunity allows a claim for a road the City failed to keep in reasonable repair if it knew or should have known of the defect. Written notice is due within 120 days and must state the exact location and nature of the defect. If the street is a state trunkline, the claim is against MDOT in the Court of Claims instead. Document the exact location and condition promptly because roadway conditions may change.

**I wasn’t wearing a helmet. Can I still recover?** Yes. If you were 21 or older with the required $20,000 medical coverage and either two years of endorsement or a safety course, riding helmetless was legal. The insurer can argue a helmet would have reduced a head injury, which goes to damages, not to whether you have a claim. Whether helmet nonuse affects damages depends on the applicable legal rules and evidence about the injury; it does not automatically bar recovery.

**Does my car policy’s uninsured motorist coverage protect me on my motorcycle?** It depends on the policy. Some automobile UM/UIM policies exclude injuries sustained while riding an owned motorcycle, while other endorsements may provide different coverage. Motorcycle UM/UIM coverage may also be available. Review both policies, including their definitions of covered persons and vehicles, exclusions, and endorsements. Read both policies; we will read them with you. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

**Does no-fault cover a moped or an electric scooter crash in Detroit?** It depends on what the vehicle is under the statute and whether a car was involved. Coverage depends on how the vehicle is classified under Michigan law and whether a motor vehicle was involved. A moped, motorcycle, and electric scooter may fall under different statutory definitions. The applicable no-fault priority and available benefits must be determined from the particular vehicle’s characteristics and the circumstances of the crash. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

**How long do I have?** One year to notify the responsible no-fault insurer, three years to sue the at-fault driver, 120 days for a road defect notice to the City, and six months for a claim against the State.
