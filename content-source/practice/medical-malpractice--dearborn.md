## The rules, by the numbers

| Rule | Figure | Statute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deadline to file suit | 2 years from the act or omission | MCL 600.5805(8) |
| Discovery rule | 6 months from discovery; never more than 6 years after the act (exceptions for retained objects, fraud, reproductive injury) | MCL 600.5838a(2) |
| Notice of Intent before suit | 182 days; provider has 154 days to respond | MCL 600.2912b |
| Affidavit of Merit | Filed with the complaint | MCL 600.2912d |
| Expert qualification | Same specialty; same board certification if the defendant is certified; majority of professional time in that specialty in the prior year | MCL 600.2169 |
| 2026 cap on noneconomic damages | $596,400 (standard) / $1,065,000 (catastrophic) | MCL 600.1483 **[VERIFY Treasury figures]** |
| Child injured before age 8 | Deadline runs to the 10th birthday | MCL 600.5851(7) |
| Federally funded provider | Administrative claim (SF-95) within 2 years; suit after denial or 6 months | 28 USC 2401(b), 2675 |

Economic damages, medical bills, lost earnings, and the cost of future care, are not capped.

## What malpractice is, and is not

A bad outcome is not malpractice. Malpractice is a bad outcome caused by a provider who failed to do what a reasonably competent provider in the same specialty would have done in the same circumstances (MCL 600.2912a). You must prove the standard of care, a breach, that the breach more likely than not caused the injury, and damages; Michigan requires proof that the patient would have had a better outcome, not merely a chance of one (MCL 600.2912a(2)). A qualified physician’s written review of the records decides, before anything is filed, whether a case exists. Many do not, and we say so.

A claim against one follows the Federal Tort Claims Act, not Michigan procedure: an administrative claim on Standard Form 95 to the Department of Health and Human Services within two years (28 USC 2401(b)), no Notice of Intent, suit in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan tried to a judge without a jury, with Michigan substantive law and caps applied. ## How a Dearborn malpractice case proceeds

1. **The records**, including the audit trail, from every provider, requested under Michigan’s Medical Records Access Act in the first week.
2. **Expert review** by a physician in the defendant’s specialty, in writing, before anything is filed.
3. **Identify the defendants and the forum**: private hospital or group (Third Circuit); deemed clinic or VA (SF-95 and the Eastern District); City EMS (EMS Act). Serving a Notice of Intent on a federal provider while the FTCA clock runs loses the case.
4. **The Notice of Intent** (MCL 600.2912b), served at least 182 days before suit, which tolls the two-year period during the notice period if served before it runs (MCL 600.5856(c)).
5. **The complaint and Affidavit of Merit** (MCL 600.2912d) in the Third Judicial Circuit Court in Detroit.
6. **Discovery, case evaluation, facilitation**, and, for the cases that do not settle, trial to a Wayne County jury; two to four years is typical.
7. **Resolution**, with minors’ and estates’ settlements approved by the court and Medicare, Medicaid, and health plan liens resolved before distribution.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long do I have to sue a Dearborn doctor or hospital?** Two years from the malpractice, with a six-month discovery rule capped at six years. Because the Notice of Intent must be served 182 days before suit and the expert review comes first, call well inside the first year.

**My care was at a community health center in Dearborn. Is that different?** It may be. If the center is federally funded and its providers are deemed federal employees, the claim is against the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act: an administrative claim within two years, no Notice of Intent, and suit in federal court without a jury. We check the center’s status at the first call.

**Is there a cap on what I can recover?** On pain-and-suffering damages only: $596,400 for most cases and $1,065,000 for the most severe permanent injuries in 2026, adjusted each January. Medical bills, lost earnings, and future care are not capped.

**I signed a consent form I could not read. Does that matter?** It can. Consent must be informed, and a form signed without an explanation the patient could understand, in a language the patient speaks, may not be. The record of what was explained, and how, is part of the case.

**My child was injured at birth at a Dearborn hospital. How long do we have?** A child injured before age eight has until the tenth birthday to file. The parents’ own claims are subject to the ordinary two-year period, so the family should not wait.

**Do I need a Notice of Intent?** Yes, for any Michigan provider: a written notice served on each defendant at least 182 days before filing, stating the standard of care, the breach, and causation. Serving it on time pauses the two-year clock during the waiting period.
