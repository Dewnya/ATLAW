## Macomb Township, MI Truck Accident Lawyer

Macomb Township’s heavy trucks travel three kinds of roads.

**Hall Road (M-59).** The township’s southern edge is a state route carrying freight between M-53 and I-94, along with delivery trucks for the retail on both sides. Left turns are made by Michigan left at median crossovers, and trucks make those U-turns too.

**The arterials and the mile roads.** Romeo Plank, Garfield, Hayes, Card, and North Avenue run north from Hall Road, and 21 Mile through 26 Mile cross them. Some segments are five lanes. Others are two lanes with ditches and no shoulders. These roads carry the township’s construction traffic: dump trucks, concrete mixers, gravel and sand trains, and equipment haulers.

**The subdivisions.** Package vans, moving trucks, garbage and recycling trucks, and school buses run through the neighborhoods every day.

In 2024, according to the Michigan State Police, 1,257 heavy trucks and buses were involved in crashes in Macomb County, compared with 3,916 in Wayne County and 2,081 in Oakland. Statewide, 110 people were killed and 3,613 injured in heavy truck and bus crashes. Macomb Township recorded 1,422 crashes of all kinds, 531 of them on Hall Road and none on an interstate.

A truck crash is not a car crash with a larger vehicle:

- **The rules are federal.** The driver and the carrier are governed by federal safety regulations, and a violation is evidence of negligence.
- **The insurance is larger.** An interstate carrier must carry $750,000 or more in insurance.
- **The records expire.** The driver’s logs and the truck’s engine data, the records that prove the case, can be lawfully discarded within months.
- **The other side moves fast.** The carrier’s insurer will have an investigator on the case within days.

## Where Macomb Township truck crashes happen

**Hall Road.** Tractor-trailers, box trucks, and delivery trucks share three or four lanes each way with retail traffic. The crashes are:

- rear-end and underride crashes at the signals and where traffic stacks up for a crossover, where a loaded truck needs far more distance to stop
- a truck making a U-turn at a Michigan-left crossover that swings wide into the far lanes or blocks them
- lane changes into a car in the blind spot on the right side of the cab
- wide right turns into store and strip-center driveways that catch a car on the inside of the turn

A car that strikes the side or rear of a trailer can go under it. That is an **underride** crash, which is often fatal or catastrophic. Trailers must carry rear impact guards that meet federal standards (49 CFR 393.86). A missing, damaged, or substandard guard is evidence against the carrier and the trailer’s owner.

In the State Police’s 2025 intersection data, Hall Road at Hayes had 67 crashes, seventh in Macomb County. Hall Road belongs to MDOT.

**Dump trucks, concrete mixers, and gravel trains on the mile roads.** The township’s construction traffic runs on county roads, some of them two lanes with ditches and no shoulders. The crashes are:

- a loaded truck crossing the center line on a narrow road or a curve, or forcing an oncoming car onto the shoulder or into a ditch
- a truck turning slowly into a construction entrance across a lane of traffic, or pulling out of one into traffic that cannot stop
- rear-end crashes into a truck slowing for a site entrance without warning
- a concrete mixer rolling over on a turn, since its load is heavy and high and shifts as the drum turns
- trucks backing into or out of a site across the road

**Spilled, falling, and tracked loads.** Gravel, sand, dirt, or debris that falls or blows from a truck is a violation of the federal cargo securement rules (49 CFR 393.100 and following) and of Michigan law, which requires a load to be secured or covered so it cannot escape (MCL 257.720). Mud and dirt tracked onto the road by trucks leaving a site can leave the road slick or rutted. A construction site’s soil erosion permit generally requires controls to keep that material off the road. The carrier, the company that loaded the truck, and the builder or site contractor can all be responsible. See our [Macomb Township personal injury page](/personal-injury/personal-injury/macomb-township) for construction-site claims.

**Seasonal weight restrictions.** In the spring thaw, the county can lower the maximum axle loads allowed on its roads to protect the pavement (MCL 257.722). An overweight truck on a restricted road is a violation. It can also mean the truck could not stop or steer as it should, and the overweight load can damage the pavement and cause the potholes and breakup that cause later crashes. The scale tickets and the bill of lading show what the truck was carrying.

**Deliveries, garbage trucks, and school buses in the subdivisions.** Package vans, furniture and appliance trucks, moving trucks, and garbage and recycling trucks run through the neighborhoods. The crashes are backing crashes, crashes with trucks stopped in the street, and children and pedestrians struck by a truck pulling away. Delivery vans are often operated by contracted delivery companies rather than by the retailer named on the package. Drivers must stop for a school bus with flashing red lights (MCL 257.682). See our [pedestrian accident page](/personal-injury/pedestrian-accidents).

**Whose road it is.** Townships do not own public roads:

- **MDOT** owns Hall Road. A road-defect claim against the State goes to the Court of Claims, with written notice filed there within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **Macomb County** (the Department of Roads) owns every other public road in the township. Written notice is due within 120 days (MCL 691.1402, 691.1404).

A notice sent to Macomb Township is sent to the wrong defendant.

## When the truck was working a construction job

A dump truck or mixer on a construction job may belong to the hauler, the concrete supplier, the excavator, or the general contractor, and the driver may work for any of them. Each company’s role changes who is responsible:

- **The trucking company or hauler** is responsible for its driver and its truck.
- **The concrete or aggregate supplier** may own the mixer or the gravel truck, or may have loaded it.
- **The general contractor or builder** controls the site entrance, the haul route on the site, flaggers, and track-out controls. It can be responsible for a crash at or near the entrance.
- **The developer or landowner** may share responsibility for site conditions.

Some construction trucks travel only within Michigan and only short distances. Michigan applies most federal safety rules to intrastate carriers (MCL 480.11a), but some short-haul drivers are exempt from log requirements, and the federal insurance minimums apply to interstate carriers. The truck’s registration and the carrier’s operating authority decide which rules and which insurance apply.

## Checking a trucking company’s safety record

Every interstate carrier, and many Michigan intrastate carriers, has a USDOT number painted on the cab door. That number pulls up the carrier’s federal record:

- **The FMCSA Company Snapshot** (on the SAFER website) shows the carrier’s registered name and address, its number of trucks and drivers, and its inspections and crashes over the past two years," and add after the bullet: including out-of-service rates for its vehicles and drivers.
- **The FMCSA Safety Measurement System** groups the carrier’s inspection and crash results by category, including unsafe driving, hours-of-service compliance, and vehicle maintenance. Some results are not shown to the public.
- **The carrier’s safety rating,** if FMCSA has rated it, is “satisfactory,” “conditional,” or “unsatisfactory.” Many carriers have never been rated.

A poor record does not by itself prove fault in a crash. It can support a claim that the carrier failed to supervise or maintain its fleet, and that a shipper, broker, or contractor was negligent in hiring a carrier with that record.

## Who is responsible, and who pays

**The carrier and the driver.** The motor carrier is responsible for its drivers’ compliance with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations, and it is liable for a driver’s negligence in the course of employment. The driver can be sued as well; a claim against the carrier does not require leaving the driver out. Michigan applies the federal rules to intrastate carriers (MCL 480.11a).

These are the rules that decide truck cases most often:

| Rule | 49 CFR | What it means for the case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hours of service | Part 395 | 11 hours driving in a 14-hour window after 10 hours off; 60/70-hour weekly limits; recorded on an electronic logging device. Some short-haul drivers are exempt from logs but not from the limits. |
| Driver qualification | Part 391 | CDL, medical certificate, driving record, background check. Negligent hiring and retention are claims against the carrier. |
| Post-crash testing | Part 382 | Alcohol within 8 hours and drugs within 32 hours after a fatal crash, or after an injury or tow-away crash with a citation. |
| Inspection and maintenance | Part 396 | Pre-trip inspections, defect reports, maintenance records. Brake, tire, lighting, and reflective-tape cases are maintenance cases. |
| Cargo securement | 393.100–393.136 | Loads must be contained or secured so they cannot shift or fall. A spilled load is evidence of a violation. |
| Equipment: rear impact guards, lighting, reflectors | Part 393 | Underride guards (393.86) and conspicuity marking. A trailer that cannot be seen at night or lacks a working guard is a defective trailer. |
| Hazardous conditions; stopped vehicles; phones | 392.14, 392.22, 392.82 | Reduce speed or stop in snow, ice, and fog; warning devices for a stopped truck; no handheld phone use. |
| Minimum insurance | Part 387 | $750,000 general freight; $1 million oil; $5 million hazardous materials (interstate carriers). |
| Leased equipment | Part 376 | The lease between an owner-operator and a carrier, and the carrier’s responsibility for the leased truck. |

**The owner of the truck or trailer.** In Michigan, a vehicle’s owner is liable for its negligent operation by someone driving with the owner’s consent (MCL 257.401). When the tractor, the trailer, and the driver belong to different companies, each may be a defendant, although a company in the business of renting or leasing vehicles is generally shielded from owner liability by federal law (49 USC 30106). When an owner-operator is leased to a carrier, the lease generally makes the carrier responsible for the truck’s operation (49 CFR 376.12).

**Michigan no-fault still applies to you, for any injury.** Your own auto insurer pays these benefits regardless of fault, and regardless of how serious the injury is (MCL 500.3107):

- your medical bills, up to the PIP medical level you chose (MCL 500.3107c)
- 85 percent of lost wages, up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year
- replacement services
- attendant care

Written notice is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). If you have no household policy, a vehicle occupant claims from the insurer of the vehicle they were in, and then the Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3114(4), 500.3172). A pedestrian or cyclist with no household policy claims through the Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3115).

Your claim against the driver and the carrier is for pain and suffering and for losses beyond no-fault, including medical bills above your PIP level. It requires a threshold injury (MCL 500.3135) and must be filed within three years (MCL 600.5805(2)). Michigan’s comparative fault rule is modified, not pure:

- **More than 50 percent at fault:** you cannot recover pain-and-suffering damages.
- **50 percent or less:** your share reduces them (MCL 500.3135(2)(b); MCL 600.2959). At exactly 50 percent, you recover half.

The noneconomic damages caps apply to medical malpractice and to product liability claims, such as a claim against the maker of a failed part (MCL 600.1483, 600.2946a), not to the claim against the driver and carrier.

The difference from a car case is the money available: the carrier’s commercial policy, often with excess coverage above it, and, on a construction job, the policies of the contractor and the supplier. If that coverage still falls short, your own underinsured motorist coverage may add to it. Your insurer’s written consent is needed before you accept a carrier’s limit. See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

**Other defendants.**

- the shipper, supplier, or broker that loaded the truck or hired an unsafe carrier
- the builder or contractor whose site entrance, flagging, or track-out controls contributed
- the store or business whose delivery practice put trucks in the road
- the maintenance contractor
- the manufacturer of a failed component or a defective guard ([product liability](/personal-injury/product-liability))
- for a government truck (MDOT, the Macomb County Department of Roads, a township vehicle, or a school bus), the agency, under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405)

**If you were the truck driver.** Workers’ compensation pays your benefits, and your employer usually cannot be sued (MCL 418.131). If you were injured in your employer’s truck, no-fault benefits generally come from the insurer of that truck (MCL 500.3114(3)). They are coordinated with comp, which is paid first (MCL 500.3109(1)). You can also sue a third party, such as another driver, or a contractor whose site entrance caused the crash. See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

## The evidence, and how fast it disappears

Carriers must keep two kinds of records for a limited time:

- hours-of-service records, for six months (49 CFR 395.8)
- driver inspection reports, for three months (49 CFR 396.11)

After that, the regulations no longer require them. Other evidence goes faster:

- store and gas-station cameras along Hall Road, doorbell cameras near subdivision entrances, and dash cameras overwrite in days to weeks
- the truck’s engine control module and event data recorder can be cleared when the truck is repaired and returned to service
- a spilled load is swept up within hours, and tracked mud is washed off the road
- a damaged rear guard can be replaced before anyone measures it

The first act in a Macomb Township truck case is a **preservation letter**. It goes to the carrier, its insurer, the tractor and trailer owners, and the shipper or supplier. Where the crash happened near a construction site, it also goes to the builder, the general contractor, and the site contractor. It demands:

- the ELD and engine data
- dash camera footage
- the driver’s qualification file and post-crash test results
- dispatch records, bills of lading, scale tickets, delivery tickets, and gate or site logs
- maintenance files
- for a construction job, the site’s traffic control plan, flagger records, and soil erosion inspection reports
- the lease, if an owner-operator was driving
- the truck and trailer themselves, for inspection of the brakes, lights, load securement, and rear guard

Once a party knows a claim is coming, destroying that evidence exposes it to spoliation sanctions.

## What to do after a Macomb Township truck crash

1. **Call 911 and get medical care the same day.** Henry Ford Macomb Hospital in Clinton Township, just south of Hall Road, and McLaren Macomb in Mount Clemens are both Level II trauma centers.
2. **Photograph the truck.** Get these shots: the cab and trailer; the company name and USDOT number on the door; both plates, and the trailer number if it differs; the load, the tarp or cover, and anything that fell or blew from it; the rear guard and reflective tape; and any mud, gravel, or debris on the road, and the site entrance it came from.
3. **Get the police report.** Crashes in the township are worked by the Macomb County Sheriff’s Office. A serious crash may produce a separate commercial vehicle inspection report from the State Police.
4. **Do not speak to the carrier’s insurer or its investigator.**
5. **Report to your own insurer and file the Application for No-Fault Benefits** promptly.
6. **Call a lawyer within days,** so the preservation letter goes out before the records expire, the road is cleaned, and the truck is repaired.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many truck accidents happen in Macomb Township?** The State’s county profile does not break out truck crashes by community. Countywide, 1,257 heavy trucks and buses were involved in Macomb County crashes in 2024. In the township, 531 of 1,422 crashes were on Hall Road.

**A dump truck crossed the center line on 24 Mile and ran me off the road. Do I have a claim?** Usually. A truck that forces you off the road is “involved” in the crash under no-fault even without contact, and the driver and the company are responsible for crossing the line. If the truck was working a construction job, the builder or contractor may share responsibility. Get the company name on the door and any witnesses.

**Gravel or mud from a truck or a construction site caused my crash. Who is responsible?** The trucking company, if the load was not covered or secured, and the company that loaded it. If mud or dirt was tracked from a site, the builder or site contractor, whose soil erosion permit generally requires controls to keep it off the road. Photograph the road, the material, and the site entrance before it is cleaned up.

**A concrete mixer rolled over in front of me. Whose fault is that?** It depends on why. A mixer carries a heavy, high load, and a driver must take turns and curves at a speed the load allows. The truck’s maintenance, its load, and the driver’s training and hours are all evidence. The concrete supplier, the hauler, or both may be responsible.

**My injuries are not severe. Does no-fault still pay?** Yes. Your no-fault benefits pay your medical bills and lost wages for any injury from the crash, regardless of how serious it is, up to your PIP level. Severity matters only for your separate claim for pain and suffering, which requires a threshold injury.

**Do I sue the trucking company or the driver?** Usually both. The company is responsible for its driver’s negligence on the job and for its own failures in hiring, training, and maintenance. Naming both is the ordinary practice.

**Is there a cap on what I can recover from a trucking company?** Not on pain and suffering against the driver or carrier. Michigan’s damages caps apply to medical malpractice and product liability claims. In practice, the limit is the insurance available: the carrier’s policy, any excess policy, the contractor’s or supplier’s policy, and your own underinsured coverage.

**Why does the lawyer need to act within days?** Because the records expire. Hours-of-service logs must be kept for six months and inspection reports for three. Camera footage lasts days or weeks. A spilled load or tracked mud is cleaned up within hours, and the truck’s electronic data and damage can disappear when it is repaired.
