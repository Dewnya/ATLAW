## Michigan No-Fault Insurance Claim Lawyer

After a Michigan car crash, the first insurance company you deal with is usually your own. Michigan’s no-fault law requires your auto insurer to pay your medical bills, 85% of your lost wages, and the cost of help around the house, regardless of who caused the crash, and to pay each bill within 30 days of receiving reasonable proof. Those are personal injury protection (PIP) benefits, and they are the part of a Michigan car accident claim that insurers most often delay, reduce, or cut off.

This page explains how Michigan no-fault insurance works, what PIP pays and for how long, which insurer is responsible, the rules your insurer must follow, why claims get denied, and what a lawyer can recover when they are. ## How Michigan no-fault insurance works

Michigan has had no-fault auto insurance since 1973 (MCL 500.3101 and following). The system is a trade. Every insured driver gives up most of the right to sue other drivers for ordinary crash injuries. In exchange, every injured person collects defined benefits from an insurer quickly, without proving fault.

The trade has three parts:

1. **First-party benefits (PIP).** Your own insurer, or another insurer the law assigns, pays your medical expenses, wage loss, replacement services, and attendant care (MCL 500.3107). Fault does not matter. A driver who caused the crash collects the same PIP benefits as the driver who was hit.
2. **Limited lawsuits against the at-fault driver.** You can sue the driver who caused the crash for pain and suffering only if your injury is death, permanent serious disfigurement, or a serious impairment of body function (MCL 500.3135). Economic losses above what PIP pays, such as wages above the monthly cap or wage loss beyond three years, can also be claimed from the at-fault driver.
3. **Vehicle damage.** Collision coverage or the mini-tort claim, up to $3,000 from the at-fault driver (MCL 500.3135(3)(e)). Property protection insurance (PPI) covers up to $1 million in damage you cause to other people’s property, but not to other cars in motion.

In 2019 the legislature rewrote parts of the law (2019 PA 21 and 22). The changes that matter to your claim: you now choose a dollar limit on PIP medical coverage, medical providers are paid under a fee schedule, family-provided attendant care is capped at 56 hours a week, and the order in which insurers are responsible changed for people without their own policy. Each is explained below.

## What Michigan no-fault benefits pay

| Benefit | What it pays | Limit | Statute |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Medical expenses | All reasonably necessary products, services, and accommodations for care, recovery, or rehabilitation, including hospital, surgery, prescriptions, therapy, home modifications, and mileage to appointments | The PIP medical limit you chose: $50,000 (Medicaid enrollees only), $250,000, $500,000, or unlimited. If no choice was made, the policy is unlimited | MCL 500.3107(1)(a), 500.3107c |
| Wage loss | 85% of the gross income you would have earned, including overtime and self-employment income | Up to $7,455 a month for crashes on or after October 1, 2026 ($7,201 for the year before), for up to 3 years from the crash | MCL 500.3107(1)(b); DIFS Bulletin 2026-20-INS |
| Replacement services | Household tasks you can no longer do: cleaning, cooking, yard work, child care, driving | Up to $20 a day for up to 3 years | MCL 500.3107(1)(c) |
| Attendant care | In-home nursing or supervision, including care by a family member, paid at a reasonable hourly rate | Family and household providers: 56 hours a week. Agency care: no hour cap, but subject to the fee schedule | MCL 500.3157(10) |
| Survivor’s loss | For a family when the injured person dies: the contributions of income and services the person would have provided | Up to $7,455 a month for 3 years | MCL 500.3108 |
| Funeral and burial | Funeral expenses | $1,750 to $5,000, depending on the policy | MCL 500.3107(1)(a) |

Two details insurers do not volunteer:

- **Wage loss is tax-free, which is why it is 85%.** The 15% reduction stands in for the taxes you would have paid. If you were laid off or temporarily unemployed at the time of the crash, you may still qualify as “temporarily unemployed” and be paid based on your earnings before the layoff (MCL 500.3107a).
- **The three-year clock runs from the crash, not from when you stop working.** Wage loss and replacement services end three years after the crash date even if you are still disabled. Losses after that date are claimed from the at-fault driver.

## Whose insurer pays

The order of responsibility, called priority, is set by MCL 500.3114, and it changed in 2019.

1. **Your own no-fault policy**, if you have one, even if you were a passenger in someone else’s car, a pedestrian, or a cyclist.
2. **The policy of a spouse or a relative you live with**, if you have no policy of your own.
3. **The Michigan Assigned Claims Plan (MACP)**, if neither you nor anyone in your household has a policy and you were a passenger, pedestrian, or cyclist (MCL 500.3172). Before 2019 the vehicle owner’s insurer paid in this situation; it no longer does. MACP medical benefits are capped at $250,000, and the claim must be filed with the MACP, which assigns it to an insurer.

Exceptions: an injured motorcyclist claims first from the insurer of the motor vehicle involved in the crash (MCL 500.3114(5)). An employee injured in an employer’s vehicle claims from the employer’s insurer. The owner or registrant of an uninsured vehicle who was driving or riding in it cannot collect PIP benefits at all (MCL 500.3113(b)), which is the single most common reason a claim is denied outright.

## The rules your insurer must follow

Michigan’s no-fault act is not only a list of benefits. It is a set of obligations on the insurer, with penalties. These are the ones that decide most disputes.

**Written notice within one year.** You must give written notice of the injury to the responsible insurer within one year of the crash, stating the name and address of the injured person and the time, place, and nature of the injury (MCL 500.3145(1)). The usual form is the insurer’s application for benefits. Missing this deadline forfeits the claim.

**Payment within 30 days.** A PIP benefit is overdue if it is not paid within 30 days after the insurer receives reasonable proof of the fact and the amount of the loss (MCL 500.3142(2)). “Reasonable proof” is the bill, the medical records that connect it to the crash, and for wage loss, a disability note and proof of earnings.

**12% interest on overdue benefits.** Overdue benefits bear simple interest at 12% a year (MCL 500.3142(4)). The interest runs whether or not the insurer acted in good faith. On a $40,000 surgical bill paid a year late, that is $4,800 the insurer owes on top of the bill.

**Attorney fees when the insurer was unreasonable.** If a court finds that the insurer unreasonably refused to pay or unreasonably delayed, the insurer pays your attorney’s fee (MCL 500.3148(1)). The question is whether the insurer’s position was reasonable when it took it, not whether it eventually lost. This provision is why a no-fault lawyer can take a dispute over a $9,000 bill.

**Independent medical examinations.** The insurer may require you to be examined by a doctor it selects (MCL 500.3151). You are entitled to a copy of the report. An insurer that cuts off benefits because its examiner says your injuries “are no longer related to the crash” has made a decision you can challenge, and the examiner’s report is the evidence to challenge.

**Fee schedule, and no balance billing.** Since July 1, 2023, most providers are paid 190% of the Medicare rate for the service, and services with no Medicare code are paid 52.5% of the provider’s charge (MCL 500.3157(2), (7)). Level I and II trauma centers are paid more. A provider who accepts no-fault payment cannot bill you for the difference (MCL 500.3157(16)). In Andary v USAA (Michigan Supreme Court, 2023), the court held that the fee schedule and the 56-hour attendant care cap do not apply to people injured before June 11, 2019.

**Coordinated coverage.** If your policy is “coordinated” (a discount for making health insurance primary), your health insurer pays first and no-fault pays what it does not (MCL 500.3109a). Medicare is secondary to no-fault by federal law regardless.

**No separate bad-faith lawsuit.** Michigan does not allow a tort claim against your own insurer for handling a claim badly. The remedies are the ones above: the benefit, 12% interest, and attorney fees. Anyone promising punitive damages against a Michigan no-fault insurer is describing another state.

## Why Michigan no-fault claims are denied or cut off

The denial letters follow patterns. The most common, and the response to each:

| The insurer says | What it usually means | The response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Not reasonably necessary” or “not related to the accident” | An IME doctor or a paper reviewer disagreed with your treating physician | Treating physician’s records and a causation letter; the IME report’s gaps; suit under MCL 500.3142 if unpaid |
| “Pre-existing condition” | You had a prior back, neck, or knee problem | Michigan law pays for aggravation of a pre-existing condition; pre- and post-crash records show the change |
| “Insufficient documentation” for wage loss | The disability note is vague or the earnings proof is incomplete | Employer verification, tax returns or pay stubs, and a disability certification with dates and restrictions |
| Attendant care hours reduced or denied | The insurer disputes that the care was needed or performed | Daily care logs, the prescribing doctor’s order for hours and tasks, and the 56-hour rule applied correctly |
| “Failure to cooperate” or missed IME | A missed appointment, an unsigned authorization, or a recorded statement not given | Cure the default promptly; cooperation obligations are real, but they do not permit a permanent cutoff |
| Policy rescinded for misrepresentation | The insurer says the application was false (address, drivers in the household, vehicle use) | Rescission is an equitable remedy the court can refuse to apply against an innocent third party; the facts of the application matter |
| Owner of an uninsured vehicle | You owned the car and it had no policy | MCL 500.3113 bars the claim; the question becomes whether you were the “owner” and whether the vehicle was “uninsured” as the statute defines them |

A cutoff is not final. It is the insurer’s position, and the statute gives you one year from each unpaid benefit to sue on it.

## What to do when your no-fault claim is denied or cut off

1. **Get the denial in writing.** Ask for the specific reason and the documents the insurer relied on, including any IME or peer review report. You are entitled to the IME report.
2. **Keep treating.** A gap in treatment is the insurer’s best argument that you recovered. If the insurer stopped paying, use health insurance, a provider who will treat on a lien, or Medicaid, and keep the bills.
3. **Do not sign a release or accept a lump sum for future benefits without advice.** PIP benefits are lifetime (or to your policy limit) for medical care. A settlement of future medical benefits trades that for a fixed amount.
4. **Calendar the one-year dates.** The one-year notice runs from the crash. The one-year-back rule runs from each unpaid bill, tolled from the date you submit a specific claim until the insurer formally denies it (MCL 500.3145(3)).
5. **Call a no-fault lawyer before the dispute ages.** Interest at 12% and attorney fees under MCL 500.3148 are the leverage. They are strongest when the record of what you submitted, and when, is complete.

## What a Michigan no-fault claim lawyer recovers

In a first-party case, the recovery is specific:

- **Every overdue benefit:** the unpaid medical bills at the fee-schedule rate, the wage loss, replacement services, and attendant care the insurer owed.
- **12% simple interest** on each benefit from the day it became overdue (MCL 500.3142).
- **Your attorney fees**, paid by the insurer, if the refusal or delay was unreasonable (MCL 500.3148).
- **Reinstatement**, so future benefits are paid as they come due.

Because the fee can be paid by the insurer when it acted unreasonably, a no-fault lawsuit can make sense for a bill of a few thousand dollars, which is why insurers treat represented claimants differently from unrepresented ones.

If your injury is a serious impairment of body function, the same crash supports a claim against the at-fault driver for pain and suffering and for economic losses beyond PIP. Michigan law bars that claim entirely if you were more than 50% at fault (MCL 500.3135(2)(b)) and reduces it by your share of fault otherwise. A lawyer who handles both claims coordinates them so that one does not undercut the other.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan no-fault insurance claims

**What does Michigan no-fault insurance cover after a car accident?** Medical expenses up to the PIP limit you chose, 85% of lost wages up to $7,455 a month for three years, replacement services at $20 a day for three years, attendant care, survivor’s loss, and funeral expenses (MCL 500.3107, 500.3108). It pays regardless of who caused the crash.

**I was hit by another driver. Why is my own insurance paying?** That is how no-fault works. Your insurer pays your PIP benefits first and fastest. Your claim against the other driver is for pain and suffering and for losses PIP does not cover, and it requires a serious impairment of body function.

**How long does my insurer have to pay a bill?** Thirty days after it receives reasonable proof of the fact and the amount of the loss (MCL 500.3142(2)). After that the benefit is overdue and bears 12% interest.

**My insurer denied my claim. Can I sue for bad faith?** Not as a separate claim; Michigan does not recognize one against a first-party insurer. You sue for the benefits themselves, plus 12% interest, plus attorney fees if the denial was unreasonable (MCL 500.3142, 500.3148).

**What is the one-year-back rule?** You cannot recover PIP benefits for any loss incurred more than one year before you file suit (MCL 500.3145(2)). Since 2019, that year is paused from the day you submit a specific claim until the insurer formally denies it (MCL 500.3145(3)). The practical rule: do not let an unpaid bill age past a year without a lawsuit.

**I don’t have car insurance. Can I get no-fault benefits?** If you were a passenger, pedestrian, or cyclist and nobody in your household has a policy, you claim through the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan, with medical benefits capped at $250,000. If you owned the uninsured car you were in, MCL 500.3113 bars your claim.

**Do I have to go to the insurance company’s doctor?** Yes, if the request is reasonable (MCL 500.3151). You are entitled to a copy of the report, and the examiner’s opinion does not override your treating physician’s; it is one piece of evidence in a dispute.

**Can my insurer pay my doctor less than the bill?** Yes. Since 2019, providers are paid under a fee schedule, currently 190% of Medicare for most services (MCL 500.3157). The provider cannot bill you for the difference. If you were injured before June 11, 2019, the fee schedule does not apply to your care (Andary v USAA, 2023).

**What if I was unemployed when the crash happened?** If you were temporarily unemployed, wage loss is based on what you earned in the period before the layoff (MCL 500.3107a). If you had a job offer you could not take, that can also support a claim. The insurer will want documentation of both.

**What PIP level should I choose?** That is an insurance decision, not legal advice, but one fact informs it: $250,000 is exhausted by a single serious crash with surgery and rehabilitation. Many people with a $250,000 limit have no other coverage for the rest of their care, except a claim against the at-fault driver, whose policy may be the $50,000 minimum.
