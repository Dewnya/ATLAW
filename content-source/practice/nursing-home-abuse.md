## What the law requires of a Michigan nursing home

Two sets of rules apply to every nursing home in Michigan that accepts Medicare or Medicaid, which is nearly all of them.

**Federal regulations** (42 CFR Part 483, the Nursing Home Reform Act requirements) set the standard of care in detail. A resident has the right to be free from abuse, neglect, exploitation, and physical or chemical restraints used for discipline or convenience (483.12). The facility must ensure that a resident who enters without pressure sores does not develop them unless clinically unavoidable, and that a resident who has them receives treatment to heal them (483.25(b)). It must keep the environment free of accident hazards and provide the supervision and assistive devices needed to prevent falls (483.25(d)). It must maintain each resident’s nutrition and hydration (483.25(g)), manage medications without significant error (483.45), and have sufficient nursing staff to meet residents’ needs (483.35). The facility’s own care plan for each resident, which it is required to write and follow (483.21), is the measure of whether it did.

**Michigan’s Public Health Code** gives every nursing home resident a bill of rights (MCL 333.20201), including the right to adequate and appropriate care, to be free from mental and physical abuse and from restraints except as ordered by a physician, and to have a family member or representative informed of changes in condition. Michigan licenses and inspects nursing homes through the Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs (LARA), which investigates complaints and can cite, fine, and in serious cases close a facility.

A violation of these rules is not automatically a lawsuit, but it is evidence of negligence, and LARA’s inspection reports and citations are public records a lawyer obtains early.

## The signs of nursing home abuse and neglect

**Neglect** is the most common harm, and it looks like this:

- **Pressure sores (bedsores)**, especially on the tailbone, hips, and heels. A sore that reaches stage 3 or 4 in a facility is, under the federal rule, presumptively the facility’s failure unless the chart shows it was unavoidable.
- **Falls**, particularly repeated falls or a fall by a resident whose care plan called for assistance or an alarm.
- **Dehydration and unexplained weight loss**, which the facility must monitor and record.
- **Infections and sepsis**, including urinary tract infections from poor catheter care and infected sores.
- **Medication errors**: wrong drug, wrong dose, missed doses, or sedating medications used to keep a resident quiet.
- **Wandering (elopement)**: a resident with dementia leaving the building.
- **Poor hygiene**: soiled bedding, unwashed residents, untreated dental problems.
- **Choking and aspiration** in residents on modified diets who were fed the wrong food or left unsupervised.

**Abuse** is intentional: hitting, rough handling, verbal threats, humiliation, sexual abuse, and resident-on-resident assault that staff failed to prevent. **Financial exploitation** by staff or others with access to a resident’s accounts is a separate harm with its own criminal and civil remedies.

The family’s observations matter, but the proof is in the facility’s records: the care plan, nursing notes, incident reports, wound-care logs, weight charts, staffing schedules, and the facility’s own report to the state.

## What to do today if you suspect abuse or neglect

1. **If the resident is in immediate danger, call 911** and have them taken to a hospital, where an independent medical record will be created.
2. **Report to Michigan Adult Protective Services** at 855-444-3911 (24 hours). APS investigates abuse and neglect of vulnerable adults in any setting.
3. **File a complaint with LARA**, which licenses nursing homes, at 800-882-6006 or online. LARA must investigate and will issue a report.
4. **Contact the Michigan Long-Term Care Ombudsman** at 866-485-9393. The Ombudsman is an independent advocate for residents, free, and can intervene with the facility.
5. **Photograph injuries, sores, and conditions**, with dates. Keep a written log of what you observe on each visit and what staff tell you.
6. **Request the medical records in writing.** A resident, or a representative with authority, has the right to the chart. The facility must produce it.
7. **Do not sign anything new**, and do not accept the facility’s explanation as the final word. A lawyer can obtain the records the family cannot.

Michigan law requires health professionals, facility staff, and others to report suspected abuse of a vulnerable adult to APS (MCL 400.11a), and requires a nursing home to report abuse and neglect to the state and to investigate internally (MCL 333.21771). A facility that did neither has a problem beyond the injury.

## Negligence or malpractice: the question that sets the deadline

This is the point on which most nursing home websites are wrong or silent, and it matters because the answer changes the deadline by a year and adds pre-suit requirements.

Michigan courts decide whether a claim against a nursing home is **ordinary negligence** or **medical malpractice** by asking whether the facility’s conduct involved professional medical judgment, and whether a jury would need expert testimony to evaluate it (*Bryant v. Oakpointe Villa Nursing Centre*, Michigan Supreme Court, 2004). A nurse’s decision about wound treatment or a physician’s medication order is malpractice. A failure to follow a care plan that said “two-person assist for transfers,” leaving a resident who could not swallow alone with a tray, or failing to supervise a resident known to wander, is ordinary negligence that any juror can judge.

The consequences:

|  | Ordinary negligence | Medical malpractice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deadline to sue | 3 years (MCL 600.5805(2)) | 2 years (MCL 600.5805(8)) |
| Pre-suit notice | None | Notice of Intent 182 days before filing (MCL 600.2912b) |
| Expert affidavit | Not required to file | Affidavit of Merit with the complaint (MCL 600.2912d) |
| Damages | No cap | Non-economic damages capped (MCL 600.1483); economic damages uncapped |

Because many nursing home cases include both kinds of claims, a lawyer files both, meets the malpractice requirements for the parts that need them, and preserves the three-year claim for the parts that do not. A family that waits past two years has lost the malpractice claims; a family that waits past three has lost everything.

**Wrongful death.** If the resident died, the claim is brought by the estate’s personal representative under the Wrongful Death Act (MCL 600.2922), recovers the resident’s conscious pain and suffering before death and the family’s loss of society and companionship, and follows the deadline of the underlying claim.

## The arbitration clause in the admission agreement

Most Michigan nursing home admission packets include an agreement to arbitrate disputes instead of going to court. Families sign them under pressure, often without reading them. Three things to know:

- Under federal rule (42 CFR 483.70(n)), a facility may not require arbitration as a condition of admission, must explain the agreement in a way the resident understands, and must allow the resident to rescind it within 30 days of signing.
- An arbitration agreement signed by a family member who did not have legal authority (a power of attorney or guardianship) to bind the resident is generally not enforceable against the resident or the estate. Who signed, and with what authority, is the first question we ask.
- Even an enforceable agreement does not eliminate the claim; it changes the forum. Arbitration before a neutral arbitrator can still produce a full recovery.

Bring the admission paperwork to the first consultation. Do not assume the clause ends the matter.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan nursing home abuse claims

**How do I report nursing home abuse in Michigan?** Adult Protective Services at 855-444-3911 (24 hours), LARA at 800-882-6006, and the Long-Term Care Ombudsman at 866-485-9393. Call 911 if the resident is in danger. All three can act today; a lawsuit comes later.

**How long do we have to sue a nursing home in Michigan?** Two years for claims involving medical judgment (malpractice), three years for ordinary negligence such as failing to follow a care plan. Most cases include both. Call before two years have passed.

**Is a bedsore proof of neglect?** Under federal rule, a facility must prevent pressure sores unless they are clinically unavoidable, and must document why. A stage 3 or 4 sore that developed in the facility is strong evidence of neglect; the chart decides.

**My mother has dementia. Can she still have a claim?** Yes. A resident’s inability to describe what happened does not reduce the claim; the facility’s records and the medical evidence establish it. Her conscious pain and suffering is compensable.

**We signed an arbitration agreement. Can we still sue?** Possibly. The facility cannot have required it as a condition of admission; the signer must have had legal authority to bind the resident; and it can be rescinded within 30 days. Even if enforceable, it moves the claim to arbitration rather than ending it.

**Can we get the medical records?** Yes. The resident or an authorized representative has the right to the full chart. The facility must produce it. We request it in writing, with the incident reports and staffing records.

**What if the resident died?** The estate’s personal representative brings a wrongful death claim, recovering the resident’s suffering before death and the family’s loss. The deadline follows the underlying claim.

**Is the nursing home responsible, or the individual aide?** The facility is responsible for its employees and for its own failures: staffing, training, supervision, care planning. Claims are brought against the facility and its operating company.

**How do I check a nursing home’s record?** Medicare’s Care Compare (medicare.gov/care-compare) for star ratings and the Special Focus Facility list; LARA’s website for inspection reports and complaint investigations.
