## Do you have a personal injury case? The four things Michigan law requires

Every personal injury claim in Michigan, whether it is a car crash, a fall, a dog bite, or a surgical error, has to prove the same four elements. If you can answer yes to all four, you likely have a case.

1. **Duty.** The other party owed you a legal duty of care. Every driver owes it to every other road user. A store owes it to customers. A doctor owes it to patients. A landlord owes it to tenants. This element is rarely disputed.
2. **Breach.** They failed to meet that duty. A driver ran a red light. A store left a spill on the floor for an hour. A surgeon operated on the wrong site. In Michigan, the standard is what a reasonably careful person (or a reasonably competent professional) would have done in the same situation.
3. **Causation.** The breach caused your injury. This is where insurers fight hardest. They will argue your back pain came from a pre-existing condition, or that you would have been hurt anyway. Medical records dated close to the incident are the answer.
4. **Damages.** You suffered a real loss: medical bills, lost wages, pain, a changed life. An incident with no injury is not a case, however careless the other party was.

Michigan adds one more hurdle in car accident cases. Because the state has no-fault insurance, your own insurer pays your medical bills and lost wages regardless of fault, and you can only sue the at-fault driver for pain and suffering if your injury is a “serious impairment of body function” under MCL 500.3135. The car accident page explains that threshold in detail. It does not apply to falls, dog bites, medical malpractice, or any case that did not involve a motor vehicle.

## What you can recover in a Michigan personal injury case

Michigan law divides compensation into two categories.

**Economic damages** are losses with a dollar figure: medical bills (past and future), lost wages and lost earning capacity, the cost of household help or in-home care, damaged property, and mileage to treatment. They are proven with records and expert testimony about future needs.

**Non-economic damages** compensate for what has no receipt: pain, suffering, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of life, disfigurement, and the effect on your marriage or family. In most Michigan injury cases there is **no cap** on non-economic damages. A jury decides.

The exception is **medical malpractice**, where Michigan caps non-economic damages (MCL 600.1483). The cap has two tiers, adjusted every January: in 2024 it was $569,000 for most cases and $1,016,000 for the most severe injuries (permanent brain or spinal cord damage, loss of reproductive ability). Economic damages in malpractice cases are not capped.

In a **wrongful death** case, the estate recovers the deceased person’s medical and funeral expenses, the income they would have provided, and the family’s loss of their companionship and guidance. Michigan has no cap on wrongful death damages outside the malpractice context.

**Punitive damages**, meant to punish the wrongdoer, are generally not available in Michigan. What Michigan allows instead are “exemplary damages” in a narrow set of cases involving intentional or malicious conduct. If a website promises you punitive damages in a Michigan negligence case, it is wrong.

## What if you were partly at fault?

Michigan uses **modified comparative negligence** (MCL 600.2959). Your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault. If you are found 20% responsible for a fall because you were looking at your phone, you recover 80% of your damages. If you are found **more than 50%** at fault, you cannot recover non-economic damages at all, though you can still recover economic damages reduced by your share. Insurers assign fault to injured people as a negotiating tactic; a lawyer’s first job is often to push that percentage down with evidence.

## How long you have: Michigan personal injury deadlines

The statute of limitations is the date after which a court will not hear your case no matter how strong it is. Michigan’s general rule is three years, but several common situations have shorter deadlines that catch people by surprise.

| Type of claim | Deadline | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Most injury claims (car crash, fall, dog bite, product) | 3 years from the injury | MCL 600.5805(2) |
| Medical malpractice | 2 years from the malpractice, or 6 months from when you discovered it, with a 6-year outer limit; a Notice of Intent must be sent 182 days before filing | MCL 600.5805(8), 600.5838a, 600.2912b |
| Wrongful death | 3 years from the death (with a limited extension for the estate’s representative) | MCL 600.5805, 600.5852 |
| No-fault (PIP) benefits after a crash | Written notice to your insurer within 1 year; unpaid benefits recoverable only 1 year back from filing | MCL 500.3145 |
| Claims against a city, county, or the state (a government vehicle, a defective road or sidewalk, a public building) | Written notice within 120 days, served on the correct agency and officer; the notice must state the injury, the defect, and the witnesses | MCL 691.1404, 691.1406 |
| Injured person under 18 | Generally at least 1 year after the 18th birthday | MCL 600.5851 |

Two practical points. First, the deadline is for *filing a lawsuit*, not for calling a lawyer. A case filed on day 1,094 of 1,095 is a case nobody had time to build. Second, the governmental notice deadlines are the ones that most often end otherwise valid claims. A fall on a city sidewalk, a crash with a DDOT bus or a county plow, an injury in a public school: 120 days, in writing, to the right office, or the claim is gone.

## What to do after an injury in Michigan

1. **Get medical care now and follow the treatment plan.** Your health comes first, and the medical record is the spine of the case. Gaps in treatment are the insurer’s favorite argument.
2. **Report it.** A crash to the police (form UD-10). A fall to the store manager or property owner, in writing. A workplace injury to your employer within 90 days (Michigan workers’ compensation requires it). A dog bite to the local animal control office.
3. **Document the scene before it changes.** Photographs of the hazard, the vehicles, the dog, the sidewalk. Names and numbers of witnesses. The spill gets mopped and the pothole gets filled; the photo is what remains.
4. **Write down what happened** while you remember it, including what the other party said. “I’m so sorry, I didn’t see you” is evidence.
5. **Do not give a recorded statement** to the other side’s insurer, and do not post about the incident or your recovery on social media. Both will be used against you.
6. **Keep everything**: bills, receipts, pay stubs showing missed work, a daily note of pain and what you could not do.
7. **Talk to a lawyer before you sign anything.** An early settlement offer ends the claim. A free consultation tells you whether it is fair.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan personal injury cases

**How long do I have to file a personal injury lawsuit in Michigan?** Three years from the date of injury for most claims (MCL 600.5805). Medical malpractice is two years. Claims involving a government body require written notice within 120 days. No-fault benefits after a crash require notice to your insurer within one year. Minors generally have until a year after turning 18.

**How much is my personal injury case worth?** It depends on the severity and permanence of the injury, your economic losses (medical bills, lost wages, future care), how clearly fault can be proven, and the available insurance. Nobody can give you a number before reviewing the medical records. We will give you an honest range once we have.

**What does a Michigan personal injury lawyer cost?** ATLAW works on contingency: no fee unless we recover money for you, and the fee is a percentage of the recovery set in writing before we begin. Michigan caps contingency fees in injury cases at one-third of the net recovery. The consultation is free.

**Can I still recover if I was partly at fault?** Yes, as long as you were not more than 50% at fault. Your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault. Above 50%, you lose the right to non-economic damages.

**Is Michigan a no-fault state for all injuries?** No. No-fault applies only to motor vehicle accidents. Falls, dog bites, medical malpractice, and other injuries follow ordinary negligence rules: the at-fault party (through their insurer) pays.

**Does Michigan cap pain and suffering damages?** Only in medical malpractice cases, where the cap was $569,000 (or $1,016,000 for the most severe injuries) in 2024 and is adjusted each year. There is no cap in car accident, premises, dog bite, product, or wrongful death cases.

**Will my case go to trial?** Most do not. The large majority of Michigan injury claims settle with the insurer before trial, often after a lawsuit is filed but before a trial date. A firm has to be willing to try the case for the insurer to pay fair value, but trial itself is the exception.

**How long does a personal injury case take?** Simple claims with clear fault and completed treatment can settle in a few months. Most take one to two years, because the value of the case cannot be known until your doctors know how well you will recover. Cases that go to trial take two to three years.

**What if the person who hurt me has no insurance?** In a car crash, your own uninsured motorist coverage (if you bought it) and your no-fault PIP benefits apply. In other cases, we look for every potentially responsible party: a property owner’s insurer, an employer, a manufacturer, a bar that served a drunk driver. An uninsured individual with no assets is, realistically, difficult to collect from, and we will tell you if that is the situation.

**Should I accept the insurance company’s first offer?** Not before a lawyer has reviewed it. First offers come early for a reason: the insurer wants to settle before the full extent of the injury is known. Once you sign the release, the claim is closed permanently, even if you need surgery six months later.
