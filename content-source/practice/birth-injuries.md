## Michigan Birth Injury Lawyer

Michigan recorded 99,420 live births in 2024. Most went as they should. In a small number, a child who was healthy at the start of labor was born with brain damage, a paralyzed arm, a fractured skull, or did not survive, because of something that happened, or did not happen, in the hours before delivery: a fetal heart tracing that showed distress and was not acted on, a cesarean ordered too late, a shoulder dystocia managed with force, a vacuum applied too long, an infection not treated, a jaundice level not checked. These are the birth injury cases, and they are among the most consequential claims in Michigan law because the injured person has a lifetime ahead.

Michigan gives a birth-injured child rules that no other malpractice claimant has: a deadline that runs to the child’s tenth birthday, a separate claim for the mother, a damages cap tier written for brain injury, and a procedure that requires the right specialists before a case can be filed. This page explains the injuries, how they are proved from the labor record, who is liable, the procedure and deadlines, and what a child’s lifetime of care is worth under Michigan law.

## How a birth injury case is proved: the labor record

Labor and delivery is the most continuously documented event in medicine, and the evidence is specific:

- **The electronic fetal monitoring strip.** The standard interpretation is the three-tier system adopted by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists: Category I (normal), Category II (indeterminate, requiring evaluation and surveillance), and Category III (abnormal: absent variability with recurrent late or variable decelerations or bradycardia, or a sinusoidal pattern), which requires prompt action, including expedited delivery if the pattern does not resolve. The nurse’s and physician’s documented interpretation, the time each was notified, and what was done are the center of most HIE cases.
- **The decision-to-incision interval.** When a cesarean is indicated for fetal distress, the time from decision to incision is measured; thirty minutes is the commonly cited benchmark for a hospital’s capability, and the facts of each case decide what the standard required.
- **Oxytocin (Pitocin) records.** Dose, rate, contraction frequency, and whether the drug was reduced or stopped when the uterus was hyperstimulated or the tracing deteriorated.
- **Cord blood gases and Apgar scores**, which show the degree and timing of oxygen deprivation at birth.
- **Neonatal records**: resuscitation, cooling therapy, seizures, imaging (head ultrasound, MRI timing and pattern), and NICU course.
- **Prenatal records**: risk factors (diabetes, hypertension, prior cesarean, macrosomia, GBS status) that should have changed the plan for labor.
- **Nursing chain-of-command policies**, which require a nurse who disagrees with a physician’s inaction to escalate.
- **Staffing and the hospital’s capabilities**: whether an obstetrician and anesthesia were in-house, whether a NICU was available, and whether transfer was indicated.

The defense in nearly every HIE and CP case is that the injury occurred before labor (an in-utero event, infection, or genetic cause). The answer is the pattern of evidence above, read by maternal-fetal medicine, neonatology, and pediatric neurology experts.

## Who is liable

- **The obstetrician or midwife** who managed labor, and the practice group that employs them.
- **Labor and delivery nurses**, and through them the hospital, for monitoring, interpretation, notification, oxytocin management, and failure to escalate.
- **The hospital**, directly, for staffing, policies, in-house coverage, and equipment, and vicariously for its employees. Where the obstetrician is a contractor, Michigan’s ostensible agency rule applies: a patient who looked to the hospital for care and was assigned the physician by the hospital may hold the hospital liable unless told and understood otherwise (Grewe v Mt Clemens General Hospital, 1978; Markel v William Beaumont Hospital, 2022, and the Supreme Court’s July 9, 2025 order).
- **Anesthesiologists and CRNAs**, for epidural and cesarean anesthesia injuries; see our [anesthesia error](about:blank) page.
- **Pediatricians and neonatologists**, for newborn screening, jaundice, hypoglycemia, infection, and resuscitation failures.
- **Government hospitals**: University of Michigan Health claims go to the Court of Claims with a six-month notice (MCL 600.6431); VA births are rare but FTCA rules would apply. See our [hospital negligence](about:blank) page.

## Two claims: the child’s and the mother’s

A birth injury produces two injured people with two sets of claims. **The child’s claim**, brought by a parent as next friend or by a conservator, covers the child’s injury, lifetime care, lost earning capacity, and non-economic damages. **The mother’s claim** covers her own physical injuries, her medical expenses, and her own non-economic damages, which Michigan courts have recognized can include emotional harm associated with her own injury; that is distinct from a general bystander claim based on the child’s injury. The father may have a loss-of-consortium claim if the mother was injured and, in a death, may recover damages available to qualifying beneficiaries under the wrongful death act. The two claims have different deadlines, below, and may be filed together when appropriate.

## Michigan’s medical malpractice procedure, applied to birth injuries

| Rule | What it requires | Statute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The child’s deadline** | A malpractice claim accruing before the child's 8th birthday may be filed until the child's 10th birthday or within the applicable period under MCL 600.5838a, whichever is later; a qualifying reproductive-system injury claim accruing before age 13 may be filed until the 15th birthday or within the applicable period under MCL 600.5838a, whichever is later. | MCL 600.5851(7), (8) |
| **The mother’s deadline** | 2 years from the act or omission, 6-month discovery rule, 6-year repose | MCL 600.5805(8), 600.5838a |
| **Notice of intent** | Served on every defendant at least 182 days before suit; tolls the period | MCL 600.2912b, 600.5856(c) |
| **Affidavit of merit** | From a qualified expert for each defendant’s specialty: an obstetrician for the obstetrician, a labor nurse for the nurses, a neonatologist for the neonatologist | MCL 600.2912d, 600.2169 |
| **Lost opportunity** | Where the claim is a reduced chance of a better outcome, the lost opportunity must exceed 50% | MCL 600.2912a(2) |
| **Damages cap** | Non-economic damages capped at $596,400 in 2026, or $1,065,000 for qualifying permanent limb-function loss from brain or spinal cord injury, permanent cognitive impairment preventing independent responsible decisions and normal daily activities, or reproductive-organ damage causing inability to procreate; economic damages, including lifetime care, are uncapped. | MCL 600.1483 |
| **Settlement approval** | A minor’s settlement must be approved by the court, with funds protected by conservatorship, structured settlement, or special needs trust | MCR 2.420 |
| **Wrongful death** | For a stillbirth or neonatal death, the wrongful death act applies, including to a fetus injured and killed in utero; the estate’s personal representative brings the claim | MCL 600.2922, 600.2922a, 600.5852 **[ATTORNEY CHECK: application to stillbirth]** |

The child’s extended deadline is not a reason to wait. Fetal monitor strips are archived and sometimes lost, nurses move on, and the evidence of what was said at the bedside fades. Michigan hospitals must generally retain medical records for at least seven years (MCL 333.20175(2)(a)), and a written preservation request should go out as soon as a family suspects a problem.

## What a Michigan birth injury case is worth

A child with permanent brain injury from HIE needs care for life, and Michigan law pays for it through economic damages that are not capped:

- **A life care plan**, prepared by a certified life care planner from the treating physicians’ prognoses: therapies, equipment, medications, home modifications, attendant care, and eventually residential or supported living, projected over life expectancy.
- **Lost earning capacity**: the income a child would have earned over a working life, projected by a vocational economist.
- **Non-economic damages** for the child, within the $1,065,000 tier, and separately for the mother within the cap.
- **The mother’s economic losses**, including her own treatment.

Public programs reduce the family’s out-of-pocket costs but do not reduce the claim: Michigan’s Early On program (birth to three), special education under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act from age three, Children’s Special Health Care Services, and Medicaid all pay for care, and the settlement is structured, through a special needs trust, so that the child keeps those benefits. Settlements in catastrophic birth injury cases are typically structured with annuities funding care over the child’s life and are approved by the court.

## What to do if your child was injured at birth

1. **Request the complete records** for mother and child, in writing, under the Medical Records Access Act (MCL 333.26261 and following): prenatal records, the labor and delivery record including the complete fetal monitoring strip, operative and anesthesia records, cord gases, the newborn and NICU record, and imaging.
2. **Write down what happened** as you remember it: who was in the room, what the monitor was doing, what was said about a cesarean, how long things took.
3. **Get the child evaluated and into therapy early.** Early On referral and pediatric neurology, physiatry, and developmental evaluations document the injury and improve outcomes.
4. **Do not sign anything from the hospital’s risk management** or accept a bill adjustment as a resolution.
5. **Call a birth injury lawyer early**, even though the child’s deadline is long. The mother’s two-year deadline is short, the records must be preserved, and the expert review that precedes the notice of intent takes months.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan birth injury claims

**How long do we have to file a birth injury claim in Michigan?** For the child, until the child's 10th birthday if the claim accrued before age 8, or within the applicable period under MCL 600.5838a, whichever is later (MCL 600.5851(7)). For the mother’s own injuries, two years. A 182-day notice of intent must precede any suit.

**Is cerebral palsy always caused by a birth injury?** No. Many cases have prenatal causes, and the defense will say so. Whether a child’s CP resulted from oxygen deprivation during labor is answered by the fetal monitoring record, cord gases, Apgar scores, the neonatal course, and the pattern of injury on MRI, interpreted by specialists.

**What is HIE?** Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy: brain injury from inadequate oxygen and blood flow around birth. It is the injury behind most birth-related cerebral palsy claims, and the standard treatment, therapeutic cooling, must begin within about six hours.

**My baby’s arm doesn’t move. Is that a claim?** A brachial plexus injury (Erb’s palsy) after shoulder dystocia can be, if the delivering provider used excessive traction instead of the recognized maneuvers. Many resolve within months; permanent injuries are evaluated by a pediatric neurologist and hand surgeon.

**Is there a cap on damages?** Non-economic damages are capped at $596,400 in 2026, or $1,065,000 for qualifying permanent injuries that meet the higher-tier requirements under MCL 600.1483.. Economic damages, meaning lifetime care and lost earning capacity, are not capped and are the largest part of a birth injury recovery.

**Can the hospital be sued, or only the doctor?** Both. The hospital answers for its nurses directly and, under Michigan’s ostensible agency rule, often for a contracted obstetrician the hospital assigned to you (Grewe; Markel v Beaumont).

**Does the mother have her own claim?** Yes. Her physical injuries, her medical expenses, and her own non-economic damages are a separate claim with a two-year deadline.

**Will a settlement affect my child’s Medicaid or SSI?** A properly established special needs trust can help preserve Medicaid or SSI eligibility. Settlements may also use structured annuities and require court approval, but whether benefits are preserved depends on the settlement arrangements and applicable program rules.

**We lost our baby. Is there a claim?** A neonatal death caused by malpractice may support a wrongful death claim brought by the estate's personal representative, under the malpractice rules and applicable estate-saving provisions. A stillbirth may also give rise to legal claims, but the applicable cause of action depends on the circumstances and Michigan's fetal-death statutes.

**How much does it cost to pursue a birth injury case?** Nothing up front. Birth injury cases require several experts, and the firm advances those costs, recovering them only from a settlement or verdict.
