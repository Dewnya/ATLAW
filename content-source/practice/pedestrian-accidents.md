## Michigan Pedestrian Accident Lawyer

In 2024, 156 pedestrians were killed on Michigan roads and 1,809 were injured, 441 of them seriously. A person on foot has no airbag, no seat belt, and no steel around them, which is why one in fourteen pedestrians hit by a car in Michigan dies and one in five is seriously hurt. It is also why pedestrian cases are, medically and legally, among the most serious a Michigan injury firm handles.

If you or a family member was hit by a vehicle, three things about Michigan law matter immediately. Your medical bills are paid by no-fault insurance even though you were not in a car, and even if you do not own one. The insurer that owes them is decided by a statute most people have never heard of, and getting it wrong wastes months. And if the driver fled, you still have a claim.

## Who pays a pedestrian’s medical bills in Michigan

Michigan’s no-fault law covers pedestrians. If you are hit by a motor vehicle, your medical expenses, 85% of your lost wages (up to $7,455 a month for crashes on or after October 1, 2026), replacement services, and attendant care are paid as personal injury protection (PIP) benefits (MCL 500.3105, 500.3107), regardless of fault. The question is which insurer pays, and for a pedestrian the order is different from the one for drivers and passengers:

1. **Your own no-fault auto policy**, if you have one, even though you were on foot.
2. **The policy of your spouse or a relative you live with**, if you have none (MCL 500.3114(1)).
3. **The insurer of the owner of the vehicle that hit you**, then the insurer of its driver, if nobody in your household has a policy (MCL 500.3115(1)). This is where pedestrians differ from passengers: the 2019 reform sent uninsured passengers to the Assigned Claims Plan, but a pedestrian with no household coverage still claims from the striking vehicle’s insurer first.
4. **The Michigan Assigned Claims Plan (MACP)**, if the vehicle was uninsured or cannot be identified, as in a hit-and-run (MCL 500.3172). MACP medical benefits are capped at $250,000.

Two consequences. A pedestrian who does not own a car is not uninsured; the law assigns them an insurer. And a claim sent to the wrong insurer is not a claim: written notice must reach the responsible insurer within one year of the crash (MCL 500.3145(1)), and the one-year-back rule limits recovery of benefits more than a year old. The responsible insurer must pay each bill within 30 days of reasonable proof or owe 12% interest and, if its refusal was unreasonable, your attorney fees (MCL 500.3142, 500.3148). ## Your claim against the driver

PIP pays bills. For pain and suffering, and for economic losses PIP does not cover, you sue the at-fault driver, if your injury is a serious impairment of body function, permanent serious disfigurement, or death (MCL 500.3135). Pedestrian injuries, which run to fractures of the legs and pelvis, head injuries, and spinal injuries, meet the threshold in most cases that reach a lawyer.

The driver’s fault is usually built from the traffic rules:

- **Crosswalks.** A driver facing a green signal must yield to a pedestrian lawfully in the crosswalk, and a driver turning on red must yield to pedestrians (MCL 257.612). Where there is no signal, the Michigan Uniform Traffic Code, adopted by most metro Detroit cities, requires a driver to slow or stop to yield to a pedestrian in a crosswalk (R 28.1702). Ann Arbor’s crosswalk ordinance goes further and requires drivers to stop for a pedestrian approaching or within a crosswalk. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: rule and ordinance citations]**
- **Speed.** Michigan’s basic speed law requires a speed that is reasonable for conditions, including pedestrians present (MCL 257.627). The data above shows why: a pedestrian struck at 25 mph usually survives; at 50 mph, nearly one in four does not.
- **Phones.** Holding a phone while driving has been a violation since June 30, 2023 (MCL 257.602b). Phone records are subpoenaed in pedestrian cases because a driver who “never saw” a pedestrian in daylight was often looking at something else.
- **Lighting and visibility.** Three-quarters of fatal pedestrian crashes happen in the dark. Darkness is not a defense; the duty to drive at a speed that allows stopping within the assured clear distance ahead (MCL 257.627(1)) applies at night.
- **Alcohol.** A drunk driver who hits a pedestrian faces a felony and a civil claim, and the bar that served them may be liable under Michigan’s dram shop law (MCL 436.1801), which has a 120-day notice rule. See our [drunk driving accident](/personal-injury/drunk-driving-accidents) page.
- **Commercial and government vehicles.** A pedestrian hit by a delivery truck, a bus, or a city vehicle has claims against the employer and, for a government vehicle, under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405).

The vehicle’s owner is liable along with the driver when the driver had permission to use it (MCL 257.401). Michigan’s minimum liability coverage is $50,000 per person (MCL 500.3009), which a serious pedestrian injury exceeds quickly; the owner’s policy, the employer’s policy, and your own underinsured motorist coverage are how the rest is paid.

## “You weren’t in the crosswalk”: fault and the 50% rule

Insurers’ first move in a pedestrian case is to put fault on the pedestrian. Michigan law gives them a tool and a limit. Under comparative fault (MCL 600.2959), your pain-and-suffering recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault and barred if you were more than 50% at fault. Your no-fault benefits are not reduced at all; PIP pays regardless.

What the insurer will say, and what the law says:

- **“Jaywalking.”** Michigan has no statewide jaywalking crime. The Uniform Traffic Code requires a pedestrian crossing outside a crosswalk to yield to vehicles, and a driver is still required to exercise due care and to keep a lookout. Crossing mid-block is a factor in comparative fault, not a bar, and the driver’s speed, attention, and sightline are weighed against it.
- **“Dark clothing at night.”** A factor the insurer will raise; not a legal duty. The driver’s headlights, speed, and the road’s lighting are the other side of the ledger.
- **“Walking in the road.”** Where there is no sidewalk, Michigan law requires a pedestrian to walk facing traffic on the left side where practicable (MCL 257.655). Many metro Detroit roads, including stretches of Telegraph, Van Dyke, and Michigan Avenue, have no sidewalk for long distances; the question is whether the pedestrian had a practicable alternative.
- **Children under seven cannot be at fault.** Michigan law conclusively presumes a child under 7 incapable of negligence (Baker v Alt, 1965); older children are judged against a child of similar age and experience, not against an adult. A driver near a school, a park, or a residential street is expected to anticipate children.

Fault in a pedestrian case is reconstructed from the police report, the vehicle’s event data recorder, surveillance and doorbell video, the driver’s phone records, and witness statements, most of which are gone within weeks. That is why the investigation starts the week we are retained, not when the insurer makes an offer.

## Hit-and-run pedestrian crashes

Leaving the scene of a crash that injures a person is a felony in Michigan (MCL 257.617), and pedestrian hit-and-runs are common: in the state’s five-year pedestrian data, more than a quarter of drivers were never identified at the scene. You still have two claims.

1. **No-fault benefits through the Assigned Claims Plan.** If the vehicle is never identified, the MACP assigns an insurer to pay your medical bills and wage loss, up to the $250,000 medical cap, unless you have your own or a household policy, which pays first (MCL 500.3172).
2. **Uninsured motorist (UM) coverage for pain and suffering.** If you or a household member carries UM coverage, it stands in for the fleeing driver’s liability insurance. Policies impose conditions: a police report within a set time, sometimes evidence of physical contact. Report the crash immediately and read the policy early.

If the driver is later identified, which happens through license plate readers, Detroit’s Project Green Light and business cameras, and body-shop reports, the claims convert to ordinary ones against the driver and owner. **[CONFIRM: the firm requests camera footage and canvasses for witnesses in hit-and-run cases]**

## Pedestrians hurt by sidewalks, roads, and government vehicles

Not every pedestrian injury involves a moving car. If you were hurt by a defective sidewalk or road, or by a city, county, or state vehicle, governmental immunity rules apply:

- **Sidewalks.** A city is liable for a sidewalk defect only if it had notice and the defect is a vertical discontinuity of two inches or more, or another unreasonably dangerous condition (MCL 691.1402a). Written notice to the city within 120 days of the injury is required (MCL 691.1404). See our [sidewalk injury](/personal-injury/sidewalk-injuries) page.
- **Roads and crosswalk signals.** The highway exception (MCL 691.1402) covers the traveled portion of the road; claims about signal timing and crosswalk design are narrowly limited. The 120-day notice applies.
- **Government vehicles.** A pedestrian hit by a DDOT or SMART bus, a police car, or a public works truck has a claim under the motor vehicle exception (MCL 691.1405), subject to the serious impairment threshold and, for state-owned vehicles, Court of Claims notice rules. See our [bus accident](/personal-injury/bus-accidents) page.

Private property is different: a pedestrian hit in a store parking lot has the ordinary claims against the driver, plus a possible claim against the property owner for lot design or lighting. Note that no-fault benefits apply in parking lots as they do on roads.

## When a pedestrian is killed

Pedestrian deaths are 14% of Michigan’s traffic deaths from 2% of its crashes. When a pedestrian dies, the family’s claims are a wrongful death action against the driver and owner (MCL 600.2922), brought by the personal representative of the estate, plus no-fault survivor’s loss benefits for dependents (up to $7,455 a month for three years) and funeral expenses (MCL 500.3108, 500.3107). The wrongful death claim has its own three-year period and notice procedures for family members. Our [wrongful death](/personal-injury/wrongful-death) page explains the process.

## What to do after being hit by a car

1. **Call 911 and get treated**, even if you can stand. Pedestrian injuries, especially head and internal injuries, worsen in the hours after a crash.
2. **Get the driver’s name, plate, and insurance**, or have someone do it. In a hit-and-run, note the vehicle’s color, type, direction, and any damage, and report it to police immediately.
3. **Identify cameras.** Businesses, homes with doorbell cameras, buses, and traffic cameras near the scene record over their footage within days to weeks.
4. **Report the crash to the auto insurer in your household**, even though you were on foot. That starts the PIP claim and the one-year clock.
5. **Do not give the driver’s insurer a recorded statement** about where you were crossing or what you were wearing until you have spoken to a lawyer. Those answers are the comparative fault case.
6. **Call a pedestrian accident lawyer.** The first tasks are identifying the responsible no-fault insurer, giving notice, and preserving the evidence of the driver’s fault.

## What a Michigan pedestrian accident case is worth

Two parts:

- **No-fault benefits**: medical care to your policy limit (or $250,000 through the MACP), wage loss, replacement services, attendant care, and, in a death, survivor’s loss and funeral expenses.
- **The claim against the driver and owner**: pain and suffering, loss of enjoyment of life, disfigurement, and economic losses PIP does not cover, reduced by any fault assigned to you.

Pedestrian cases are valued high relative to other crash cases because the injuries are severe and the defendant’s fault is often clear, and they are limited by policy size more than by liability. The questions that decide the number: the injury and its permanence, the medical documentation, how many policies apply (driver, owner, employer, your UM/UIM), and the comparative fault facts.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan pedestrian accidents

**I was hit by a car but I don’t have car insurance. Who pays my medical bills?** If anyone in your household has a Michigan no-fault policy, that insurer. If not, the insurer of the vehicle that hit you (owner first, then driver) under MCL 500.3115. If the vehicle was uninsured or fled, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan. A pedestrian is never without a no-fault insurer; the task is identifying the right one within a year.

**Does Michigan no-fault cover pedestrians?** Yes. PIP benefits are paid for accidental bodily injury arising out of the use of a motor vehicle, whether or not the injured person was in one (MCL 500.3105).

**I was crossing outside the crosswalk. Can I still recover?** Yes. Your no-fault benefits are not affected by fault at all. Your claim against the driver is reduced by your percentage of fault and barred only if you were more than 50% at fault (MCL 600.2959). Crossing mid-block is a factor, weighed against the driver’s speed, attention, and lookout.

**What if the driver fled?** Report it to police immediately. Your no-fault benefits come through your household policy or the Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3172), and uninsured motorist coverage on a household policy can pay for pain and suffering. We investigate to identify the driver.

**Can I sue the driver?** If your injury is a serious impairment of body function, permanent serious disfigurement, or death (MCL 500.3135). Most pedestrian injuries that require hospital treatment meet the standard.

**The driver was on the phone. Does that matter?** Yes. Holding a phone while driving has been illegal in Michigan since June 30, 2023 (MCL 257.602b), and phone records can be subpoenaed. It is evidence of negligence and affects how a jury values the case.

**My child was hit near school. Is the child at fault for running into the street?** A child under 7 cannot be negligent under Michigan law, and an older child is held to a child’s standard. Drivers near schools and residential streets are expected to anticipate children.

**I was hit by a city bus. Is the city immune?** No. The motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405) allows a claim for negligent operation of a government vehicle, subject to the injury threshold. Notice rules and shorter procedures can apply, so call promptly.

**I tripped on a broken sidewalk, not a car. Is that a pedestrian case?** It is a premises or governmental liability case with its own rules, including the two-inch rule and a 120-day notice to the city (MCL 691.1402a, 691.1404). See our sidewalk injury page.

**How long do I have?** One year to give written notice to the no-fault insurer, three years to sue the driver, 120 days to notify a government for a sidewalk or road defect, and, if the driver had been drinking at a bar, 120 days after hiring a lawyer to notify the bar.
