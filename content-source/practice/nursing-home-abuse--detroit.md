## The rules a Detroit facility must follow

**Federal regulations** (42 CFR Part 483) set the standard of care for every Medicare- and Medicaid-certified facility, which includes all 28 in Detroit. A resident has the right to be free from abuse, neglect, exploitation, and restraints used for discipline or convenience (483.12). The facility must ensure that a resident who enters without pressure sores does not develop them unless clinically unavoidable, and that existing sores are treated (483.25(b)); keep the environment free of accident hazards and provide the supervision and devices needed to prevent falls (483.25(d)); maintain nutrition and hydration (483.25(g)); manage medications without significant error (483.45); and have sufficient staff to meet residents’ needs (483.35). The facility’s own care plan for each resident (483.21) is the measure of whether it did.

**Michigan’s Public Health Code** gives every nursing home resident a bill of rights (MCL 333.20201): adequate and appropriate care, freedom from mental and physical abuse and from restraints except on a physician’s order, and notice to a family member or representative of changes in condition. Michigan licenses and inspects nursing homes through the Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs (LARA), which investigates complaints and can cite, fine, and in serious cases close a facility. A facility must report abuse and neglect to the State and investigate internally (MCL 333.21771), and health professionals and staff must report suspected abuse of a vulnerable adult to Adult Protective Services (MCL 400.11a). Abuse of a vulnerable adult is also a crime (MCL 750.145m to 750.145r), prosecuted in Wayne County and by the Attorney General’s Health Care Fraud Division. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: prosecution references]**

A regulatory violation is not automatically a lawsuit, but it is evidence of negligence, and LARA’s survey reports and citations for a Detroit facility are public records a lawyer obtains early.

**Adult foster care and homes for the aged.** Many Detroit residents are in smaller licensed adult foster care homes or homes for the aged rather than nursing homes. Those facilities are licensed by LARA under different rules (MCL 400.701 et seq., 333.21301 et seq.) and are not subject to 42 CFR Part 483; a claim against one is an ordinary negligence claim measured against the licensing rules and the resident’s care agreement. Unlicensed “group homes” raise the question of who is responsible at all.

## What neglect looks like in a Detroit facility, and where the proof is

- **Pressure sores**, especially on the tailbone, hips, and heels. A stage 3 or 4 sore that developed in the facility is, under the federal rule, presumptively the facility’s failure unless the chart shows it was unavoidable.
- **Falls and fractures**, especially a hip fracture in a resident whose care plan called for a two-person assist, a bed alarm, or a low bed (a visitor’s fall at a facility is a premises case; see our [Detroit slip and fall page](/personal-injury/slip-and-fall/detroit)).
- **Dehydration, malnutrition, and unexplained weight loss.**
- **Medication errors**: missed doses, wrong doses, and sedation used for staff convenience.
- **Elopement**: a resident with dementia leaving the building.
- **Infections and sepsis** from untreated wounds, catheters, or aspiration.
- **Physical, sexual, and financial abuse**, by staff or by other residents, which the facility must prevent, investigate, and report.

The family’s observations matter, but the proof is in the facility’s own records: the care plan, nursing notes and flow sheets, incident reports, wound-care logs, weight charts, medication administration records, staffing schedules and payroll-based staffing data, and the facility’s own report to the State. The resident or an authorized representative has the right to the full chart, and we request it in writing, with the incident reports and staffing records, before the facility knows a claim is coming. **[CONFIRM: firm’s practice]** Transfers to Detroit Receiving, Henry Ford, Sinai-Grace, or Henry Ford St. John produce a second record, the hospital’s admission note, that often describes the resident’s condition on arrival more frankly than the facility’s chart does. ## Where to report today

1. **Call 911** if the resident is in immediate danger.
2. **Michigan Adult Protective Services**, 855-444-3911, 24 hours. APS investigates abuse and neglect of vulnerable adults in any setting.
3. **LARA**, 800-882-6006 or online. LARA licenses nursing homes, must investigate, and issues a report.
4. **The Michigan Long-Term Care Ombudsman**, 866-485-9393, an independent, free advocate for residents who can intervene with the facility.
5. **Detroit Police** for an assault or theft, and the **Attorney General’s** Health Care Fraud hotline, 800-24-ABUSE (800-242-2873), for prosecution referrals.

All of these can act today; a lawsuit comes later and does not depend on them.

## Negligence or malpractice: the question that sets the deadline

Michigan courts decide whether a claim against a nursing home is ordinary negligence or medical malpractice by asking whether the conduct involved professional medical judgment and whether a jury would need expert testimony to evaluate it (*Bryant v Oakpointe Villa Nursing Centre*, Mich 2004). A nurse’s wound-treatment decision or a physician’s medication order is malpractice, governed by the rules on our [Detroit medical malpractice page](/personal-injury/medical-malpractice/detroit). Leaving a resident who could not swallow alone with a tray, ignoring a care plan that said “two-person assist,” or failing to supervise a resident known to wander is ordinary negligence any juror can judge.

|  | Ordinary negligence | Medical malpractice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deadline | 3 years (MCL 600.5805(2)) | 2 years (600.5805(8)), with the 6-month discovery rule (600.5838a) |
| Pre-suit notice | None | Notice of Intent 182 days before filing (600.2912b) |
| Expert affidavit | Not required to file | Affidavit of Merit with the complaint (600.2912d) |
| Noneconomic damages | No cap | Capped: $596,400 / $1,065,000 in 2026 (600.1483) |

## The arbitration clause

Many Detroit admission packets include an agreement to arbitrate. Three things to know. Under the federal rule (42 CFR 483.70(m)), a facility may not require arbitration as a condition of admission, must explain the agreement in a way the resident understands, and must allow rescission within 30 days of signing. An agreement signed by a family member without legal authority, a power of attorney or guardianship, to bind the resident is generally not enforceable against the resident or the estate; who signed, and with what authority, is the first question we ask. And an enforceable agreement changes the forum, not the claim; arbitration before a neutral arbitrator can still produce a full recovery. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

## Choosing and checking a Detroit facility

Medicare’s Care Compare (medicare.gov/care-compare) rates every certified facility on health inspections, staffing, and quality measures and lists Special Focus Facilities with persistent serious problems. LARA’s inspection reports are public. Among the 23 Detroit facilities, the ratings vary widely, and a facility’s ownership often changes without notice to residents’ families. Two are rated 1 star overall and below average on staffing. The Michigan Long-Term Care Ombudsman can advise a family before placement as well as after a problem.

## What a Detroit nursing home case is worth

The injury (a stage 4 sore requiring surgery, a hip fracture, sepsis, death); whether the claim is malpractice or negligence, because the cap applies to one and not the other; the strength of the chart against the facility’s own care plan; and the regulatory history, prior citations for the same deficiency, a Special Focus designation, or staffing below the facility’s own reported levels. ## Frequently asked questions

**Who do I call today about neglect in a Detroit nursing home?** Adult Protective Services at 855-444-3911 (24 hours), LARA at 800-882-6006, and the Long-Term Care Ombudsman at 866-485-9393. Call 911 if the resident is in danger. All three can act today; a lawsuit comes later. **[VERIFY numbers]**

**How long do we have to sue a Detroit nursing home?** Two years for claims involving medical judgment, three years for ordinary negligence such as failing to follow a care plan. Most cases include both. Call before two years have passed.

**Is a bedsore proof of neglect?** Under federal rule, a facility must prevent pressure sores unless clinically unavoidable and must document why. A stage 3 or 4 sore that developed in the facility is strong evidence of neglect; the chart decides.

**We signed an arbitration agreement at admission. Can we still sue?** Possibly. The facility cannot have required it as a condition of admission, the signer must have had legal authority to bind the resident, and it can be rescinded within 30 days. Even if enforceable, it moves the claim to arbitration rather than ending it.

**Can we get the facility’s records?** Yes. The resident or an authorized representative has the right to the full chart. We request it in writing, with the incident reports and staffing records.

**How do I check a Detroit nursing home’s record?** Medicare’s Care Compare for star ratings and the Special Focus Facility list; LARA’s website for inspection reports and complaint investigations. We read both before valuing a claim.

**My parent is in an adult foster care home, not a nursing home. Do the same rules apply?** No. Adult foster care homes and homes for the aged are licensed under different Michigan rules and are not subject to the federal nursing home regulations. A claim against one is an ordinary negligence claim measured against the licensing rules and the care agreement.

**My parent died in a Detroit nursing home. Is that a wrongful death case?** If the death was caused by neglect or abuse, yes, brought by the estate through the Wayne County Probate Court, with the deadline set by whether the underlying claim is malpractice or negligence.
