## Hartford Disability Denial Lawyer

The Hartford is one of the two largest group disability insurers in the United States. It reached that position in November 2017 by acquiring Aetna’s group life and disability business for $1.45 billion, a transaction The Hartford itself described as making it “the second largest group life and disability insurer,” insuring more than 20 million people. Michigan workers whose employers bought disability coverage from Aetna before 2017, or from The Hartford at any time, are insured by **Hartford Life and Accident Insurance Company**, the entity that issues the group policies and decides the claims.

The Hartford also gave its name to the Supreme Court decision that every long-term disability claimant should know. In *Heimeshoff v. Hartford Life & Accident Insurance Co.*, decided in 2013, the Court held that a policy’s own deadline for filing suit, in that case three years from the date proof of loss was due, is enforceable even though it begins running before the claimant has finished the mandatory administrative appeal. A Hartford claimant who spends two years in appeals and then looks up the deadline may find it has already passed. The clause is in the policy, and it has to be read on the day the denial letter arrives.

This page explains who your insurer is, how Hartford group disability claims are denied and terminated, the forms and reports particular to this insurer, the ERISA rules that govern the appeal, how the appeal is built, what happens in court, and what a Michigan disability insurance lawyer does. ## The forms and reports particular to Hartford claims

Hartford’s claim files have a vocabulary of their own, and a claimant who understands the documents understands the denial.

**The Attending Physician’s Statement (APS) and Physical Capacity Evaluation (PCE). Hartford uses physician-completed medical assessments to evaluate a claimant’s functional limitations. Documented Hartford forms include the Attending Physician’s Statement (APS) and Physical Capacity or Physical Capabilities Evaluation (PCE). Hartford’s published Attending Physician’s Statement includes a section called "Level of Functionality." It asks the treating physician to identify how long the patient can sit, stand, and walk during a workday and to evaluate activities such as lifting, carrying, bending, reaching, and fine manipulation. Certain activities are rated as continuously (5.5–8 hours), frequently (2.5–5.5 hours), occasionally (up to 2.5 hours), or never. The form expressly states that unspecified functional restrictions may be treated as absent. This makes detailed and accurate physician responses important. Incomplete restrictions, unsupported estimates, or inconsistencies between the form and treatment records can affect how Hartford evaluates a claim. An appeal should examine the actual APS, PCE, or other assessment used in the case and identify whether the physician’s conclusions accurately reflect the medical evidence. Additional treating-provider explanations may be appropriate to address pain, fatigue, concentration, attendance, unscheduled breaks, or other functional limitations that the form does not fully capture.**. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current Hartford form names]**

**The Employability Analysis Report.** When Hartford moves a claim from the own-occupation to the any-occupation definition, typically at 24 months, it commissions an **Employability Analysis Report** from its vocational rehabilitation unit, which uses occupational software and the capacity profile from the Ability Analysis to identify occupations the claimant can supposedly perform at the policy’s earnings threshold, commonly 60 percent of pre-disability earnings, sometimes indexed. The report names occupations by Dictionary of Occupational Titles code and asserts wage data. The appeal tests each occupation against the claimant’s actual restrictions, the earnings threshold, whether the occupation exists in the claimant’s labor market in the sense the policy requires, and whether the capacity profile the report assumed was the treating physicians’ or the reviewer’s.

**Medical-record reviews. Hartford has used physicians and other medical professionals to conduct independent reviews of existing records, sometimes through outside review vendors, without performing another physical examination. These reviews can affect how the insurer interprets treating-provider opinions, diagnostic findings, and work-related restrictions. When challenging a denial, the reviewer’s specialty, the evidence considered, communications with treating providers, and the reasoning supporting the conclusions may be important. In Calvert v. Firstar Finance, Inc., 409 F.3d 286 (6th Cir. 2005), the Sixth Circuit explained that the absence of a physical examination may raise questions about a claim review’s reliability in some circumstances. In Fenwick v. Hartford Life & Accident Insurance Co. (2021), the court applied that principle but upheld Hartford’s decision based on the particular record. The relevant question is not simply whether Hartford used a file review, but whether the overall medical evaluation was adequate and consistent with the available evidence.**. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: Calvert v. Firstar Finance, 409 F.3d 286 (6th Cir. 2005); Rose v. Hartford Financial Services Group, 268 F. App’x 444 (6th Cir. 2008), and any more recent Sixth Circuit or E.D. Mich. decisions involving Hartford]**

**The Social Security estimate.** Hartford policies offset the LTD benefit by Social Security disability benefits the claimant receives **or is estimated to be eligible for**. Hartford may reduce the benefit by an estimated SSDI amount before any award, unless the claimant signs a reimbursement agreement promising to repay from the eventual award. When the award arrives, Hartford’s repayment demand follows.

## The ERISA rules Hartford must follow

Most Hartford group policies are employer-sponsored and governed by ERISA and the Department of Labor’s claims regulation, 29 CFR 2560.503-1:

- The **denial letter** must state the specific reasons and plan provisions, describe what would perfect the claim, explain the appeal and the right to sue, and, under the 2018 amendments, explain why Hartford disagrees with the treating physicians and with any Social Security award.
- The claimant has **at least 180 days** to appeal, and the appeal is mandatory before suit.
- The claimant is entitled to the **complete claim file** free of charge, including the Medical Record Review, the Ability Analysis as completed by any reviewer, the Employability Analysis Report and its underlying data, the surveillance, and the internal guidelines relied on.
- **New evidence** Hartford develops during the appeal, including a new file review, must be disclosed to the claimant with time to respond before the appeal is decided.
- The appeal must be decided by **someone new**, consulting a health professional not involved in the initial denial, within **45 days** plus one 45-day extension.
- Violations that are more than minor cost Hartford its deference: the claimant is deemed to have exhausted the appeal and the court reviews de novo, under 29 CFR 2560.503-1(l)(2).

## Building the appeal against Hartford

The appeal is the trial. The federal judge reads the file as Hartford closed it and, in most cases, asks only whether Hartford’s decision was arbitrary and capricious. The appeal must prove the disability and prove the process was unreasonable.

**Proving the disability**

1. Obtain and read the complete claim file. Identify the Medical Record Review physician and specialty, the Ability Analysis and who completed it, the Employability Analysis Report and its assumed capacity profile, and every record Hartford did not have.
2. Update the medical record through the appeal date.
3. Obtain treating-specialist opinions that answer Hartford’s reviewer point by point and complete the capacity grid with clinical support, adding the capacities the grid omits.
4. Add objective testing where the condition allows: imaging, functional capacity evaluation, neuropsychological testing, cardiopulmonary testing.
5. Obtain a vocational assessment that addresses the occupation as the policy defines it and each occupation in the Employability Analysis Report.
6. Include the Social Security decision and its evidence.
7. Add statements from family, former supervisors, and coworkers.

**Proving the process was unreasonable**

1. Document the reviewer’s specialty mismatch, the treating opinions ignored, and Hartford’s unexercised right to examine the claimant.
2. Compare the denial letter to the record.
3. Document every procedural failure: late decisions, undisclosed new evidence, requests not made, explanations missing.
4. Set out the structural conflict of interest under *Glenn*.
5. Write the argument tying the evidence to the policy language and the law.

**Calculate the contractual lawsuit deadline. The policy at issue in Heimeshoff required an ERISA benefits lawsuit to be filed within three years after proof of loss was due. The Supreme Court upheld the reasonable contractual limitation despite its beginning before administrative review was completed. In Rice v. Jefferson Pilot Financial Insurance Co., 578 F.3d 450 (6th Cir. 2009), the Sixth Circuit likewise enforced a contractual three-year limitation measured from when proof of claim was required to be submitted. These decisions establish the legal significance of proof-of-loss provisions, but they do not impose an identical deadline on every Hartford policy. Claimants must review their own plan and calculate the applicable filing period rather than assuming it begins with the final denial.**. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: typical Hartford limitation language and the Sixth Circuit’s treatment of it]**

## If the appeal is denied

Suit is filed under 29 USC 1132(a)(1)(B) in federal court, in Michigan the Eastern District (Detroit, Ann Arbor, Flint, Bay City) or Western District (Grand Rapids, Kalamazoo, Lansing, Marquette), on the administrative record. If the plan grants Hartford discretion, the standard is arbitrary and capricious with the conflict of interest weighed under *Glenn*; if Hartford forfeited deference through procedural violations, de novo. Remedies are the benefits owed with interest and, in the court’s discretion, attorney fees to a claimant who achieves some success on the merits. Remand to Hartford for a new decision is a common outcome.

For Hartford policies **not** governed by ERISA, individual policies and policies issued to governmental and church employers, Michigan law applies: a jury, discovery, contract damages, and 12 percent penalty interest under MCL 500.2006 on benefits not timely paid when the claim was not reasonably in dispute.

## The Social Security connection

Hartford requires claimants to apply for Social Security disability, offsets the benefit by the award (and by an estimate of it before the award, absent a reimbursement agreement), and demands repayment of the overlap when back pay arrives. The SSDI award is also evidence: an insurer that required the application, took the offset, and disregarded the award’s finding of disability has created the inconsistency that courts weigh in finding arbitrariness, and the 2018 ERISA rules require Hartford to explain its disagreement with the award in the denial letter. ## What to do when Hartford denies or terminates your claim

1. **Note the date you received the letter** and count 180 days.
2. **Read the policy for the contractual limitation period** and calculate the deadline for suit from the date proof of loss was due, not from the denial.
3. **Request the complete claim file in writing**, citing 29 CFR 2560.503-1(h)(2)(iii), including the Employability Analysis Report and the Medical Record Review, and wait for it before appealing.
4. **Do not let your physician complete another Hartford form without reviewing the first one.** The grid matters.
5. **Respond to every information request** completely, with proof of delivery.
6. **Do not sign a reimbursement agreement, release, or buyout** without advice; ask whether the reimbursement agreement is the better alternative to an estimated offset.
7. **Keep treating**, and assume surveillance.
8. **Call a lawyer before the appeal is filed.**

## Frequently asked questions about Hartford disability denials

**How long do I have to appeal a Hartford denial?** At least 180 days from receipt of the denial under ERISA; the letter must state the deadline. Separately, the policy sets a deadline for filing suit, often three years from when proof of loss was due, which the Supreme Court enforced in *Heimeshoff v. Hartford*. Both must be calculated when the denial arrives.

**My coverage was through Aetna. Why is Hartford deciding my claim?** The Hartford acquired Aetna’s group life and disability business in November 2017. Policies written by Aetna are now administered by Hartford Life and Accident Insurance Company, and the appeal goes to the address on the denial letter.

**What is the Employability Analysis Report?** Hartford’s vocational report, prepared when the claim moves to the any-occupation definition, identifying occupations it says you can perform at the policy’s earnings threshold. It is built on a capacity profile that may have come from Hartford’s reviewer rather than your physicians. The appeal tests both the profile and each occupation.

**My doctor filled out Hartford’s form and now they say I can do sedentary work.** The capacity grid is read literally, and blank or quickly checked boxes are read as unrestricted. Your physician can complete a corrected statement that explains the earlier form, supports each limitation clinically, and addresses the capacities the grid does not ask about.

**Hartford reduced my benefit for Social Security I have not received.** Hartford policies allow an offset for the estimated SSDI benefit unless you sign a reimbursement agreement promising to repay from the eventual award. Ask which alternative applies to your policy, and coordinate the SSDI application so that the award, the offset, and the repayment demand are handled together.

**The denial was based on a doctor who never saw me.** Hartford commonly relies on Medical Record Reviews by non-examining physicians. Courts in Michigan’s federal circuit have treated reliance on a file review to reject treating physicians, when the insurer had the right to examine the claimant and did not, as evidence of an arbitrary decision. The appeal makes that point with the record to support it.

**Can I get damages for what the denial did to my family?** Not under ERISA, which allows recovery of the benefits, interest, and in the court’s discretion attorney fees. Under a non-ERISA Hartford policy governed by Michigan law, contract damages and 12 percent penalty interest are available.

**Will a jury decide my case?** Not if ERISA governs. A federal judge decides on Hartford’s file. A jury is available only for individual, governmental, and church-plan policies under Michigan law.

**Hartford offered a lump-sum settlement. Should I take it?** Not without analysis. Buyouts are priced below the present value of the benefits and end the claim and often the related riders. Evaluate it against the policy’s remaining term, your prognosis, and the strength of the claim.
