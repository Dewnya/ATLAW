## Ann Arbor, MI Slip and Fall Lawyer

For twenty years, a fall on someone else’s property in Michigan was close to unwinnable, because a hazard that was “open and obvious” ended the case before it began. That rule is gone. Since the Michigan Supreme Court’s July 2023 decision in *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil*, a fall case turns on four questions:

- who had the duty to keep the place safe
- what they knew about the hazard, and when
- what they did about it
- how much of the fall was your fault

In Ann Arbor, the first question has several possible answers:

- **The University of Michigan,** whose buildings, stadium, hospital, and walks are State property with their own rules and notice deadlines.
- **Landlords and management companies,** in a city with a large share of renters, many of them students in older houses with porches, exterior stairs, and balconies.
- **Downtown and Briarwood stores and restaurants,** and the public parking structures that serve them.
- **The City of Ann Arbor,** responsible for public sidewalks, with a 120-day notice.
- **Homeowners,** including those who rent lawn parking on football Saturdays.

## Where Ann Arbor falls happen, and who is responsible

**Downtown and Briarwood stores and restaurants.** On Main, State, Liberty, and in Kerrytown, at Briarwood and the State Street and Washtenaw Avenue shopping centers, the falls are:

- spills and freshly mopped floors without signs
- meltwater and slush tracked inside the entrance in winter
- merchandise or boxes in the aisle
- steep or worn stairs in older downtown buildings, including stairs down to basement bars and restaurants
- potholes, broken curbs, and unlit areas in the lots

A shopper or diner is an **invitee**, owed the full duty of care: the owner must inspect, fix what it finds, and warn of what it cannot fix. The case is decided on **notice**:

- **Actual notice:** an employee saw the hazard or caused it.
- **Constructive notice:** it existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it.

In a shopping center, the owner and its management company usually control the common walks and lot, the tenant controls its own floor, and a snow-removal or maintenance contractor may share the duty. The leases and service contracts decide who, so we request them with the footage.

**Parking structures.** Downtown Ann Arbor’s public parking structures are owned by the City, managed by the Downtown Development Authority, and run by an operating contractor, and the University operates its own structures for staff, patients, and visitors. The falls are:

- ice at the entrances, ramps, and top decks
- stairwells with broken treads, missing handrails, or failed lighting
- cracked and heaved concrete, and drains that pool and freeze

Who owns the structure decides the rules. A privately owned structure is an ordinary premises claim. A structure owned by the City or the University raises governmental immunity, where the claim depends on the public building exception, and an operator under contract may be a separate defendant.

**Student rentals, apartments, and condominiums.** Ann Arbor has a large share of renters. Many students live in older houses converted to apartments, with porches, exterior wooden stairs, balconies, and basement units. Under MCL 554.139, landlords must keep the premises and common areas fit for their intended use and in reasonable repair. The usual claims are:

- rotted porch boards, stair treads, and balcony railings
- loose or missing handrails
- dark stairwells, walks, and parking areas
- basement stairs that do not meet the code

A lot with an inch or two of snow is still “fit” for parking under *Allison v AEW Capital* (2008), so tenant snow cases depend on whether the accumulation actually prevented use. The general negligence claim after *Kandil-Elsayed* is a separate route. A lease clause waiving the landlord’s liability for its own negligence is void. The City’s rental housing inspection records and housing code violations can show what the landlord knew.

A guest at a tenant’s party who falls on a broken porch or balcony may have a claim against the landlord as well as the tenant. In a condominium, the association usually controls the common walks, stairs, and lots, and its snow-removal contract and maintenance records are the evidence. See our [tenant injury page](/personal-injury/tenant-injuries).

**Homes and football Saturdays.** Duty depends on why you were there:

- **A social guest** at a friend’s or relative’s house is a **licensee**. The owner must warn of hidden dangers the owner knows about, but need not inspect for unknown ones.
- **A guest there for the owner’s business,** such as a paying customer, is owed more.

On football Saturdays, many homeowners near Michigan Stadium rent lawn and driveway parking. A fan who paid to park is there for the owner’s business. A fall into a hole in the yard, on a broken driveway, or on unlit steps is a claim against the homeowner, usually paid by the homeowner’s insurance. A fall at a friend’s house is paid by the homeowner’s insurance, not by the friend personally.

**City sidewalks.** The City of Ann Arbor, as the municipal corporation in which a sidewalk runs beside a city, county, or state road, must keep that sidewalk in reasonable repair (MCL 691.1401(e), 691.1402a(1)). A fall on a broken, heaved, or sunken public sidewalk is a claim against the City, including a sidewalk along a state trunkline. The rules:

- The City must have known, or should have known, of the defect at least 30 days before the fall (MCL 691.1402a(2)).
- A defect under two inches is presumed to be reasonable repair unless the evidence shows otherwise, and the judge decides whether the presumption is rebutted (MCL 691.1402a(3), (4)).
- Written notice must be served on the City within 120 days, stating the exact location, the defect, the injury, and the witnesses (MCL 691.1404).

Measure and photograph the defect with a ruler before it is patched. Tree roots lifting slabs in the older neighborhoods are a recurring cause. A walkway inside a shopping center or apartment complex belongs to its owner, and the claim is an ordinary premises claim.

**Schools and City buildings.** Ann Arbor Public Schools is a governmental agency. A fall caused by a dangerous or defective condition of one of its buildings proceeds under the public building exception. That exception requires the agency to have known, or to have had reason to know, of the defect and to have failed to fix it within a reasonable time, with written notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1406). The same rule applies to City Hall, the Justice Center, City recreation buildings, and the public library. A fall on a City or county park path, at Gallup or Argo Park, or on the Border-to-Border Trail, outside a building, has no comparable exception and is difficult to bring.

**Hospitals, nursing homes, and senior communities.** A fall at University of Michigan Health is a claim against the University (see below). A fall at Trinity Health Ann Arbor or a private clinic may be an ordinary premises claim, if the cause was a wet floor or a broken step, or medical malpractice, if the cause was a failure to assess or supervise a patient at risk of falling. Which one decides the deadline and the procedure. A resident’s fall in a nursing home or assisted living community raises the same question. See our [nursing home abuse page](/personal-injury/nursing-home-abuse) and [Ann Arbor medical malpractice page](/personal-injury/medical-malpractice/ann-arbor).

**Workplaces.** A delivery driver, contractor, or staffing-agency worker who falls at a store’s dock, a construction site, or a commercial building is not that business’s employee. Their own employer’s workers’ compensation pays their benefits. The property owner or the general contractor that controlled the dock, the site, or the icy lot can be sued as a third party, and the comp insurer is repaid from that recovery (MCL 418.827). A business’s own employee generally cannot sue the employer (MCL 418.131). See our [Ann Arbor workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation/ann-arbor).

## Falls on University of Michigan property

The University of Michigan is a constitutional state institution, so a fall on its property is a claim against the State, heard in the Michigan Court of Claims by a judge, not a jury. Written notice, or the claim itself, must be filed within six months (MCL 600.6431), and a building claim needs its own notice within 120 days (MCL 691.1406).

Where you fell on University property changes whether there is a claim at all:

- Inside a building open for use by members of the public, such as classrooms, the hospital, the libraries, Crisler Center, and Michigan Stadium, the public building exception can apply: a dangerous or defective condition of the building that the University knew about, or should have known about, and failed to fix (MCL 691.1406). A broken stadium step, a loose railing in a residence hall stairwell, or a defective floor surface are the kinds of claims it covers. The exception has its own 120-day notice, which should be served along with the Court of Claims notice.
- **Outside a building,** on the Diag, a campus walk, a plaza, or a University lot, the claim is much harder. The sidewalk duty in MCL 691.1402a applies to municipal corporations, such as the City, not to the State or the University, and the highway exception does not reach a walk that is not part of a public road. A fall on a campus walk outside a building may have no immunity exception that fits.
- **A fall caused by a University employee or contractor,** such as a cleaning crew that left a wet floor unmarked, or a contractor’s equipment on a walk, may be a claim against the contractor in the ordinary courts.

Visitors to Michigan Stadium on game days are often from out of town. The notice deadlines apply to them as well.

## A parking-lot fall can be a no-fault claim

If you fell while getting into or out of your own car, or while loading it, Michigan’s no-fault law may cover your medical bills and lost wages even though no crash occurred. An injury from a parked vehicle is covered when the person was “occupying, entering into, or alighting from” it, or loading or unloading it (MCL 500.3106(1)).

- That claim runs on the no-fault notice rules: written notice to the auto insurer within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)).
- It sits alongside any premises claim against the lot’s or structure’s owner.
- A worker hurt on the job while loading, unloading, or getting into or out of a parked vehicle is generally limited to workers’ compensation for that injury, with narrow exceptions (MCL 500.3106(2)).

For a student, the no-fault insurer may be a parent’s policy.

## The law after *Kandil-Elsayed*

Under the old rule (*Lugo v Ameritech*, 2001), an owner owed no duty to protect visitors from a danger an average person would have noticed, and insurers denied fall claims on that basis. In *Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil* (Michigan Supreme Court, July 28, 2023), decided with *Pinsky v Kroger*, the Court changed that:

- Whether a hazard was open and obvious no longer decides whether a duty existed.
- It is now a factor in whether the owner breached the duty of reasonable care, and in how much of the fault belongs to the person who fell.
- Both are questions for a jury.

That does not make every fall a case. A visible hazard still matters. If the jury assigns you some fault, your damages are reduced by that share. Above half, you recover no pain-and-suffering damages, only reduced economic losses (MCL 600.2959).

Duty also depends on why you were there:

- **Invitee** (a shopper, diner, tenant in a common area, ticket holder, or paying parking customer): owed inspection and repair.
- **Licensee** (a social guest at a home): owed a warning of hidden dangers the owner knows about.
- **Trespasser:** owed protection from willful and wanton misconduct.

Treating every visitor the same overstates or understates the claim.

## Snow and ice

An owner must take reasonable steps, within a reasonable time after snow or ice accumulates, to make walkways, entrances, stairs, and lots safe. What is reasonable depends on the property:

- a coffee shop or grocery store opening at 7 a.m. after an overnight storm is expected to have treated its entrance
- an apartment complex or rental house, its stairs and walks before residents leave for class or work
- a parking structure, its ramps and top deck

A contractor with a salting contract that missed the morning run may share the fault. The law does not require an owner to keep every surface bare at every moment of a storm.

Some ice is a condition the owner created, and those cases are stronger than natural accumulation:

- black ice from refreezing meltwater
- a leaking gutter or downspout
- snow piled where it melts across the walk
- a drain in a parking deck that pools and freezes

Ann Arbor’s city code requires owners to clear snow and ice from the public sidewalks beside their property within a set time after a snowfall ends (City Code chapter 49, section 4:60). At a home, snow over one inch must be cleared within 24 hours after the snowfall ends. At a business, snow that fell before 6 a.m. must be cleared by noon. Michigan courts have generally held that an ordinance like this does not by itself make the owner liable to a pedestrian. A municipality’s duty to repair sidewalks does not extend to natural accumulations of snow and ice. An owner whose property caused the ice, by drainage or plowing, can be liable. Photograph the ice, the path, the alternatives you had, and the weather the same day.

## What to do after a fall in Ann Arbor

1. **Get medical care the same day** and tell the provider where and how you fell. University of Michigan Health and Trinity Health Ann Arbor serve the city.
2. **Photograph the hazard immediately,** from several angles, with something for scale, before it is mopped, salted, repaired, or patched. Photograph your shoes.
3. **Report the fall** to the store or building management, the landlord or association, the parking operator, the University, or the City, and get the incident report number. On University property, a report to the University’s Division of Public Safety and Security may also be made. Do not give a recorded statement to any insurer or risk manager.
4. **Get witness names,** including employees, and note the time. It matters for constructive notice.
5. **Measure a sidewalk or step defect** with a ruler in the photograph.
6. **Keep the shoes** unworn and unwashed.
7. **Call a lawyer within days.** Footage may be overwritten in days, and a porch or step may be repaired within the week. If the City, a school district, or the University may be involved, a 120-day or six-month notice is already running.

## The courts and deadlines

- **Fall claims up to $25,000** are filed in the 15th District Court at the Ann Arbor Justice Center, 301 East Huron Street. A fall in a neighboring township may go to the 14A District Court instead.

## Frequently asked questions

**I slipped on ice outside a downtown store. Who is responsible?** Usually the store or the building owner and its management company, whichever controlled the entrance, and possibly their snow-removal contractor. If you fell on the public sidewalk rather than the store’s own walk, the question is different: the owner’s ordinance duty to clear the sidewalk generally does not make it liable to you, unless its property caused the ice. Since 2023, an obvious hazard no longer ends the case.

**I fell inside a University building. Can I sue the University?** Possibly. The claim goes to the Court of Claims, with written notice filed within 120 days. It must fit the public building exception: a dangerous or defective condition of the building that the University knew about, or should have known about, and did not fix.

**I fell on a walk on campus, outside a building. Is that the same?** No, and it is often harder. The sidewalk rule that makes the City responsible for public sidewalks does not apply to the University’s walks and plazas, and the building exception does not reach outside the building. If a contractor or someone else caused the hazard, that may be a separate claim.

**I fell on the stairs at Michigan Stadium. Who do I file against?** The University, in the Court of Claims, with notice filed within 120 days, if the public building exception applies. Photograph the step, the railing, and the lighting, and report the fall before you leave the stadium.

**I fell on the porch of my student rental. Do I have a case against the landlord?** Possibly. A landlord must keep the premises and common areas fit for their use and in reasonable repair, and rotted boards, broken stairs, and loose railings are the usual claims. The City’s rental inspection records and your maintenance requests are the evidence.

**I fell on a broken City sidewalk. What do I need to do?** Photograph and measure the defect with a ruler, and serve written notice on the City within 120 days, stating the exact location, the defect, the injury, and the witnesses. The City must have known or should have known of the defect at least 30 days before. A defect under two inches is presumed to be reasonable repair.

**I fell in a parking structure. Who is responsible?** It depends on who owns it. A private structure is an ordinary premises claim against the owner and its operator. A structure owned by the City or the University raises governmental immunity and a shorter notice period. A fall getting out of your own car may also be a no-fault claim.

**How long do I have?** Three years to sue a private owner; 120 days for written notice to the City or a school district; 120 days for a fall in a University building; and one year to notify a no-fault insurer. The footage disappears far sooner.
