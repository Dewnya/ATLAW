## The rules a Dearborn facility must follow

**Federal regulations** (42 CFR Part 483) set the standard of care for every Medicare- and Medicaid-certified facility. A resident has the right to be free from abuse, neglect, exploitation, and restraints used for discipline or convenience (483.12). The facility must prevent pressure sores unless clinically unavoidable and treat existing ones (483.25(b)); keep the environment free of accident hazards and provide the supervision and devices needed to prevent falls (483.25(d)); maintain nutrition and hydration (483.25(g)); manage medications without significant error (483.45); and have sufficient staff to meet residents’ needs (483.35). The care plan the facility must write and follow for each resident (483.21) is the measure of whether it did.

**Michigan’s Public Health Code** gives every resident a bill of rights (MCL 333.20201): adequate and appropriate care, freedom from mental and physical abuse and from restraints except on a physician’s order, and notice to a family member or representative of changes in condition. Michigan licenses and inspects facilities through the Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs (LARA). A facility must report abuse and neglect to the State and investigate (MCL 333.21771); health professionals and staff must report suspected abuse of a vulnerable adult to Adult Protective Services (MCL 400.11a); and abuse of a vulnerable adult is a crime (MCL 750.145m to 750.145r).

**Assisted living, homes for the aged, and adult foster care.** Many Dearborn residents live in assisted living, homes for the aged, adult foster care homes, or continuing care retirement communities rather than a certified nursing home. Those settings are licensed by LARA under different rules (MCL 400.701 et seq., 333.21301 et seq.) and are not subject to 42 CFR Part 483 unless they include a certified skilled nursing unit; a claim against one is measured against the licensing rules and the resident’s care agreement.

## Language, faith, and a resident’s care

The federal rules require more than physical safety. A resident has the right to be informed of their care and treatment in a language and manner they understand (42 CFR 483.10), and Medicaid-certified facilities must provide meaningful language access. A resident has the right to food that accommodates their preferences and religious and cultural needs, including halal meals where requested (42 CFR 483.60). **[ATTORNEY CHECK: confirm 483.10 and 483.60 subsections and Title VI framing]** A violation of these rights is not by itself a lawsuit. It becomes part of one when it causes harm: a resident who could not report pain and developed an untreated fracture or sepsis; a resident who refused meals that violated their faith and lost weight while the facility charted “poor appetite”; a medication error that a resident who understood the nurse would have caught. The care plan, the interpreter records, the dietary orders, and the weight charts are where that evidence is.

## What neglect looks like, and where the proof is

- **Pressure sores**, especially on the tailbone, hips, and heels; a stage 3 or 4 sore that developed in the facility is presumptively its failure unless the chart shows it was unavoidable.
- **Falls and fractures**, especially in a resident whose care plan called for a two-person assist, a bed alarm, or a low bed (a visitor’s fall is a premises case; see our [Dearborn slip and fall page](/personal-injury/slip-and-fall/dearborn)).
- **Dehydration, malnutrition, and weight loss.**
- **Medication errors** and sedation for staff convenience.
- **Elopement** by a resident with dementia.
- **Infections and sepsis** from untreated wounds, catheters, or aspiration; and [brain injuries](/personal-injury/brain-injury) from unwitnessed falls.
- **Physical, sexual, and financial abuse** by staff or other residents.

The proof is in the facility’s records: the care plan, nursing notes, incident reports, wound-care logs, weight charts, medication administration records, staffing schedules, and its report to the State. ## Negligence or malpractice: the question that sets the deadline

Michigan courts decide whether a nursing home claim is ordinary negligence or medical malpractice by asking whether the conduct involved professional medical judgment and whether a jury would need expert testimony to evaluate it (*Bryant v Oakpointe Villa Nursing Centre*, Mich 2004). A nurse’s wound-treatment decision or a physician’s medication order is malpractice, governed by the rules on our [Dearborn medical malpractice page](/personal-injury/medical-malpractice/dearborn). Leaving a resident who could not swallow alone with a tray, ignoring a care plan that said “two-person assist,” or failing to supervise a resident known to wander is ordinary negligence any juror can judge.

|  | Ordinary negligence | Medical malpractice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deadline | 3 years (MCL 600.5805(2)) | 2 years (600.5805(8)), with the 6-month discovery rule (600.5838a) |

| Expert affidavit | Not required to file | Affidavit of Merit with the complaint (600.2912d) |

Most nursing home cases include both. We file both, meet the malpractice requirements for the parts that need them, and preserve the three-year claim for the parts that do not. A family that waits past two years has lost the malpractice claims; past three, everything.

## The arbitration clause

Many admission packets include an agreement to arbitrate. A facility may not require it as a condition of admission, must explain it in a way the resident understands, and must allow rescission within 30 days (42 CFR 483.70(n)). An agreement signed by a family member without legal authority, a power of attorney or guardianship, to bind the resident is generally not enforceable against the resident or the estate; and an agreement explained only in a language the signer did not read raises its own question. An enforceable agreement changes the forum, not the claim. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Who do I call today about neglect in a Dearborn nursing home?** Adult Protective Services at 855-444-3911, LARA at 800-882-6006, and the Long-Term Care Ombudsman at 866-485-9393. Call 911 if the resident is in danger.

**My mother speaks only Arabic and the staff do not. Is that neglect?** Not by itself, but a resident has the right to be informed of their care in a language they understand, and a facility that cannot communicate with a resident about pain, medication, or meals is at risk of the harms that are neglect. If she was harmed, the language barrier is part of the evidence.

**The facility will not provide halal meals and my father has stopped eating. What can we do?** Raise it in writing with the administrator and the care-planning team, and call the Ombudsman. Federal rules require reasonable accommodation of religious and cultural food preferences. If weight loss or dehydration follows, that is a neglect claim.

**How long do we have?** Two years for claims involving medical judgment, three years for ordinary negligence. Most cases include both; call before two years have passed.

**We signed an arbitration agreement at admission. Can we still sue?** Possibly. It cannot have been a condition of admission, the signer must have had authority to bind the resident, and it can be rescinded within 30 days. Even if enforceable, it moves the claim rather than ending it.

**My parent is in assisted living, not a nursing home. Do the same rules apply?** No. Assisted living, homes for the aged, and adult foster care homes are licensed under different Michigan rules. A claim against one is measured against those rules and the care agreement.
