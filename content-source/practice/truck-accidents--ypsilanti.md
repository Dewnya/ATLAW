## Ypsilanti Truck Accident Lawyer

Ypsilanti’s heavy trucks travel four kinds of roads:

- **I-94,** through Ypsilanti Township between Detroit and Chicago, with interchanges at Huron Street, Michigan Avenue, and Rawsonville Road. The township recorded 188 crashes on interstate routes in 2024.
- **Michigan Avenue (US-12) and Ecorse Road,** east through the township toward Wayne County, past strip retail, truck stops, and industrial sites.
- **The Willow Run area,** where warehouses, distribution centers, and the airport’s cargo operations generate tractor-trailer and box-truck traffic day and night.
- **Downtown Ypsilanti,** where Michigan Avenue, Huron Street, Hamilton Street, and Washtenaw Avenue carry trucks through the one-way grid, past Depot Town, Eastern Michigan University, and the Ypsilanti Transit Center.

In 2024, according to the Michigan State Police, 755 heavy trucks and buses were involved in crashes in Washtenaw County. Statewide, 110 people were killed and 3,613 injured in heavy truck and bus crashes.

A truck crash is not a car crash with a larger vehicle:

- **The rules are federal.** The driver and the carrier are governed by federal safety regulations, and a violation is evidence of negligence.
- **The insurance is larger.** An interstate carrier must carry $750,000 or more in insurance.
- **The records expire.** The driver’s electronic logs and the truck’s engine data, the records that prove the case, can be lawfully discarded within months.
- **The other side moves fast.** The carrier’s insurer will have an investigator on the case within days.

## Where Ypsilanti truck crashes happen

**I-94 through the township.** The crashes are:

- rear-end and underride crashes in the backups at the Huron Street, Michigan Avenue, and Rawsonville Road ramps, and in work zones
- lane changes into a car in the blind spot on the right side of the cab
- trucks merging from short entrance ramps
- fatigue crashes on overnight runs between Detroit and Chicago

A car that strikes the side or rear of a trailer can go under it. That is an **underride** crash, which is often fatal or catastrophic. Trailers must carry rear impact guards that meet federal standards (49 CFR 393.86). A missing, damaged, or substandard guard is evidence against the carrier and the trailer’s owner.

Federal rules require a truck driver to use extreme caution in hazardous conditions, to reduce speed, and to stop if conditions become too dangerous (49 CFR 392.14). A jackknife or loss-of-control crash on an icy ramp or overpass is often a speed-for-conditions case.

**Michigan Avenue and Ecorse Road.** East of the city, both roads are four and five lanes with frequent driveways into truck stops, gas stations, warehouses, and industrial sites. The crashes are:

- a truck turning left across traffic into a driveway or yard
- a truck pulling out of a driveway into a lane, slowly, and being struck or striking a car (MCL 257.652)
- wide right turns that catch a car or cyclist on the inside of the turn
- trucks parked or stopped on the shoulder or in a lane at night, without warning devices (49 CFR 392.22)

**Willow Run: warehouses, distribution, and air cargo.** The area around Willow Run Airport moves freight by road and air. The crashes are:

- tractor-trailers entering and leaving yards and loading docks
- trucks backing across a road into a dock or yard
- box trucks and delivery vans leaving distribution centers on tight schedules
- yard trucks and forklifts on shared roads and lots

A crash at a loading dock or in a yard can involve the carrier, the warehouse operator, the shipper, and a staffing agency that supplied the driver or the dock worker.

**Downtown and EMU.** On the downtown grid, trucks share the one-way streets with pedestrians, cyclists, buses, and parked cars. Hamilton Street at Washtenaw Avenue had 132 crashes in 2024, more than any other intersection in Washtenaw County. The crashes are:

- right-turning trucks striking pedestrians in a crosswalk or cyclists on the inside of the turn
- trucks changing lanes where one-way streets merge and split
- delivery trucks double-parked or backing into alleys
- construction trucks entering and leaving sites on and near the EMU campus

See our [pedestrian accident page](/personal-injury/pedestrian-accidents) and [bicycle accident page](/personal-injury/bicycle-accidents).

**Township county roads and seasonal weight limits.** Dump trucks, gravel haulers, and construction trucks use the township’s county roads, including Whittaker, Textile, and Grove Roads. The Washtenaw County Road Commission sets seasonal weight restrictions in spring, when the roads are softest. A truck running overweight in violation of those limits, or a load of gravel or debris spilled on a two-lane road, is evidence against the hauler (MCL 257.720, 257.722).

**Lost and spilled loads.** Cargo, gravel, debris, or equipment that falls from a truck is a violation of the federal cargo securement rules (49 CFR 393.100 and following) and of Michigan law (MCL 257.720). The carrier and the company that loaded the truck can both be responsible.

**Buses.** TheRide runs from the Ypsilanti Transit Center downtown along Washtenaw Avenue and through the city and township. A crash with a TheRide bus is a claim under the motor vehicle exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1405). A bus passenger’s no-fault benefits follow the bus-passenger priority rules (MCL 500.3114(2)). See our [bus accident page](/personal-injury/bus-accidents).

## Whose road it is, and whose vehicle

**Road owners.** A road-defect claim depends on who owns the road:

- **MDOT** owns I-94, US-12, and M-17, and any state trunklines through the city. A road-defect claim goes to the Court of Claims, with written notice filed there within 120 days (MCL 691.1404).
- **The City of Ypsilanti** owns its local streets. Written notice is due within 120 days (MCL 691.1402, 691.1404).
- **The Washtenaw County Road Commission** owns the township’s public roads. Townships do not own roads. Written notice is due within 120 days.

**Public vehicles.**

- **A truck owned by Eastern Michigan University** is a claim against the State in the Court of Claims, decided by a judge. A verified notice, or the claim itself, must be filed with that court within six months (MCL 600.6431).
- **A City, township, or Road Commission truck,** such as a plow, a garbage truck, or a road maintenance vehicle, is a claim under the motor vehicle exception against the agency that owns it (MCL 691.1405).
- **A private contractor’s truck** working for a public body, such as a plow or a construction truck under contract, is a claim against the contractor in the ordinary courts.

## Checking a trucking company’s safety record

Every interstate carrier, and many Michigan intrastate carriers, has a USDOT number painted on the cab door. That number pulls up the carrier’s federal record:

- **The FMCSA Company Snapshot** (on the SAFER website) shows the carrier’s registered name and address, its number of trucks and drivers, and its inspections and crashes over the past two years, including out-of-service rates.
- **The FMCSA Safety Measurement System** groups the carrier’s inspection and crash results by category, including unsafe driving, hours-of-service compliance, and vehicle maintenance. Some results are not shown to the public.
- **The carrier’s safety rating,** if FMCSA has rated it, is “satisfactory,” “conditional,” or “unsatisfactory.” Many carriers have never been rated.

A poor record does not by itself prove fault in a crash. It can support a claim that the carrier failed to supervise or maintain its fleet, and that a shipper or broker was negligent in hiring a carrier with that record.

## Who is responsible, and who pays

**The carrier and the driver.** The motor carrier is responsible for its drivers’ compliance with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations. An employer is liable for its driver’s negligence within the scope of the employment, and the driver can be sued as well. Michigan applies the federal rules to intrastate carriers (MCL 480.11a), so a local hauler that never leaves Washtenaw County is held to the same standards.

These are the rules that decide truck cases most often:

| Rule | 49 CFR | What it means for the case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hours of service | Part 395 | 11 hours driving in a 14-hour window after 10 hours off; 60/70-hour weekly limits; recorded on an electronic logging device. Some short-haul drivers are exempt from logs but not from the limits. |
| Driver qualification | Part 391 | CDL, medical certificate, driving record, background check. Negligent hiring and retention are claims against the carrier. |
| Post-crash testing | Part 382 | Alcohol within 8 hours and drugs within 32 hours after a fatal crash, or after an injury or tow-away crash with a citation. |
| Inspection and maintenance | Part 396 | Pre-trip inspections, defect reports, maintenance records. Brake, tire, lighting, and reflective-tape cases are maintenance cases. |
| Cargo securement | 393.100–393.136 | Loads must be contained or secured so they cannot shift or fall. |
| Equipment: rear impact guards, lighting, reflectors | Part 393 | Underride guards (393.86) and conspicuity marking. A trailer that cannot be seen at night or lacks a working guard is a defective trailer. |
| Hazardous conditions; stopped vehicles; phones | 392.14, 392.22, 392.82 | Reduce speed or stop in snow, ice, and fog; warning devices for a stopped truck; no handheld phone use. |
| Minimum insurance | Part 387 | $750,000 general freight; $1 million oil; $5 million hazardous materials. |

**The owner of the truck or trailer.** In Michigan, a vehicle’s owner is liable for its negligent operation by someone driving with the owner’s consent (MCL 257.401). When the tractor, the trailer, and the driver belong to different companies, each may be a defendant. Lessors’ liability is limited by the same statute.

**Michigan no-fault still applies to you.** Your own auto insurer, or a household policy, pays these benefits regardless of fault (MCL 500.3107):

- your medical bills, up to the PIP medical level on the policy
- 85 percent of lost wages, up to the monthly cap DIFS sets each year
- replacement services
- attendant care

Written notice is due within one year (MCL 500.3145(1)). If you have no household policy, as is common for a pedestrian, cyclist, or EMU student without a car, a vehicle occupant claims from the insurer of the vehicle they were in, and then the Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3114(4), 500.3172). A pedestrian or cyclist with no household policy claims through the Assigned Claims Plan (MCL 500.3115).

Your claim against the driver and the carrier is for pain and suffering and for losses beyond no-fault, including medical bills above your PIP level. It requires a threshold injury (MCL 500.3135) and must be filed within three years (MCL 600.5805(2)). Michigan’s comparative fault rule concerns **your** share of fault, not which defendants can be sued:

- **More than 50 percent at fault:** you cannot recover pain-and-suffering damages.
- **50 percent or less:** your share reduces them (MCL 500.3135(2)(b); MCL 600.2959). At exactly 50 percent, you recover half.

The noneconomic damages caps apply to medical malpractice and to product liability claims, such as a claim against the maker of a failed part (MCL 600.1483, 600.2946a), not to the claim against the driver and carrier.

The difference from a car case is the money available: the carrier’s commercial policy, often with excess coverage above it. If that coverage still falls short, your own underinsured motorist coverage may add to it. Your insurer’s written consent is needed before you accept the carrier’s limit. See our [no-fault insurance claim page](/personal-injury/car-accident-insurance).

**Other defendants.**

- the shipper or broker that loaded the truck or hired an unsafe carrier
- the warehouse or distribution center whose yard, dock, or loading practice caused the crash
- the maintenance contractor
- the manufacturer of a failed component or a defective guard ([product liability](/personal-injury/product-liability))
- for a government vehicle, the agency that owns it, and for a road defect, the road owner, each with its own notice

**If you were the truck driver or a dock worker.** Workers’ compensation pays your benefits, and your employer usually cannot be sued (MCL 418.131). If you were injured in your employer’s truck, no-fault benefits generally come from the insurer of that truck (MCL 500.3114(3)), coordinated with comp, which is paid first (MCL 500.3109(1)). You can also sue a third party, such as another driver, a shipper, or a warehouse operator that is not your employer. See our [workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation).

## The evidence, and how fast it disappears

Carriers must keep two kinds of records for a limited time:

- hours-of-service records, for six months (49 CFR 395.8)
- driver inspection reports, for three months (49 CFR 396.11)

After that, the regulations no longer require them. Other evidence goes faster:

- the cameras at truck stops, gas stations, warehouses, and businesses along Michigan Avenue and Ecorse Road overwrite in days to weeks
- the truck’s engine control module and event data recorder can be cleared when the truck is repaired and returned to service
- a spilled load is swept up within hours
- a damaged rear guard can be replaced before anyone measures it

The first act in an Ypsilanti truck case is a **preservation letter.** It goes to the carrier, its insurer, the tractor and trailer owners, and the shipper or broker. Where the crash involved a warehouse or a dock, it also goes to the warehouse operator. It demands:

- the ELD and engine data
- dash camera footage
- the driver’s qualification file and post-crash test results
- dispatch records, bills of lading, and delivery records
- maintenance files
- the truck and trailer themselves, for inspection of the brakes, lights, load securement, and rear guard

Once a party knows a claim is coming, destroying that evidence exposes it to spoliation sanctions.

## What to do after an Ypsilanti truck crash

1. **Call 911 and get medical care the same day.** Trinity Health Ann Arbor, which has an Ypsilanti mailing address but sits in Superior Township, and University of Michigan Health in Ann Arbor are Level I trauma centers.
2. **Photograph the truck.** Get these shots: the cab and trailer; the company name and USDOT number on the door; both plates, and the trailer number if it differs; the load and anything that fell from it; the rear guard and reflective tape; and the ramp, driveway, intersection, or crosswalk.
3. **Get the police report.** Freeway crashes are generally worked by the State Police, city streets by the Ypsilanti Police Department, and township roads by the Washtenaw County Sheriff’s Office. A serious crash may produce a separate commercial vehicle inspection report.
4. **Note whether the crash was in the city or the township.** The responding agency is a clue, and it decides the road owner and the district court.
5. **Do not speak to the carrier’s insurer or its investigator.**
6. **Report to your own insurer, or your parents’ insurer if you are a student,** and file the Application for No-Fault Benefits promptly.
7. **Call a lawyer within days,** so the preservation letter goes out before the records expire and the truck is repaired.

## The courts and deadlines

- **Ypsilanti truck cases against private carriers** are generally filed in the Washtenaw County Trial Court (22nd Circuit) at 101 East Huron Street in Ann Arbor, with a jury drawn from Washtenaw County.
- **Cases against out-of-state carriers,** which are common on I-94, may be filed in, or removed to, the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan.
- **Claims against EMU, MDOT, or the State** go to the Court of Claims.
- **Claims up to $25,000** go to the 14A-1 District Court at 4133 Washtenaw Avenue in Pittsfield Township for a crash in the city, or the 14B District Court at 7200 S. Huron River Drive for a crash in the township.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many truck accidents happen in Ypsilanti?** The State’s county profile does not break out truck crashes by community. Countywide, 755 heavy trucks and buses were involved in Washtenaw County crashes in 2024. The township recorded 188 crashes on I-94 and other interstate routes.

**A semi changed lanes into me on I-94 near the Huron Street exit. Who is at fault?** Usually the truck driver, who must make sure a lane is clear before moving into it. A truck has large blind spots on its right side and directly behind the trailer. The truck’s dash camera and engine data are often the deciding evidence, and they are overwritten quickly.

**A truck pulled out of a warehouse driveway on Ecorse Road in front of me. Who is responsible?** Usually the truck driver and the carrier, and possibly the warehouse operator if its driveway, yard, or loading practice put the truck in the road. A driver entering a road from a driveway must yield to traffic on the road.

**A delivery truck hit me while I was walking downtown. Who pays my medical bills?** A no-fault policy in your household pays first, possibly a parent’s if you are a student. If there is none, the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan pays. Your claim for pain and suffering is against the driver and the company.

**Do I have to prove the trucking company was more than 50 percent at fault?** No. Michigan’s 50 percent rule concerns your own share of fault. If you were more than 50 percent at fault, you cannot recover pain-and-suffering damages. Every company that contributed to the crash can be named, whatever its share.

**Is there a cap on what I can recover from a trucking company?** Not on pain and suffering against the driver or carrier. Michigan’s damages caps apply to medical malpractice and product liability claims. In practice, the limit is the insurance available: the carrier’s policy, any excess policy, and your own underinsured coverage.

**How fast can a truck legally go on I-94?** Michigan limits trucks over 10,000 pounds to 65 mph on freeways posted at 70 or 75. A truck driving faster than conditions allow can be negligent even below the limit (49 CFR 392.14).

**Why does the lawyer need to act within days?** Because the records expire. Hours-of-service logs must be kept for six months and inspection reports for three. Camera footage lasts days or weeks. The truck’s electronic data and the trailer’s damage can disappear when the truck is repaired.
