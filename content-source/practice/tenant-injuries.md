## Tenant Injury Lawyer Michigan

Half of Detroit’s households rent, and renters across metro Detroit are hurt every year by conditions a landlord was obligated to fix: an unlit stairwell, a parking lot that stays iced for days, a broken entry lock, a railing that gives way, a furnace that leaks carbon monoxide, a child’s exposure to lead paint. Michigan law gives tenants two protections most do not know they have. A statute, MCL 554.139, makes every residential landlord promise that the premises and common areas are fit for their intended use and kept in reasonable repair, and a lease cannot waive it. And on July 20, 2026, the Michigan Supreme Court decided Bowerman v Red Oak Management, which rejected the “mere inconvenience” argument landlords had used for years to defeat these claims.

## What your landlord owes you: MCL 554.139

Every residential lease in Michigan includes two promises by the landlord, whether or not the lease mentions them (MCL 554.139(1)):

- **(a)** That the premises and all common areas are **fit for the use intended by the parties**.
- **(b)** To **keep the premises in reasonable repair** during the lease and to comply with the applicable health and safety laws of the state and the local unit of government, except where the disrepair was caused by the tenant’s willful or irresponsible conduct.

The statute says it “shall be liberally construed” in favor of tenants (554.139(3)). These covenants sit on top of the ordinary duties every property owner owes, which are explained on our [premises liability](/personal-injury/premises-liability) and [slip and fall](/personal-injury/slip-and-fall) pages. The covenants can be modified only in a lease with a term of at least one year (554.139(2)), and Michigan’s Truth in Renting Act separately prohibits any lease clause that excuses the landlord from liability for its own negligence or for failing to perform a duty imposed by law (MCL 554.633(1)(e)). A clause in your lease saying the landlord is not responsible for injuries is void.

**Common areas** are the parts of the property the landlord controls and two or more tenants share: hallways, stairwells, entries, laundry rooms, parking lots, walkways, and trash areas (Allison v AEW Capital Management, 2008). The fitness covenant applies to them; the reasonable-repair covenant applies to the leased premises. The Michigan Supreme Court has held that the reasonable-repair covenant does not reach common areas, so a common-area claim is a fitness claim (Allison; Bowerman v Red Oak Management, 2026).

## The July 2026 decision: Bowerman v Red Oak Management

For eighteen years, landlords defended common-area cases with a sentence from Allison: a condition that is a “mere inconvenience of access” does not make a common area unfit. Parking lots with one to two inches of snow were held fit for parking; walkways with hazards tenants could step around were held fit for walking.

In Bowerman, a 75-year-old resident of an apartment building for elderly and disabled tenants fractured her ankle stepping into a ten-foot trench, four inches deep, left uncovered and unmarked next to the trash dumpster for nearly three weeks after a contractor replaced a concrete pad. The trial court and the Court of Appeals dismissed her claim under 554.139 as a mere inconvenience. On July 20, 2026, the Michigan Supreme Court reversed. The holdings that matter for every tenant case:

- **Fitness is measured by the use the parties intended.** Because the building housed elderly and disabled residents, the landlord had to ensure they had reasonable access to a means of disposing of their trash. The tenant population is part of the standard.
- **“Mere inconvenience” is not a test.** The Court said the phrase had become “a trapdoor in the analysis.” Whether a hazard is an inconvenience of access “may be relevant, but it is not dispositive.”
- **Liberal construction means what it says.** The Court applied 554.139(3) in the tenant’s favor.
- **The contractor is liable too.** The company that dug the trench and left it unmarked could be sued for ordinary negligence even after it left the site, and the tenant’s own knowledge of the trench goes to comparative fault, not to whether the contractor breached its duty.

The Court left open whether a 554.139 breach supports personal injury damages as a contract claim or only through negligence. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: Bowerman footnote 8 and the pleading of 554.139 claims]** For a tenant injured in a common area in Michigan today, the practical effect is that a case dismissed under Allison in 2024 is a jury question in 2026.

## The 2023 decision: open and obvious is no longer a bar

Tenant cases were also dismissed for two decades because the hazard was “open and obvious.” In Kandil-Elsayed v F & E Oil (July 28, 2023), the Supreme Court held that the obviousness of a hazard goes to the landlord’s breach and the tenant’s comparative fault, both for a jury, and that a landlord should anticipate harm from an obvious hazard a tenant must confront, such as the only route to the parking lot, the mailbox, or the trash. Bowerman applied that rule to its facts. The combination of the two decisions is the reason this page exists: tenant injury law in Michigan is more favorable to tenants than it has been since the 1990s.

## Inside your unit: notice, repairs, and negligent work

Inside the leased unit, the landlord is not in possession, so its liability depends on knowledge and repair:

- **Defects the landlord knew or should have known about and failed to repair** within a reasonable time after notice are a breach of the reasonable-repair covenant (554.139(1)(b)) and of the Michigan Housing Law’s requirement that owners keep dwellings in good repair (MCL 125.471). Written repair requests, texts, emails, and portal tickets are the evidence; keep every one, and send repair requests in writing even when the manager prefers calls.
- **Hidden defects that existed at move-in** and that the landlord knew of are the landlord’s responsibility to disclose.
- **Repairs the landlord undertook and did poorly** are ordinary negligence whoever performed them, and the contractor is a defendant in its own right (Bowerman).
- **Code violations** found by a city inspector are evidence of negligence. Detroit, Dearborn, Southfield, Warren, and most metro cities require rental registration and periodic inspection; the inspection file, obtained by Freedom of Information Act request, often shows the defect was cited before you were hurt.

## Negligent security in apartment buildings

A landlord is not an insurer against crime, but it has duties in the common areas it controls. Michigan’s Supreme Court has held that a landlord must respond reasonably to an ongoing criminal act in a common area of which it has notice, which ordinarily means calling the police (Bailey v Schaaf, 2013), and that landlords and merchants are not required to anticipate crime in general (MacDonald v PKT, 2001). The cases that succeed involve a specific failure: a broken entry door or gate the landlord was told about and left unrepaired, security cameras that were advertised but not working, a guard contracted for but absent, lighting out for weeks, or a history of the same crime on the property that the landlord ignored. Police calls-for-service records, prior incident reports, and your own complaints are the evidence. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current scope of the landlord security duty]**

## Lead paint, carbon monoxide, and fire

- **Lead.** Most of metro Detroit’s rental housing predates the 1978 lead paint ban. Federal law requires landlords to disclose known lead hazards and provide the EPA pamphlet before a lease (42 USC 4852d), and Michigan’s lead abatement law and local ordinances regulate lead-safe conditions in rentals (MCL 333.5451 and following). A child’s elevated blood lead level traced to deteriorated paint, dust, or soil at a rental is a negligence and 554.139 claim against the landlord, with lifelong damages. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current Michigan lead-safe rental requirements, including Detroit’s ordinance]**
- **Carbon monoxide and smoke detectors.** State and local codes require working smoke alarms in rental units and carbon monoxide detection where there are fuel-burning appliances or attached garages. A furnace, water heater, or space heater that leaks carbon monoxide, or a fire in a unit without working alarms, is a 554.139(1)(b) and code-violation case. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: specific code citations]**
- **Bedbugs, mold, and sewage.** Habitability conditions that cause illness are claims under the fitness and repair covenants and the Housing Law, proved with medical records, inspection reports, and the repair-request history.

## Guests and visitors of tenants

A tenant’s guest injured in a common area has the same claim against the landlord as the tenant; the landlord’s duty in common areas runs to the people it should expect there, and a tenant’s visitors are among them. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: status of tenant’s guests in common areas]** A guest injured inside the unit by a condition the tenant controlled may have a claim against the tenant’s renters insurance; a guest injured by a condition the landlord was obligated to repair has a claim against the landlord.

## Public housing and subsidized housing

If your landlord is a housing commission (the Detroit Housing Commission, for example), it is a governmental agency. Your claim for a defective condition of the building proceeds under the public building exception to governmental immunity (MCL 691.1406), with written notice to the agency within 120 days of the injury. A private landlord who accepts Section 8 vouchers is an ordinary defendant, and HUD’s housing quality standards and the inspection reports they generate are evidence of the standard of care.

## Your landlord cannot retaliate for your complaint

Tenants hesitate to report hazards or pursue claims because they fear eviction. Michigan law prohibits a landlord from evicting a tenant in retaliation for complaining to a government agency about conditions, for exercising rights under the lease or the law, or for membership in a tenant organization, and an eviction within 90 days of such an act is presumed retaliatory (MCL 600.5720). **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current text of 600.5720]** Reporting a dangerous condition to the city and to the landlord in writing is protected, and it is also the notice the injury case will need.

## Your own fault

Michigan’s comparative fault statute reduces your pain-and-suffering recovery by your percentage of fault and bars it above 50% (MCL 600.2959). After Kandil-Elsayed and Bowerman, your knowledge of the hazard is argued here, not used to dismiss the case. The answer in most tenant cases is the same one the Supreme Court gave: a tenant who must use the stairwell, the walkway, or the trash area to live in their home has no alternative, and the landlord was obligated to make it fit for that use.

## What to do after you are hurt at your rental

1. **Report the condition to the landlord in writing** the same day, with photographs, even if you reported it before. Keep a copy.
2. **Photograph and measure the hazard** before it is fixed.
3. **Get medical care** and describe where and how you were hurt.
4. **Request the city’s rental inspection file** or let us do it.
5. **Do not sign anything from the landlord’s insurer**, and do not give a recorded statement before speaking to a lawyer.
6. **Do not withhold rent as a remedy for the injury**; it invites an eviction and complicates the case. Rent-escrow remedies for repairs are a separate matter we can advise on.
7. **Call a tenant injury lawyer.** Preservation letters, the FOIA request, and identification of the management company, the owner, and any contractor come first.

## What a Michigan tenant injury case is worth

Medical expenses, lost wages and earning capacity, pain and suffering, loss of enjoyment of life, scarring, and in a death the damages under the wrongful death act (MCL 600.2922), reduced by any fault assigned to you. There is no serious impairment threshold; that rule belongs to motor vehicle cases. The defendants are the owner, the management company, and often a maintenance, snow-removal, or security contractor, each with its own liability policy, which is why tenant cases are more fully collectible than most injury claims.

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan tenant injuries

**Can I sue my landlord for an injury in Michigan?** Yes. A landlord that failed to keep the premises and common areas fit for their intended use or in reasonable repair (MCL 554.139), or that was otherwise negligent, is liable for the injuries that result. The claim is against the landlord’s liability insurance.

**What is MCL 554.139?** The statute that writes two covenants into every Michigan residential lease: the premises and common areas are fit for the use intended by the parties, and the premises will be kept in reasonable repair and in compliance with health and safety laws. It is liberally construed in favor of tenants, and its protections cannot be waived in a lease shorter than one year.

**My lease says the landlord isn’t responsible for injuries. Is that enforceable?** No. The Truth in Renting Act prohibits lease clauses that excuse a landlord from liability for its own negligence or for failing to perform a legal duty (MCL 554.633(1)(e)).

**I fell in the parking lot on ice. The landlord says it was “just an inconvenience.”** That argument comes from Allison v AEW (2008). In Bowerman v Red Oak Management (July 2026), the Michigan Supreme Court held that “mere inconvenience” is not the test and that fitness is measured by the use the parties intended, including who the tenants are. The question is for a jury.

**I knew about the hazard. Does that end my case?** No. Since Kandil-Elsayed (2023) and Bowerman (2026), your knowledge goes to your percentage of fault, which reduces the recovery and bars it only above 50% (MCL 600.2959).

**I was hurt inside my apartment. Is the landlord liable?** If the landlord knew or should have known of the defect and failed to repair it in a reasonable time, or repaired it negligently, yes (MCL 554.139(1)(b); MCL 125.471). Written repair requests are the key evidence.

**I was assaulted in my building. Can I sue the landlord?** Possibly, if the landlord failed to respond to an ongoing crime it knew about (Bailey v Schaaf, 2013) or left a specific security failure, such as a broken entry lock it had been told about, unrepaired. Police records and your complaints are the evidence.

**My child has elevated lead levels. Is the landlord responsible?** If the source is deteriorated paint, dust, or soil at the rental, the landlord can be liable under negligence, MCL 554.139, and lead-safe housing laws. Federal law also required lead disclosure before your lease. These cases require prompt testing of the unit.

**Will my landlord evict me if I make a claim?** Michigan law prohibits retaliatory eviction for complaining about conditions or exercising your legal rights, and presumes retaliation within 90 days of a protected act (MCL 600.5720).

**How long do I have?** Three years from the injury for a lawsuit (MCL 600.5805(2)); 120 days to give written notice if the landlord is a housing commission; and evidence of the condition disappears with the first repair, so documentation is immediate.
