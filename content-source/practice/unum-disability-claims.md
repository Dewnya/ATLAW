## Unum Disability Denial Lawyer

Unum Group, based in Chattanooga, Tennessee, is one of the largest disability insurers in the United States. Its subsidiaries, principally **Unum Life Insurance Company of America** for group policies, with **Provident Life and Accident Insurance Company**, **The Paul Revere Life Insurance Company**, and **First Unum Life Insurance Company** for individual and New York business, provide disability coverage to millions of American workers. If your employer offers long-term disability coverage, there is a fair chance the policy says Unum.

Unum is also the company whose claims handling produced one of the most significant regulatory actions in disability insurance history. In **November 2004**, after a multistate market conduct examination led by the insurance regulators of Maine, Massachusetts, and Tennessee, Unum’s subsidiaries entered into Regulatory Settlement Agreements that **48 states and the District of Columbia** adopted, with the **U.S. Department of Labor** as a party. Unum paid a **$15 million** fine, agreed to offer reassessment of qualifying claims denied or terminated going back to January 1, 1997, through a process that identified more than 200,000 potentially eligible claims, and agreed to specific changes in how it evaluated claims, including how it treated the opinions of treating physicians and the significance of Social Security disability awards, under a monitoring regime that carried a contingent fine of **$145 million** for non-compliance. In **October 2005** the California Department of Insurance, which had declined to join the multistate agreement, imposed its own **$8 million** penalty and a separate reassessment for about 26,000 California claimants. In the reassessment that followed, Unum reported an average overturn rate of approximately 40 percent among claims reassessed through June 2007. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: verify every figure against the 2004 RSA, UnumProvident’s 2004–2008 Forms 10-K, and the regulators’ reassessment reports; the commonly reported reversal rate is roughly 40 percent]**

That was twenty years ago, and the company has changed. The reason the history still belongs on this page is that the practices the regulators identified in 2004, denials resting on in-house physicians’ file reviews, treating physicians’ opinions discounted without explanation, Social Security awards disregarded, claims closed at the own-occupation transition, are the same categories of conduct that an ERISA court weighs today in deciding whether a denial was arbitrary and capricious. A Michigan claimant whose denial letter from Unum fits one of those patterns is not inventing a grievance; she is describing something a state insurance commissioner once documented.

In Evans v. UnumProvident Corp., 434 F.3d 866 (6th Cir. 2006), the Sixth Circuit affirmed a finding that UnumProvident acted arbitrarily and capriciously in terminating disability benefits. The court considered the insurer’s reliance on internal physician file reviews, factual inaccuracies, failure to adequately address reliable medical evidence, and conflict of interest.

## What the 2004 agreements required, and why it still matters

The Regulatory Settlement Agreements did not simply fine Unum. They required specific changes in how claims were evaluated, and those changes are a useful checklist for reading a Unum denial letter today:

- **Treating physicians.** Under the October 2005 amendment, Unum agreed to give significant weight to a properly qualified attending physician’s opinion unless it was not adequately supported by medically acceptable clinical or diagnostic standards and was inconsistent with other substantial evidence in the record. The claim file had to document specific reasons for rejecting the opinion.
- **Social Security awards.** Unum agreed to give significant weight to Social Security disability awards, subject to specified exceptions. The 2018 ERISA disability-claims amendments separately required insurers to explain their basis for disagreeing with a Social Security disability determination presented to the plan.
- **In-house medical reviews.** Unum agreed to changes in how its physicians reviewed files, including requiring reviewers to consider all of the claimant’s conditions together rather than one at a time.
- **Independent examinations.** Unum agreed to obtain an independent medical examination in specified circumstances rather than deciding on a file review.
- **Governance.** Unum created oversight of its claims operation with regulatory monitoring and reporting.

A denial that discounts the treating physician without a stated reason, disregards a Social Security award, rests on a single-condition file review, or declines an examination the plan permitted is a denial that departs from what Unum agreed to do in 2004. In an ERISA appeal, that departure is documented, not argued; the appeal states what the file shows and lets the court compare it to the standard Unum itself accepted.

## The ERISA rules Unum must follow

Most Unum group policies are employer-sponsored and governed by ERISA and the Department of Labor’s claims regulation, 29 CFR 2560.503-1:

- The **denial letter** must state the specific reasons and plan provisions, describe what would perfect the claim, explain the appeal and the right to sue, and, under the 2018 amendments, explain why Unum disagrees with the treating physicians and with any Social Security award.
- The claimant has **at least 180 days** to appeal; the appeal is mandatory before suit.
- The claimant is entitled to the **complete claim file** free of charge, including the physician reviews, the vocational analysis, the surveillance, and the internal guidelines relied on.
- **New evidence** developed during the appeal must be disclosed with time to respond before the appeal is decided.
- The appeal must be decided by **someone new**, consulting a health professional not involved in the initial denial, within **45 days** plus one 45-day extension.
- Violations that are more than minor cost Unum its deference: deemed exhaustion and de novo review under 29 CFR 2560.503-1(l)(2).

## Building the appeal against Unum

The appeal is the trial. The federal judge reads the file as Unum closed it and, in most cases, asks only whether Unum’s decision was arbitrary and capricious.

**Proving the disability**

1. Obtain and read the complete claim file. Identify each physician reviewer and specialty, the occupational classification Unum applied, the transferable skills analysis and its assumed capacities, and every record Unum did not have.
2. Update the medical record through the appeal date.
3. Obtain treating-specialist opinions that answer the reviewers point by point and state function-by-function restrictions against the policy’s definition of disability, regular occupation as normally performed or any gainful occupation at the earnings threshold.
4. Add objective testing where the condition allows; for a condition Unum may call “self-reported,” document every objective finding that exists and cite the medical authority on the condition’s objective basis.
5. Obtain a vocational assessment addressing the occupation as the policy defines it and each occupation in the transferable skills analysis.
6. Include the Social Security decision and its evidence.
7. Add statements from family, former supervisors, and coworkers.

**Proving the process was unreasonable**

1. Document the reviewer’s specialty mismatch, the treating opinions discounted without a stated reason, the conditions reviewed one at a time, and Unum’s unexercised right to examine.
2. Compare the denial letter to the record and to the 2004 checklist.
3. Document every procedural failure.
4. Set out the structural conflict of interest under *Glenn*.
5. Write the argument tying the evidence to the policy language and the law.

**Preserve the litigation deadline.** Some Unum policies contain a three-year contractual limitation period for filing suit, calculated from dates specified in the policy. ## If the appeal is denied

Suit is filed under 29 USC 1132(a)(1)(B) in federal court, in Michigan the Eastern District (Detroit, Ann Arbor, Flint, Bay City) or Western District (Grand Rapids, Kalamazoo, Lansing, Marquette), on the administrative record. If the plan grants Unum discretion, the standard is arbitrary and capricious with the conflict of interest weighed under *Glenn*; if Unum forfeited deference through procedural violations, de novo. Remedies are the benefits owed with interest and, in the court’s discretion, attorney fees to a claimant who achieves some success on the merits. Remand to Unum for a new decision is a common outcome.

For Unum policies **not** governed by ERISA, the individual policies Provident and Paul Revere sold to physicians, dentists, and other professionals in large numbers through the 1980s and 1990s, and policies issued to governmental and church employers, Michigan law applies: a jury, discovery, contract damages, and 12 percent penalty interest under MCL 500.2006 on benefits not timely paid. The individual professional policies, many of them “own occupation” to age 65 with no any-occupation transition, were a central subject of the 2004 examination and remain in force for thousands of insureds.

## The Social Security connection

Unum requires claimants to apply for Social Security disability, offsets the LTD benefit by the award (and by an estimate before the award, absent a reimbursement agreement), and demands repayment of the overlap when back pay arrives. The SSDI award is also evidence that Unum agreed in 2004 to treat as significant and that the 2018 ERISA rules require it to address in the denial letter. ## Frequently asked questions about Unum disability denials

**How long do I have to appeal a Unum denial?** At least 180 days from receipt of the denial under ERISA; the letter must state the deadline. Separately, the policy sets a deadline for filing suit, often three years from when proof of claim was due, which is enforceable. Calculate both when the denial arrives.

**What was the 2004 Unum settlement?** After a multistate market conduct examination, Unum’s subsidiaries agreed with 48 states, the District of Columbia, and the U.S. Department of Labor to pay a $15 million fine, reopen and reassess claims denied or closed since 1997 (more than 200,000 claimants were eventually eligible), and change how they evaluated claims, particularly the weight given to treating physicians and Social Security awards, under monitoring with a $145 million contingent fine. California imposed a separate $8 million penalty in 2005.

**Does the settlement help my claim?** Not directly; the reassessment closed years ago and the agreements are not binding in an individual case. Indirectly, yes: they document the practices to look for in your claim file, and a denial that departs from what Unum agreed to do is a denial a court can see as arbitrary.

**The denial was based on a doctor who never saw me.** Unum relies heavily on physician file reviews, which was a central finding of the 2004 examination. Courts in Michigan’s federal circuit have treated reliance on a file review to reject treating physicians, when the insurer had the right to examine the claimant and did not, as evidence of an arbitrary decision.

**My benefits were cut off at 24 months.** Either the own-occupation period ended and Unum found you able to perform any gainful occupation, or the mental illness or self-reported symptoms limitation was applied. The letter says which; the appeal answers the specific finding.

**Unum says my fibromyalgia is “self-reported.”** Many Unum policies limit disabilities based on self-reported symptoms to 24 months. The appeal documents every objective finding that exists, including tender-point examinations, specialist diagnoses, and co-existing conditions, and cites the medical authority on the condition. The policy’s exact definition controls.

**Unum reduced my benefit for Social Security I have not received.** Unum policies allow an estimated offset unless you sign a reimbursement agreement. Ask which alternative applies, and coordinate the SSDI application so the award, the offset, and the repayment demand are handled together.

**I have an old Provident or Paul Revere individual policy. Does ERISA apply?** Usually not. Individual policies you bought yourself are governed by Michigan law: a jury, discovery, contract damages, and 12 percent penalty interest. Many of these policies are own-occupation to age 65, and they were a central subject of the 2004 examination.

**Can I get damages for what the denial did to my family?** Not under ERISA, which allows recovery of the benefits, interest, and in the court’s discretion attorney fees. Under a non-ERISA Unum policy governed by Michigan law, contract damages and penalty interest are available.
