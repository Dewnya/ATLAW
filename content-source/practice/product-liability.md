## Michigan Product Liability Lawyer

In 2024, federal regulators recalled 950 vehicle models covering 29.3 million vehicles, plus 3.7 million pieces of automotive equipment, 1.4 million tires, and 610,000 child seats. Consumer product recalls ran alongside them: appliances, power tools, e-bike batteries, furniture, children’s products, and medications. Behind every recall are people who were hurt before the defect was acknowledged, and in Michigan their claims are governed by a product liability statute, enacted in 1995, that is more demanding of injured people than the law of most states.

That statute decides what you must prove for a design defect, when a seller rather than a manufacturer can be sued, how misuse and alteration are treated, what a product more than ten years old does to your case, and how much you can recover for pain and suffering. It also contained, until February 2024, a provision that barred nearly every lawsuit against a drug manufacturer in Michigan; the repeal reopened cases that had been impossible for 28 years. This page explains each rule, the three kinds of defect, the deadlines, and what to do with the product that hurt you.

## Michigan’s product liability statute

Michigan’s rules are in the Revised Judicature Act, MCL 600.2945 through 600.2949a, enacted as part of the 1995 tort reform. A “product liability action” is any action for death, injury, or property damage caused by the production of a product, whatever legal theory is pleaded (MCL 600.2945(h)), so the statute governs negligence, implied warranty, and strict liability claims alike. The provisions that decide cases:

| Provision | What it says | Statute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Design defect standard** | A manufacturer or seller is not liable for a design defect unless the product was not reasonably safe when it left the defendant’s control and a practical and technically feasible alternative design was available that would have prevented the harm without significantly impairing the product’s usefulness or desirability and without creating equal or greater risk | MCL 600.2946(2) |
| **Compliance with government standards** | Compliance with federal or state safety standards, or agency approval, relevant to the harm is a defense in production-defect claims that the plaintiff must overcome | MCL 600.2946(4) **[ATTORNEY CHECK: current text and whether the presumption is rebuttable]** |
| **Warnings** | No duty to warn of a risk that is or should be obvious to a reasonably prudent user or is a matter of common knowledge; a manufacturer’s duty to warn runs to foreseeable users; a sophisticated user may be presumed to know the risk | MCL 600.2947(4), 600.2948(2) |
| **Misuse** | No liability for harm caused by misuse that was not reasonably foreseeable; whether misuse was foreseeable is a question of law for the judge | MCL 600.2947(2) |
| **Alteration** | No liability for harm caused by an alteration or modification that was not reasonably foreseeable | MCL 600.2947(1) |
| **Non-manufacturing sellers** | A seller other than the manufacturer is liable only if it failed to exercise reasonable care (including a breach of implied warranty) or breached an express warranty | MCL 600.2947(6) |
| **Non-economic damages cap** | Pain and suffering and other non-economic damages are capped at $280,000 adjusted for inflation, or $500,000 adjusted where the defect caused death or permanent loss of a vital bodily function; the 2026 figures set by the State Treasurer are $596,400 and $1,065,000 | MCL 600.2946a **[ATTORNEY CHECK: 2026 Treasury notice]** |
| **Gross misconduct exception** | If the court finds the defendant had actual knowledge the product was defective and that there was a substantial likelihood it would cause the injury, and willfully disregarded that knowledge, the design-defect standard, the cap, and the misuse and alteration defenses do not apply | MCL 600.2949a |
| **Products more than ten years old** | For a product in use ten years or more, the plaintiff must prove the case without the benefit of any presumption | MCL 600.5805(12) **[ATTORNEY CHECK: subsection number]** |
| **Intoxication** | If the injured person was impaired by alcohol or drugs and, as a result, was 50% or more the cause of the injury, the defendant is not liable | MCL 600.2955a |
| **Comparative fault** | Damages are reduced by the plaintiff’s percentage of fault; non-economic damages are barred above 50% | MCL 600.2959 |

Michigan’s design-defect rule, which requires proof of a feasible alternative design, is the provision that distinguishes Michigan from strict-liability states. A product can be dangerous and still not “defective” under Michigan law unless an engineer can show how it should have been built. That proof is the center of every Michigan design case, and it is why the product itself must be preserved.

## The 2024 repeal: drug cases are back in Michigan

From 1996 to February 2024, MCL 600.2946(5) provided that a drug approved by the FDA and labeled in compliance with FDA requirements was not defective or unreasonably dangerous, with narrow exceptions for fraud on the FDA and bribery. Michigan was the only state with such a rule, and it barred Michigan residents from claims other Americans brought over opioids, diabetes drugs, antipsychotics, and contraceptives. The legislature repealed the provision in 2023 Public Act 309, effective February 13, 2024.

Michigan residents injured by a prescription or over-the-counter drug can now bring product liability claims under the same statute that governs other products, subject to its design-defect standard, its cap, and federal preemption rules that apply to drug labeling. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: application of the repeal to injuries before February 13, 2024]** Medical devices were never covered by the drug immunity; device claims are governed by the Michigan statute and by federal preemption for devices approved through the FDA’s premarket approval process.

## The three kinds of product defect

1. **Manufacturing defect.** The product left the factory different from its design and from the other units on the line: a cracked weld, a contaminated batch, a missing component. Proof compares the unit that hurt you to the specification, which is why the unit must be kept.
2. **Design defect.** Every unit is built as designed, and the design is the danger: a mower without a blade brake, a vehicle with a fuel tank that ruptures in a rear impact, a space heater without a tip-over switch, a lithium battery pack without thermal protection. Under MCL 600.2946(2), the case is proved by an engineer who shows a feasible, safer alternative design that existed when the product was made.
3. **Warning defect.** The product is dangerous in a way that is not obvious and the manufacturer failed to warn or instruct adequately: a drug interaction, a chemical’s inhalation hazard, a tool’s kickback risk. Michigan excuses warnings for obvious risks and for sophisticated users, so the case turns on whether a reasonably prudent user would have known.

A single case often pleads two or three of these, plus breach of implied warranty under the Uniform Commercial Code (MCL 440.2314), which Michigan courts treat as a parallel theory with the same defect requirement.

## Who can be sued

- **The manufacturer** of the product and of the component that failed. In vehicle cases this often means both the automaker and a tier-one supplier, many of them headquartered in Oakland and Macomb counties.
- **The seller**, including a dealer or retailer, but in Michigan only for its own negligence or an express warranty (MCL 600.2947(6)), not simply for having sold the product. This matters because it limits the Michigan defendants in a case against an out-of-state manufacturer.
- **The installer, repairer, or modifier**, in ordinary negligence.
- **An employer** is generally immune under workers’ compensation for a worker injured by a machine on the job, but the machine’s manufacturer is not; workplace product cases are a major category. See our [workers’ compensation](/personal-injury/workers-compensation) page.
- **A rental company or lessor** that placed the product in the stream of commerce.

Where a product defect caused or worsened a car crash, such as an airbag non-deployment, a seat-back collapse, a roof crush, or a tire tread separation, the product claim runs alongside the no-fault and third-party claims described on our [car accident](/personal-injury/auto-accidents) page, and the vehicle must be preserved before the insurer totals and salvages it.

## Evidence: the product is the case

Product liability cases are won and lost on physical evidence, and the defendants know it.

| Step | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **Keep the product**, every piece, unrepaired and unaltered | The defect is proved by inspecting it; Michigan courts can dismiss a case or instruct a jury against a party who lost or destroyed the product (spoliation) |
| **Keep the packaging, manual, warnings, and receipt** | Warning claims and the date and place of purchase |
| **Photograph the scene and the product as found** | Position, settings, and conditions at the time of injury |
| **Do not return the product to the store or the manufacturer**, even for a refund or “investigation” | The manufacturer’s engineers will examine it without you; a joint inspection protocol is the alternative |
| **Preserve the vehicle** in a crash case before the insurer salvages it | Event data recorder, airbag module, seat, restraint, and tire evidence |
| **Identify the model, serial number, lot number, and date of manufacture** | Recall status, the ten-year rule, and the applicable design |
| **Search recalls**: NHTSA for vehicles, CPSC for consumer products, FDA for drugs and devices | A recall is evidence the manufacturer knew; it is not required for a claim |

## Children’s products, e-bikes, and household hazards

Michigan families see the same product injuries the national recall data shows: furniture and television tip-overs, button batteries and magnets swallowed by children, crib and sleeper defects, lithium-ion battery fires in e-bikes and scooters, space heaters, pressure cookers, and power tools. Children’s cases carry two Michigan rules worth knowing: a child’s lawsuit deadline is extended to age 19 (MCL 600.5851), and the higher damages tier applies where the defect caused death or permanent loss of a vital bodily function (MCL 600.2946a).

## Frequently asked questions about Michigan product liability

**Is Michigan a strict liability state for defective products?** Not in the way most people mean. Michigan’s statute requires proof that the product was not reasonably safe and, for a design claim, that a feasible, safer alternative design existed (MCL 600.2946(2)). Negligence is not required for a manufacturing defect, but the plaintiff must prove the defect.

**How long do I have to sue?** Three years from the injury (MCL 600.5805). Michigan does not generally extend that period for late discovery of the cause, so call promptly. A child’s claim is extended to age 19.

**Is there a cap on damages?** Non-economic damages (pain and suffering) are capped at an inflation-adjusted amount, $596,400 in 2026, or $1,065,000 where the defect caused death or permanent loss of a vital bodily function (MCL 600.2946a). Economic damages, such as medical bills and lost wages, are not capped. The cap does not apply if the manufacturer knew of the defect and willfully disregarded it (MCL 600.2949a).

**Can I sue the store that sold it?** Only if the seller was itself negligent or made an express warranty (MCL 600.2947(6)). The manufacturer and the component maker are the primary defendants.

**I threw the product away. Do I still have a case?** It is much harder. Michigan courts can penalize a party who failed to preserve the product. If any part, the packaging, photographs, or an identical unit from the same lot exists, call anyway.

**The product was recalled. Does that prove my case?** No, but it is strong evidence that the manufacturer knew of the defect, and the recall notice identifies the defect and the affected units. A claim does not require a recall.

**I was hurt by a prescription drug. Can I sue in Michigan?** Since February 13, 2024, yes. The immunity for FDA-approved drugs in MCL 600.2946(5) was repealed by 2023 PA 309. Drug claims are now governed by the general product liability statute and federal preemption rules.

**I was using the product in a way the manual didn’t describe. Is that misuse?** Only if the use was not reasonably foreseeable, which is decided by the judge (MCL 600.2947(2)). Manufacturers must anticipate foreseeable uses, including some the manual does not mention.

**The product was fifteen years old. Can I still sue?** Yes, within three years of the injury, but without any presumption in your favor (MCL 600.5805(12)). The case is proved on the engineering evidence alone.

**I was hurt by a machine at work. Can I sue the manufacturer?** Yes. Workers’ compensation bars a lawsuit against your employer, not against the machine’s manufacturer, and the two claims proceed together, with the compensation carrier holding a lien on the product recovery.
