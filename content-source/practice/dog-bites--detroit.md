## Detroit Dog Bite Lawyer

Michigan’s dog bite law is short and strict. If a dog bites you without provocation while you are on public property or lawfully on private property, the owner is liable for the full amount of your damages, regardless of whether the dog had ever bitten before or whether the owner knew it was dangerous (MCL 287.351). You do not have to prove negligence. In most of Michigan, that makes the case about the injury and the insurance. In Detroit, three more questions come first: who owns the dog, whether that owner has a homeowner’s or renter’s policy, and what happens when the dog was loose and no owner comes forward. ## The statute, and the two defenses

**Strict liability.** Under MCL 287.351 you prove three things: a dog bit you; you were on public property or lawfully on private property, including the owner’s own property, as a guest, customer, tenant, delivery driver, utility worker, mail carrier, or a child playing with permission (the statute specifically covers people performing a duty imposed by law or by the postal service); and you did not provoke the dog. The dog’s history and the owner’s knowledge are irrelevant. There is no “one free bite” in Michigan.

**Provocation.** The owner’s main defense and the one insurers reach for first. Michigan courts judge provocation by whether the person’s conduct would provoke a dog, not by what the person intended, so it can be unintentional (stepping on a sleeping dog). Petting a dog, walking past a fence, entering a yard with permission, or a child reaching toward a dog is not provocation; hitting, teasing, or cornering a dog may be. The witnesses to the seconds before the bite decide it.

**Trespass.** A person unlawfully on private property is not covered by the statute. A child who wanders into a yard is the contested case; a meter reader or a delivery driver is lawfully present.

**Comparative fault does not reduce a statutory claim.** Michigan courts have held that the ordinary comparative-fault rule does not apply to the dog bite statute; the owner’s defenses are limited to trespass and provocation (*Hill v Sacka*, Mich App 2003). **[ATTORNEY CHECK: the firm’s reading of Hill v Sacka]** Injuries from a dog that did not bite, a knockdown, a [fall](/personal-injury/slip-and-fall) while fleeing, a [bicycle crash](/personal-injury/bicycle-accidents) caused by a chasing dog, are negligence claims, where comparative fault does apply and the owner’s knowledge of the dog’s behavior matters.

## Who pays in Detroit

**Homeowner’s and renter’s insurance.** Dog bite liability is covered by the standard homeowner’s policy and most renter’s policies, typically with $100,000 to $500,000 in liability coverage. The owner reports the bite, the insurer pays the claim, and the owner is not out of pocket; the insurer cannot require the owner to give up the dog. This is why suing a neighbor or a relative is less of a barrier than it sounds. Some policies exclude certain breeds or dogs with a bite history; the policy must be read.

**Uninsured owners.** In a city where half of households rent and many renters carry no insurance, the owner often has no policy. The claim is then against the owner personally, which is worth what the owner can pay, and the search for another responsible party begins: the landlord, a co-owner or “keeper” of the dog, the owner’s employer if the dog was on a job site, or the owner of a business or property that harbored the dog (see our [premises liability page](/personal-injury/premises-liability)).

**Landlords.** A Detroit landlord is generally not liable for a tenant’s dog. The exception that matters in the city’s rental market: a landlord who knew the dog was dangerous and had the ability to remove it, through the lease or otherwise, and did not, may be liable (*Szkodzinski v Griffin*, Mich App 1988). Prior complaints to the landlord or to the Detroit Housing Commission are the evidence; the Housing Commission’s status as a public body brings governmental immunity into the analysis, and a tenant’s dog is not a building defect the public building exception covers. **[ATTORNEY CHECK: landlord liability standard; DHC status]** See our [tenant injury page](/personal-injury/tenant-injuries).

**Stray and loose dogs.** A dog with no identifiable owner has no insurer, and the statute requires an owner. Detroit Animal Care and Control, a City agency, is immune from most suits over how it handles strays under the Governmental Tort Liability Act. **[ATTORNEY CHECK]** The practical path is identification: animal control’s pickup records, neighbors, and the dog’s microchip often establish an owner who let the dog roam, and an owner whose dog was loose in violation of Detroit’s leash and licensing ordinance is liable under the statute like any other owner (Detroit City Code, Chapter 6). If no owner is ever found, the victim’s own health insurance pays the medical bills and there is usually no civil claim.

**Delivery and utility workers.** Postal carriers, Amazon, UPS, FedEx, and food-delivery drivers, meter readers, and home health aides bitten on a Detroit route have the statutory claim against the owner and a workers’ compensation claim against the employer at the same time; the comp insurer has a lien on the recovery (MCL 418.827(5)). See our [Detroit workers’ compensation page](/personal-injury/workers-compensation/detroit).

## The Dangerous Animals Act

Separate from the civil claim, Michigan’s Dangerous Animals Act (MCL 287.321 to 287.323) lets a prosecutor or animal control petition the court to have a dog that has bitten a person declared dangerous, with destruction or strict confinement as the remedies, and makes the owner of a dog previously known to be dangerous subject to felony charges if it causes serious injury or death. A dangerous-animal finding is powerful evidence in the civil case, and the civil case does not depend on it. Detroit Animal Care and Control’s bite report and any prior complaints about the dog, obtained by Freedom of Information Act request, are where the dog’s history is found.

## Injuries, children, and scarring

Dog bites cause puncture and crush wounds, torn tendons and nerves, hand and forearm fractures, and facial injuries. Bite wounds are often left open and treated with antibiotics rather than closed, and infection is a routine complication. Michigan requires rabies risk to be assessed; the biting dog is observed for ten days, and if it cannot be found, the victim receives post-exposure treatment. Children’s Hospital of Michigan, Detroit Receiving, and Henry Ford are where many of the city’s serious bite injuries are treated. **[CONFIRM: hospital list]**

**Children** are bitten more often than adults and on the face and head far more often, because of their height. A facial bite on a Detroit child means scar revision surgeries over years as the child grows, and psychological injury, fear of dogs, nightmares, anxiety, that Michigan law compensates and that should be documented by a counselor. Two rules specific to children: a minor’s settlement must be approved by the Wayne County Probate Court or the trial court (MCR 2.420), with the funds protected until adulthood; and the limitations period is tolled until the child’s 19th birthday (MCL 600.5851(1)). The evidence does not keep that long. Settle or sue when the medical picture, including the plastic surgeon’s projection of future revisions, is clear.

**Damages** include medical treatment and future scar revision, lost wages (including a parent’s time off work), pain and suffering, permanent disfigurement, and psychological injury. Michigan does not cap these damages in a dog bite case. A fatal attack is a wrongful death claim brought by the estate; see our [Detroit wrongful death page](/personal-injury/wrongful-death/detroit).

## What to do after a dog bite in Detroit

1. **Get medical care the same day**, even for a bite that looks minor. The record dated that day is the case, and infection and rabies are the medical reasons.
2. **Identify the dog and the owner**: name, address, and vaccination status. If the owner refuses, note the address and the dog’s description; animal control will identify them.
3. **Report the bite to Detroit Animal Care and Control** (or to the police if animal control cannot be reached). The report triggers the rabies observation, creates the official record, and surfaces prior bites. Treating physicians must also report a bite where rabies is suspected (Mich Admin Code R 325.180(6)).
4. **Photograph the wounds** that day and as they heal, and the location of the bite.
5. **Get witness names.** Provocation is decided by what witnesses say happened in the seconds before the bite.
6. **Do not give a recorded statement** to the owner’s insurer or accept an early offer; facial scarring is not valued until it has matured.
7. **Ask about the owner’s insurance and the landlord.** If the owner has none, the search for another responsible party begins immediately.

## Frequently asked questions

**The dog had never bitten anyone before. Does the owner still have to pay?** Yes. Michigan’s statute makes the owner liable regardless of the dog’s history or the owner’s knowledge, as long as you were lawfully present and did not provoke the dog.

**I was bitten by a stray in Detroit. Who pays?** If no owner can be identified, usually no one but your health insurer; the statute requires an owner, and the City’s animal control agency is generally immune. Many “strays” turn out to have owners, through microchips, neighbors, or animal control’s records, and an owner whose dog was loose is liable under the statute. Report the bite the same day so the search can start.

**The owner rents and has no insurance. Is there any point?** Possibly. The claim against the owner personally is worth what the owner can pay. If the landlord knew the dog was dangerous and could have required its removal, the landlord may be liable, and the landlord usually is insured.

**The owner is my neighbor and I do not want to sue them.** In nearly every insured case, the claim is paid by the owner’s homeowner’s or renter’s insurance, not by the neighbor, and the insurer cannot make them give up the dog. Many cases settle without a lawsuit ever being filed.

**What counts as provocation?** Conduct that would provoke a dog, judged objectively: hitting, teasing, cornering, stepping on it. Petting a dog, walking past it, or entering a yard with permission is not provocation.

**My child was bitten on the face. How long do we have?** Until the child’s 19th birthday to sue, but the case should be brought when the medical picture is clear. A child’s settlement is approved by the court and the funds are protected until the child is an adult.

**I was bitten delivering packages in Detroit. Is that a comp case or a dog bite case?** Both. Workers’ compensation pays your medical bills and wage loss; the owner is liable under the statute for your pain, suffering, and scarring; and the comp insurer is reimbursed from the recovery.

**Do I have to report the bite?** Yes, to Detroit Animal Care and Control. The report starts the rabies observation and creates the record of the dog’s history. Your doctor is also required to report it.
